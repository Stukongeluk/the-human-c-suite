# Good leadership: reasons over rank, and no surprises

Read this when a decision arrives without its reasons, a change lands as a surprise, people are told to "be flexible" instead of being given a plan, challenge is treated as disloyalty, leaders go quiet after being questioned, or a leader asks how to handle a team that pushes back. Also read it when the user faces leadership like that and asks how to respond.

Good leaders don't need rank to win an argument. They want the best outcome for the people doing the work and for the company, so they share their reasoning, ask to be challenged and change course when someone finds a better route. Rank decides who is accountable for a decision, not who is right about it.

## 1. Six principles

1. **Reasons, not rank.** A decision is only as strong as its reasons. Leaders explain theirs, and anyone may challenge them, up or down the organisation. A title, seniority or "the board wants it" is not a reason.
2. **No surprises for the people affected.** Changes to people's work are shared while those people can still shape them: the problem first, then the options, then the decision, with dates. Kim and Mauborgne's "fair process" names three elements: engage people in decisions that affect them, explain the final thinking, and make expectations clear. People accept decisions that go against them far better when the process was fair.
3. **Challenge is free risk analysis.** The people closest to the work see problems leaders can't. Treat pushback as information: thank, check it against the facts, and update visibly or explain why not.
4. **Silence is a warning sign, in both directions.** Teams that stop speaking up, and leaders who stop explaining after being questioned, both starve decisions of information. Research on organisational silence finds that the most common reason people stay quiet is fear of being seen negatively; another is the belief that speaking up won't change anything. DORA's research on software teams finds that high-trust cultures where information flows freely predict better delivery and organisational performance.
5. **Leaders serve.** The job is to make other people's work possible: clear goals, resources, obstacles removed, and protection from chaos. Leaders who admit what they don't know, credit others' strengths and stay open to feedback see better team learning, engagement and retention. Nobody has to prove their worth by sounding certain.
6. **"Be flexible" is not a plan.** Flexibility is what people give when leaders do their part: early information, reasons, a realistic timeline and time to adapt. Asking people to absorb surprises moves the cost of poor planning onto the people with the least say.

## 2. In practice, when you lead

- **Write decisions down.** Use a one-page decision record: the problem, the options considered, the decision, the reasons, who was consulted, what would change your mind and a review date. Share it with everyone affected.
- **Open a window before big changes.** Share the problem and the draft options, give a fixed period for input (one to two weeks is often enough), then decide and say what you did with the input. "We heard X and changed Y; we heard Z and didn't, because..." builds more trust than agreement does.
- **Say what is decided and what is open.** "We've decided" and "we're leaning towards" are different messages. Mixing them up is how rumours start.
- **Give notice in proportion to the impact.** Team or role changes are announced well before they take effect, by people's own manager first, never on the day in an all-hands.
- **Make challenge expected.** Ask "what are we missing?" and let the most junior or the most affected people speak first. Answer challenge with "thanks, let's check", never with "be more positive". Check whether anything has changed because of input; if nothing ever does, people will stop giving it.
- **Disagree and commit, in both directions.** Once people have really been heard, they commit to the decision. The same holds for leaders who are overruled by better evidence.
- **Ask how you're doing.** Use skip-level conversations and an anonymous pulse with statements like "I can raise concerns without fear" and "decisions that affect me are explained". Act on the results.
- **Involve representatives on time.** In the Netherlands, the works council must be asked for advice on major decisions at a moment when its advice can still substantially influence the decision (article 25(2) WOR). A reorganisation announced first and "consulted" afterwards can be challenged at the Enterprise Chamber. Until the works council has advised, call it a proposed or intended decision, in the announcement too, and keep the parts it could change open. Plan the dates around its advice: if the final decision departs from the advice, implementation waits a month (article 25(6) WOR). Germany, the Nordics and the EU rules: [european-and-nordic-practice.md](european-and-nordic-practice.md).

## 3. Warning signs: decision by ambush

| Sign | What it costs | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Big changes announced in an all-hands without warning | Trust, rumours, regretted leavers, and sometimes a legal challenge | Consult first, tell people through their own manager, then announce |
| Leaders go quiet after being questioned, then announce finished decisions | Decisions made with less information; teams learn that input is pointless | Publish decision records and answer challenge with evidence |
| "Just adapt" or "be flexible" as the answer to questions | The cost of poor planning shifts to the teams; burnout | Give dates, reasons and what is still open |
| People who challenge are called negative or disloyal | The best people go quiet or leave. Managers tend to rate employees who challenge lower than those who support, even when the challenge is useful | Judge people on outcomes; thank useful challenge in public |
| Decisions justified by title, seniority or "the board wants it" | Weak decisions survive because nobody can examine them | State the actual reasons and evidence |
| Information kept within an inner circle | Teams plan on guesses, and trust between groups erodes | Share by default; keep confidential only what must be, and say that it is |

## 4. When you face leadership like this

Most people who notice this pattern are not in charge. When the user is an engineer, manager or adviser:

- **Assume pressure before malice.** Leaders who go quiet are often under pressure, afraid of losing face, or used to a different way of working. Make it easy for them to explain.
- **Ask for the reasons, not a reversal.** "What problem does this solve? Which options did you consider? What would change your mind? What is still open?" Ask in private first, then put the questions in writing.
- **Offer a process, not a complaint.** Propose a one-page decision record, a short input window or a no-surprises agreement for team changes, and pilot it on one upcoming decision.
- **Challenge in a way that can be heard.** Bring evidence and an alternative, go private before public, let leaders save face, and never label people. A precise question often lands better than a confident objection.
- **Find allies with a stake:** other managers, HR, finance, the works council and the teams affected.
- **Use the formal channels when needed, in proportion.** In the Netherlands, the works council's advice right covers major decisions such as reorganisations (article 25 WOR); if there is no works council, a union or HR can help. The confidential adviser (vertrouwenspersoon) is for unwanted behaviour such as intimidation or bullying, and for integrity concerns, not for disagreements about a business decision. More: [hard-situations.md](hard-situations.md).
- **Protect your team meanwhile.** Turn surprises into clear priorities, keep the promises you can keep, and don't feed cynicism.
- **Know when it's enough.** If real attempts change nothing, it is legitimate to escalate or to leave. Keep a factual record of what was asked and what happened.

## 5. Culture without stereotypes

People bring different expectations about hierarchy from their countries, sectors and past employers. Someone used to formal hierarchy may hear public challenge as an attack. Someone used to Dutch directness may read silence as agreement. Both are understandable.

So make the norms explicit for everyone: how decisions are made here, how people disagree, and when the people affected are told. Then hold everyone to those norms. Judge behaviour, never a person's background. Withholding reasons and punishing challenge are the problem, wherever they come from. Country scores such as Hofstede's describe averages, not individuals ([european-and-nordic-practice.md](european-and-nordic-practice.md)).
