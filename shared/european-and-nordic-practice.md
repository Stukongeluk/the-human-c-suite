# European and Nordic practice: change without discarding people

Read this when demand drops, roles change or end, AI or monitoring tools come into the workplace, works councils or unions are involved, the company operates in Europe, or a leader wants a culture with more trust and less hierarchy.

The Nordic countries, Germany and the Netherlands have spent decades learning how to change companies quickly without leaving people to carry the cost alone. Some of these practices are law where the company operates; all of them are good management anywhere. Use the law as the floor, not the goal.

This is not legal advice. Rules differ per country, depend on company size and collective agreements, and change often: several did in 2025 and 2026. Date what you cite and check it with HR and employment counsel before anyone relies on it.

## 1. Five values worth borrowing

1. **Trust instead of control.** In low power-distance cultures, people expect to be consulted and to disagree openly with their manager. On Hofstede's power distance index, Denmark scores 18, Sweden and Norway 31, Finland 33, Germany 35, the Netherlands 38 and the United States 40, against France's 68. In practice, leaders set direction, limits and outcomes, and teams decide how to get there. Copenhagen began a public-sector "trust reform" in 2012 and went looking for reporting and control demands that got in the way of the work. Most employees welcomed it, but it took years and was never fully implemented. Researchers concluded that trust and control work best as complements: a few controls that matter, not none.
   - Apply it: remove a report, approval step or status meeting that nobody uses. Replace activity tracking with outcomes the team helped set. Keep the controls that protect safety, money, security and customers.
2. **Decide with people, not about them.** Works councils, worker board seats and agreements between employers and unions give employees a real say, and the evidence doesn't show the feared costs. When Germany required one third of board seats for worker representatives, firms paid no higher wages and built 40–50% larger capital stocks, and they outsourced less. The OECD finds that AI adoption tends to turn out better for workers when their representatives are consulted, and that consultation can produce designs that keep the productivity gain and improve job quality.
   - Apply it: bring representatives in at the problem stage, with the real numbers and enough time, while they can still change the plan. Treat their objections as free risk analysis.
3. **Protect the person, not the job.** Denmark's "flexicurity" rests on three legs: employers can hire and dismiss fairly easily, people who lose their job get a generous safety net, and active labour-market policy helps them into new work. Sweden's job security councils (such as TRR, since 1974) are funded by employers through collective agreements and step in as soon as notice is given, with coaching, training and money. The OECD found that over 85% of displaced Swedish workers found a new job within a year, the highest share in the OECD.
   - Apply it: change happens, but nobody goes through it alone. Fund support from the day of notice, not after the last day.
4. **A working life that lasts.** Norway's government, employers and unions have run a joint agreement for a more inclusive working life since 2001 (the IA agreement, now 2025–2028) to cut sickness absence and keep people from dropping out of work. People working in companies that signed it were more likely to stay in work after long-term sick leave. Iceland's trials from 2015 to 2019 cut the week to 35–36 hours without cutting pay for about 2,500 workers. Productivity and service stayed the same or improved in most workplaces and wellbeing rose sharply; afterwards 86% of Iceland's workforce moved to shorter hours or gained the right to.
   - Apply it: plan for sustainable hours, a right to switch off and early, practical help to keep people at work after illness. Treat burnout as a planning failure, not a personal weakness.
5. **Consensus that holds.** Big changes are negotiated rather than announced. Sweden's 2022 reform, which made dismissal rules more flexible and paired them with paid study support for adults, was built on an agreement between employers and unions. Consensus is slower to reach and faster to implement, because the people who must carry the change helped design it.
   - Apply it: spend the time up front. A plan the affected people helped shape survives contact with reality better than one they first hear about at a town hall.

## 2. Practices by situation

| Situation | European practice | What a company can do anywhere |
|---|---|---|
| Demand drops, and the drop will pass | Short-time work, such as Germany's Kurzarbeit: fewer hours for many people, with public money topping up pay, instead of dismissals | Reduce hours across the affected teams, leaders first, and use a public scheme where one exists. Use the freed time for training. Don't use it to postpone a structural decline (see section 4) |
| Roles change or disappear (AI, automation, strategy) | Swedish job security councils from the day of notice; Finland's duty to fund training for dismissed staff; Swedish paid study for adults | Offer coaching, paid training and job-search support from the first day, with a budget per person (Finland's legal minimum is a training value of one month's salary). Give priority for rehiring |
| Skills for the next five years | Danish competence funds: in the industry agreement, up to two weeks a year of self-chosen training, with most lost wages covered | Give every employee a yearly learning budget and paid time they choose themselves, not only training their current role requires |
| AI, analytics or monitoring tools at work | Co-determination on monitoring systems (Germany, Netherlands); the AI Act's duty, from December 2027, to inform before using high-risk AI at work | Involve representatives before choosing the tool. Agree its purpose, the data it uses and who sees what. Report on teams, never rank individuals, and explain how any automated decision about a person is made and how to challenge it |
| Restructuring | EU collective redundancies rules: consult in good time with a view to reaching an agreement, including ways to avoid or reduce dismissals and to soften them through redeployment and retraining | Run consultation as a joint design session: share the numbers, present alternatives, and change the plan when representatives find a better route |
| Pay and promotion | The EU Pay Transparency Directive: pay ranges before the interview, no questions about pay history, gender pay gap reporting | Publish pay ranges and how people progress, and check and close unexplained gaps, before the law makes you |
| An always-on culture | A legal right to disconnect in several countries, such as France, Spain, Belgium and Portugal | Agree hours when nobody is expected to answer. Roster and pay on-call, and make sure leaders don't send late-night messages that look like orders |
| Illness and long absence | Norway's inclusive working life agreement: early dialogue, adapted work, staying in contact | Adapt the work before the person drops out. Keep contact warm and practical, and judge managers on how many people return |

## 3. Country quick reference

Checked in November 2026; verify before relying on any of it.

**European Union**
- **Collective redundancies (Directive 98/59/EC):** inform and consult workers' representatives in good time with a view to reaching an agreement, and notify the authorities. Each member state sets its own thresholds and procedure.
- **Information and consultation (Directive 2002/14/EC):** a general right to information and consultation on developments that affect jobs and work organisation, in undertakings with 50 or more employees or establishments with 20 or more, as the member state chooses.
- **AI Act:**
  - Emotion recognition in the workplace has been prohibited since February 2025, except for medical or safety reasons.
  - AI used for recruitment, decisions on promotion or dismissal, task allocation, or monitoring and evaluating workers is high-risk (Annex III). From 2 December 2027, employers must inform workers' representatives and the affected workers before using a high-risk system at work (Article 26(7)). The 2026 Digital Omnibus moved this date from August 2026, so it doesn't apply yet; say so when you mention it. National information, consultation and co-determination rules already apply.
  - Since August 2026, an AI system that talks to people, such as a support bot, must tell them they are dealing with an AI unless that's obvious (Article 50). That's a disclosure duty, not a right to reach a human, though offering a human route is good practice.
- **Platform Work Directive (2024/2831):** digital labour platforms must inform and consult workers and their representatives about automated monitoring and decision-making, and ensure human oversight. It must be transposed by 2 December 2026, and most member states are behind.
- **Pay Transparency Directive (2023/970):** the transposition deadline was 7 June 2026 and most member states missed it; the Commission has said it won't pause the directive. Pay gap reporting starts in 2027 for employers with 150 or more employees and in 2031 for those with 100 to 149.
- **Sustainability reporting (CSRD, after Omnibus I):** from financial years starting in 2027, mandatory reporting, including on the company's own workforce (ESRS S1), applies only to companies with more than 1,000 employees and €450 million turnover. Smaller companies can report voluntarily under a simplified standard.
- **Right to disconnect:** no EU law yet. The Commission's consultation on a Quality Jobs Act, which closed in September 2026, covers the right to disconnect, telework and algorithmic management; the Commission planned a proposal for late 2026.

**Netherlands**
- A works council is mandatory from 50 employees.
- It has an advisory right on major decisions such as reorganisations (article 25 WOR), with appeal to the Enterprise Chamber. It has a consent right on social policy, including systems suited to monitoring attendance, behaviour or performance (article 27(1)(l) WOR).
- Collective dismissal of 20 or more people in one UWV work area within three months requires notifying UWV and the unions and consulting them (WMCO). Settlement agreements the employer initiates count towards the 20, and the notification comes before the decision is final. Dismissed employees generally get a statutory transition payment. Employer notice periods run from one to four months by length of service, minus the time a UWV procedure took.
- Short-time work (werktijdverkorting) currently covers only calamities outside normal business risk. A bill to replace it with a broader crisis scheme (Wet personeelsbehoud bij crisis) was before parliament in 2026; check whether it has passed.

**Germany**
- A works council exists only where employees have elected one (possible from five employees), so ask whether there is one rather than assuming.
- Where there is one, it has a co-determination right over technical systems suited to monitoring employees' behaviour or performance (§87(1) no. 6 BetrVG), which covers many analytics, productivity and AI tools. Temporarily reducing working hours, as in Kurzarbeit, also needs its agreement (§87(1) no. 3 BetrVG).
- For major operational changes in companies with more than 20 employees, the employer negotiates a reconciliation of interests and a social plan with the works council (§§111–112 BetrVG).
- Collective dismissals above set thresholds (for example 10% or more than 25 people in an establishment of 60 to 499 within 30 days) must be notified to the employment agency in advance (§17 KSchG). Termination agreements the employer initiates count towards the threshold, and mistakes in the notification can make the dismissals void.
- Where the Dismissal Protection Act applies (more than ten employees, and more than six months' service), dismissals for operational reasons need a social selection (Sozialauswahl) among comparable employees, weighing length of service, age, maintenance obligations and severe disability (§1(3) KSchG). Performance rankings are not a selection method.
- Statutory notice periods grow with service, up to seven months to the end of a month after 20 years (§622 BGB).
- Supervisory boards have one third worker representatives from 500 employees and half from 2,000.
- **Kurzarbeitergeld:** the maximum duration is 24 months until 31 December 2026 and falls back to the statutory 12 months from 1 January 2027, unless the government extends it again.

**Sweden**
- The Co-determination Act (MBL) requires employers to negotiate with the unions before major changes.
- In redundancies, the Employment Protection Act (LAS) sets a selection order by length of service, unless a collective agreement or a list agreed with the union says otherwise. There is one order per operational unit and per collective agreement area; job category is not a criterion. Since 2022 every employer may exempt three employees from it. Leaders can't simply choose who stays. Notice periods run from one month to six months by length of service.
- People dismissed for lack of work generally have priority for re-employment for nine months. The priority applies to people with more than twelve months' employment in the last three years.
- Dismissing five or more people must be notified to the Public Employment Service (Arbetsförmedlingen) in advance: at least two months ahead for 5 to 25 people, four months for 26 to 100 and six months for more than 100.
- Job security councils are funded through collective agreements; TRR covers white-collar workers in the private sector.
- Since 2022, adults with a long work history can get transition study support (omställningsstudiestöd) of about 80% of salary, up to a ceiling, for up to roughly a year of full-time study. Nearly 21,000 people used it in 2025, seven in ten of them over 40.

**Denmark**
- Wages and most working conditions are set by collective agreements rather than law; there is no statutory minimum wage.
- Sectoral competence funds pay for self-chosen training.
- Trust-based management is spreading in the public sector, led by Copenhagen.

**Norway**
- Government, employers and unions renew the inclusive working life (IA) agreement every few years; the current one runs from 2025 to 2028.
- The Working Environment Act gives employees strong participation rights, with working environment committees from 50 employees.

**Finland**
- **Co-operation Act:** since 1 July 2025, the full act applies from 50 employees. Companies with 20 to 49 employees negotiate only when the planned measures affect at least 20 employees within 90 days. Minimum negotiation periods were shortened, which unions opposed.
- **Change security:** employers with 30 or more employees must offer training or coaching to people dismissed for economic reasons after five years of service, worth at least one month's salary. People aged 55 or over get more.
- Since 1 June 2026, only employers with 50 or more employees must offer the work to people they dismissed if they need staff for similar work within four months (six months after 12 years of service); collective agreements can still require it of smaller employers. The notice for temporary lay-offs fell from 14 to seven days at the same time.

## 4. What doesn't travel, and the evidence's limits

- **The state does part of the work.** Nordic models rest on high trust, strong unions and tax-funded safety nets. A company can copy the practices, not the welfare state. Where the public safety net is thin, the company's own package matters more, not less.
- **Short-time work is a bridge, not a destination.** Germany's scheme kept an estimated 2.2 million jobs at the peak of the COVID crisis in 2020 (IMK). But Germany's IAB warned in 2026 that extending it during a structural crisis can delay changes the company needs, and some support goes to jobs that would have survived anyway. Use it when demand will come back; if it won't, redeploy and retrain instead.
- **Transition support helps, and it isn't magic.** Sweden's strong re-employment rates also reflect a strong labour market, and earnings still fall a few percent on average after displacement. An IFAU evaluation found counselling for blue-collar workers didn't shorten unemployment but did lead to longer-lasting next jobs. Swedish study support has so far reached mostly people who already had more education.
- **Shorter weeks need redesigned work.** Iceland's trials were mainly in the public sector and some outcomes were self-reported. Hours fell because meetings, schedules and tasks were redesigned, not because people did the same work faster.
- **Agreements can miss their targets.** Norway's first IA agreement aimed to cut sickness absence by 20% and didn't reach it. The parties extended it anyway and have renewed it ever since.
- **The rules are moving, in both directions.** Finland reduced its consultation duties in 2025, the EU cut sustainability reporting scope by about 90% and delayed AI Act obligations, and pay transparency and platform work rules are arriving late. Check the current state and date it.
- **Country scores aren't people.** Hofstede's scores are national averages from old surveys. Use them to understand expectations, never to stereotype a colleague.

## 5. Using this in an answer

- Use the practice, not the lecture: one line that names it, why it works and what it costs, then what to do. Most answers need one practice, not a tour of the Nordic model.
- Pick what fits the company's countries and size. For a company in several countries, the strictest rule that applies usually sets the process, and doing that everywhere is often simpler and fairer.
- Model when savings from dismissals really arrive. Consultation, notice periods and transition or severance payments mean the first-year saving is often well below the annual figure, while the costs come first.
- Check what exists before relying on it: whether there is a works council or a union agreement, which collective agreement applies, and whether a public scheme is open to this company. Ask when you don't know.
- In documents for others, cite the original source, not this file.
