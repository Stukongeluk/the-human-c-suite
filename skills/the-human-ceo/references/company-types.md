# Company types: fit the advice to the organisation

Read this when the organisation isn't a software company, when its ownership or stage shapes the decision, or when the people affected work in factories, stores, wards, warehouses, sites or public services.

A startup with nine months of runway, a third-generation family manufacturer, a hospital, a municipality and a fund-owned services group face different pressures, answer to different owners and have different levers. The values don't change: people are not the first cost to cut, leaders go first, the long term counts and humans decide. What changes is how you make the case, which levers exist and where the harm lands. Identify the type early, say which type you assumed, and adjust.

## 1. What changes and what doesn't

- **The same everywhere:** the non-negotiables, the cost ladder (least human harm first), facts before opinions, team-level measures, voice for the people affected, and leaders owning the result.
- **What differs:**
  - *Who you persuade:* a founder and investors, a family, a board and public shareholders, a fund with an exit date, members, funders, or elected officials and citizens.
  - *The long-term argument in their terms:* runway, the next generation, enterprise value at exit, the mission, public trust.
  - *The systems of record:* an order book, a till, a roster, a patient record, a case system.
  - *The legal floor:* sector rules, safety law, procurement law, public-law duties, co-determination.
  - *Who carries the cost:* frontline and shift workers, subcontractors, agency staff and the people they serve, who often have the least voice and the thinnest buffer.
- **Frontline work is not office work.** Hours, schedules, staffing levels, pace and safety are where decisions hurt or help people every day. A labour budget, a roster or a quota is a people decision, even when it is presented as an operations or software decision.

## 2. Ownership and stage

| Type | What drives decisions | The long-term case, in their terms | Humane levers that fit | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Startup or scale-up** (founder- and investor-funded) | Runway, growth targets, the next funding round | Know whether you are "default alive": profitable on current money and growth, without another round (Graham, 2015). Hiring too fast is the main killer; the most humane layoff is the one you never needed | Monthly runway scenarios shared with the team, slower hiring, burn and pricing first, founders' pay first. If cuts can't be avoided: one cut deep enough not to repeat, tied to the new strategy, with generous terms (Airbnb, 2020: at least 14 weeks' pay, equity cliff waived, recruiters turned into a placement team) | Overtime and crunch used as a substitute for runway; equity promised as pay; a second and third round of cuts |
| **SME or family business** | The owner's money and name, the bank, succession, few buffers, often no HR department | Family firms that plan for the next generation run frugally, borrow less, avoid layoffs and keep people longer; they trail in booms and hold up better in downturns (Kachaner, Stalk and Bloch, 2012). Reputation in the region is an asset | The owner's draw and dividend go first; reduced hours or a short-time scheme for a temporary dip; keep apprentices and scarce skills; talk to the bank early with a cash forecast | One person decides everything; loyalty mistaken for "no feedback needed"; family members promoted past better candidates; succession left unplanned |
| **Listed company** | Quarterly earnings, guidance, analysts, activists, buybacks | Most finance chiefs say they would give up economic value to smooth earnings, and companies run for the long term grew revenue and earnings faster ([evidence.md](evidence.md)). Long-term shareholders want a durable plan | Guide on the metrics that build value (retention, quality, capability), explain investment openly, use the board and long-term investors | R&D, training or maintenance cut to make a quarter; layoffs announced to lift the share price; buybacks funded by cuts |
| **Owned by private equity or another fund** | An exit in a few years, debt to service, EBITDA and the multiple | Effects differ hugely by deal: employment fell 13% after buyouts of listed firms but rose 13% after buyouts of private firms, with productivity up 8% on average (Davis et al.). A buyer pays for retained customers, recurring revenue and capable teams; due diligence checks churn and key-person risk | Operational improvement before headcount; management incentives tied to retention, quality and customer measures as well as EBITDA; covenant headroom planned, not discovered | Cost-out just before the sale that shows up as churn in due diligence; repeated reorganisations; dividend recapitalisations that load the company with debt |
| **Employee-owned, cooperative or foundation-owned** | Members' or the foundation's purpose, slower capital, members' voice | Firms with broad employee ownership laid off about half as many workers in the 2001 and 2008 recessions (Kurtulus and Kruse, 2017) | Shared sacrifice designed in advance; redeployment across the group; members involved in the numbers early | Insiders protected at the expense of temporary staff and new hires; decisions that stall because no one owns them |
| **Non-profit, charity or social enterprise** | Funders, restricted grants, overhead ratios, volunteers, mission | Underfunding overhead (IT, finance, training, fundraising) traps non-profits in a "starvation cycle" that weakens the mission (Gregory and Howard, 2009). Outcomes for beneficiaries depend on stable, skilled staff | Full-cost budgets shown honestly to funders; a reserves policy; diversified income; protect the people who deliver the mission | Low pay and overwork excused by "passion"; frontline roles cut to keep the overhead ratio looking good; mission drift to follow money |
| **Public sector or state-owned** | Budgets set politically, accountability, procurement and public law, citizens who can't take their business elsewhere | Public trust is the asset. When a system is presumed right and people aren't heard, citizens and staff carry the harm: the UK Post Office's Horizon cases, where the High Court found in 2019 that bugs in the Horizon software could have caused the shortfalls blamed on sub-postmasters, and the Dutch childcare benefits scandal, where the tax authority, partly through risk profiling, wrongly accused more than 20,000 families of fraud and the cabinet resigned in January 2021 | Decisions with reasons people can challenge; human review of automated decisions about people; fixing the process before chasing the people who struggle with it; keeping core expertise in-house | Targets that reward the wrong behaviour; "the computer says no"; core expertise outsourced to consultants; budget cuts passed straight to the front line |

Ownership mixes are common (a family firm with a PE minority, a state-owned company run commercially, a startup inside a listed group). Use the pressures that actually apply, and name whose interests a decision serves.

## 3. Sectors and frontline work

| Sector | Where value and risk sit | Systems of record (read-only, aggregated) | Humane practice with evidence | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Manufacturing and industry** | Safety, quality, uptime, energy and materials, scarce skilled trades that take years to rebuild | ERP, MES, quality (scrap, rework, complaints), maintenance (CMMS), safety and near-miss logs, process historians | Safety as the lead measure of operational discipline: at Alcoa under Paul O'Neill the lost-workday rate fell from 1.86 to about 0.2 per 100 workers while market value rose from $3 billion to about $27.5 billion. In the 2008 downturn Toyota kept idle US plant workers on pay for training and improvement work instead of laying them off. Use short-time work for a temporary dip ([european-and-nordic-practice.md](european-and-nordic-practice.md)) | Maintenance and training cut now, breakdowns and quality failures later; experts retiring without knowledge transfer; vision or sensor systems turned into individual surveillance |
| **Retail, hospitality and frontline service** | Availability, service, staff turnover; labour is the largest controllable cost, so it gets cut first | POS, workforce management and rostering, stock accuracy, store-level customer scores, turnover and absence per store | Stable schedules pay: in a randomised trial in 28 Gap stores, more predictable schedules raised median sales by 7% and labour productivity by 5%, and Gap moved all US stores to two weeks' notice without on-call shifts. Good-jobs retailers (Costco, Mercadona, Trader Joe's, QuikTrip) invest in staff through operational choices: simplify, cross-train, run with some slack (Ton) | Rosters optimised to the quarter hour with short notice; part-time staff without enough hours; staffing to budget rather than to demand; under-staffing that shows up as empty shelves and lost sales |
| **Healthcare and care** | Patient safety, continuity of care, burnout, agency costs | Rostering, staffing levels per ward or team, incident reports, absence, agency spend, patient-reported experience; patient records only in aggregate (health data is special-category data under the GDPR) | Staffing levels save lives: each extra patient per nurse was associated with 7% higher odds of death within 30 days of surgery, and with more burnout (Aiken et al., 2002). Buurtzorg's self-managing teams of up to 12 nurses were found by KPMG (2015) to be a low-cost home-care provider, using fewer hours per client, not explained by patient mix; counting nursing-home, physician and hospital costs too, total costs per patient were about average | Skill mix diluted to save money; visits timed to the minute; IT and admin that take time from care; agency staff as a permanent solution |
| **Logistics and warehousing** | On-time delivery, damage, peaks, pace and injuries | WMS and TMS, injury and near-miss logs, telematics in aggregate | Design pace with the people doing the work: ergonomics, rotation, predictable peaks. Quotas are now regulated in places: California's AB 701 (in force since 2022) requires large warehouses to disclose quotas and bars quotas that prevent breaks, bathroom use or compliance with safety law | Individual pick-rate rankings and "time off task" tracking; driver cameras used for discipline; agency workers used as an untrained buffer |
| **Construction, field service and utilities** | Safety, crew scheduling, subcontractor chains, weather and permits | Project controls, safety logs, field service management, subcontractor payments | Stop-work authority for anyone who sees danger; subcontractors paid on time; apprenticeships kept through downturns | Schedule pressure overriding safety; risk pushed down a subcontractor chain; disguised self-employment |
| **Professional services** (consulting, law, accounting, agencies) | People are the product: utilisation, leverage, the pipeline from junior to senior | Time and billing at team level, pipeline, realisation, attrition by cohort | Protect learning time in utilisation targets; when AI takes over junior tasks, redesign the apprenticeship instead of removing the juniors ([hard-situations.md](hard-situations.md)) | Utilisation targets that leave no time to learn; up-or-out burnout; no juniors today means no seniors in five years |
| **Financial services and other regulated industries** | Conduct, operational resilience, model risk | Incident and complaint data, control testing, model inventories | Named accountable people and tested resilience are regulatory expectations (for example the UK's Senior Managers Regime and the EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act, applying since January 2025) | Cutting operations, compliance or engineering staff until the controls exist only on paper |
| **Software and digital** | The default the rest of this skill's references are written for | See [data-sources.md](data-sources.md) | | |

## 4. Small organisations

Most companies are small. Below about 50 people there is often no HR department, no data warehouse and no board paper, and the owner knows everyone by name.

- **Keep the method, shrink the format.** One page and a conversation beat a board pack. The facts come from the bank account, the accountant's figures, the order book and talking to people.
- **The law still applies.** Small employers often have lighter procedures but not none: dismissal protection, notice and consultation rules can apply from a few employees. Point to employment counsel or the employers' association, and to [european-and-nordic-practice.md](european-and-nordic-practice.md) for country thresholds.
- **Everyone is a key person.** Losing one of eight people can lose a customer relationship or the only person who knows the machine. Map who knows what before any change.
- **The owner is the leader who goes first,** and often the person with the most to lose. Be practical about the owner's own situation as well as the staff's.

## 5. Technology and product outside software companies

For the CTO and CPTO seats when the organisation makes, sells, cares or serves rather than ships software:

- **The users are colleagues.** Nurses, drivers, store staff and machine operators live with the system every shift. Design with them on the floor, not from a requirements document, and measure whether it saves them time.
- **Uptime means the work stops.** A till, a scanner or a ward system that is down stops the work, so plan offline modes, support at shift hours and roll-outs outside peaks.
- **Operational technology is safety-critical.** Never change control systems or connect agents to them without the plant's engineers; read data from historians or exports, not from the control network.
- **Algorithmic management is a people decision.** Scheduling, task allocation, quotas and monitoring tools need the same care as a reorganisation: the people affected and their representatives involved before the tool is chosen, team-level measures, human review of decisions about people, and the legal floor (works council consent on monitoring systems, the AI Act's high-risk rules for AI used to allocate tasks or monitor workers, quota laws such as AB 701).
- **Public services:** AI used to decide on citizens' access to public benefits and services is high-risk under the AI Act (its obligations apply from December 2027), needs a data protection impact assessment and human review, and must be tested for bias, including through proxies such as nationality or postcode. Fix the process that causes the errors before building a system to catch the people who make them.
