## 1. The true cost of a cut

A layoff looks cheap because only one side of it is on the slide. Model both sides, by quarter, before anyone treats a headcount number as a saving.

| Line | What to include | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Gross saving | Fully loaded cost of the roles (salary, employer charges, benefits, equipment), from the date each person actually leaves | Payroll in aggregate, HR system |
| Delay | Consultation and information duties, notice periods and any notification period before the first exit; in much of Europe these push savings back by months | [european-and-nordic-practice.md](european-and-nordic-practice.md), counsel |
| One-off costs | Severance or statutory payments, a social plan, paid notice, outplacement and transition support, legal and advisory fees | Law, collective agreements, quotes |
| Lost work and revenue | Work that still has to be done (overtime, contractors, delays), customer accounts at risk, slower sales or delivery | CRM, account owners, delivery plans |
| Rehiring | Roles refilled within 12–24 months, recruiting fees and ramp-up time; replacing an employee can cost one-half to two times their annual salary (Gallup) | Hiring history, recruiters |
| People who leave next | A 1% downsizing was associated with 31% more voluntary turnover the following year (Trevor & Nyberg; see evidence.md) | Attrition data |
| Knowledge and quality | Lost know-how, errors, incidents, customer service quality | Incident, quality and support data |

Then show:
- the **net saving per quarter and per year**, including the quarter the decision is meant to fix;
- **break-even**: how much lost revenue or extra attrition would wipe out the saving;
- the same table for each alternative on the cost ladder, and for "do nothing".

Evidence to keep in mind: downsizing has been associated with lower subsequent profitability, most strongly in R&D-intensive, growing and low-capital-intensity industries (Guthrie & Datta, 2008). Cost programmes rarely stick: one line of research found only 10% of cost reduction programmes showed sustained results three years later (cited by McKinsey), and a Bain study of 470 companies found only 12% of transformations delivered sustainable performance improvement, while two-thirds of executives had run three or more transformations in five years.

## 2. Cash before cuts

When the problem is cash, solve for cash, in the order that harms the fewest people:

- **A 13-week cash forecast,** updated weekly, with base and downside cases. It shows when the real pinch comes and how much each lever buys.
- **Working capital:** collect receivables (invoicing on time, disputes resolved, terms enforced), right-size inventory, and time capex. Don't stretch payments to small suppliers to fix your own cash: it moves your problem onto people with less room than you.
- **Spend:** vendors, licences, cloud, office space, energy, consultants. Renegotiate before you cancel.
- **Revenue and pricing:** price increases where value supports them, revenue leakage (unbilled work, discounts nobody approved), churn.
- **Financing:** talk to the bank before a breach, not after: a waiver, an amendment or a reset of covenants costs money but is often cheaper than the alternatives. Owners' contribution, a bridge from existing investors, public guarantee or short-time work schemes where they exist (check the current terms).
- **Owners and leaders first:** dividends, distributions, owners' drawings and leadership bonuses stop before frontline jobs.

## 3. Budgets without blanket percentages

"Cut every cost centre by 8%" feels fair and is usually the worst option: it cuts the efficient and the bloated alike, it lands on payroll because that is most of most budgets, and it rarely sticks.

Instead:
- **Start from the target's purpose:** the cash, margin or covenant number the board actually needs, and by when.
- **Zero-base the spend, not the people:** challenge every non-payroll line from zero, by activity and vendor.
- **Fund what creates value, cut what doesn't:** rank initiatives by return and strategic fit, and stop low-value work explicitly instead of shaving everything.
- **Plan headcount through flows:** hiring freeze with exceptions, natural attrition, internal moves. Show the attrition you expect and the roles that must still be filled.
- **Protect investment with explicit criteria:** training, maintenance, security, quality and customer-facing capacity are cut only with their future cost written next to the saving.
- **Leaders first:** leadership bonuses and perks are part of the budget conversation, not exempt from it.
- **Owners per line:** every saving has an owner, a date and a measure, and the budget says what stops or waits.

## 4. Honest numbers: adjusted figures and one-off charges

- **"One-off" means once.** Restructuring that happens every year is a cost of running the business. Adjusted measures that exclude it overstate profit and make cuts look free.
- **Rules agree.** For US-listed companies, SEC rules forbid adjusting a non-GAAP performance measure to remove items labelled non-recurring, infrequent or unusual when a similar charge is reasonably likely within two years or occurred in the prior two. Elsewhere, treat that as good practice.
- **Reconcile everything:** every adjusted figure next to the reported one, with each adjustment named and explained.
- **Show what the P&L hides:** the cost of attrition and rehiring, training as an investment, and the people indicators (regretted attrition, time to fill, engagement) next to the financials in board packs.
- **Label estimates,** state assumptions, and show sensitivities on the two or three numbers that matter most.

## 5. Incentives that reward the long term

People do what they are paid to do. A bonus on EBITDA alone pays for cutting training, maintenance and people this year and leaving the bill for next year.

- **Balance the scorecard:** financial results plus customer measures (retention, satisfaction), people measures (regretted attrition, engagement, safety) and quality or risk measures.
- **Make part of it multi-year,** paid in deferred cash or shares that vest over three or more years.
- **Define the profit measure honestly:** recurring restructuring stays in; no bonus for a cut that costs more than it saves.
- **Malus and clawback** for results later found to be wrong or bought at the long term's expense.
- **Never pay a bonus for headcount reduction** as such.
- **Leaders go first:** when results disappoint, leadership bonuses and pay are cut before frontline jobs, and the scheme says so in advance.

## 6. Capital allocation: buybacks, dividends and investment

- **Invest first** in what clears the company's hurdle rate (its cost of capital plus risk), including people, training and maintenance. Return to shareholders what remains as genuine excess cash.
- **No buybacks to hit an earnings-per-share target.** Firms that would just have missed analysts' EPS forecasts were sharply more likely to buy back shares, and those EPS-driven buybacks went with cuts in employment and investment (Almeida, Fos & Kronlund, 2016).
- **Know the pattern you'd be joining:** from 2003 to 2012, 449 S&P 500 companies spent 54% of their earnings on buybacks and 37% on dividends (Lazonick, 2014).
- **Never fund a payout with layoffs or borrowing** to make a short-term number.
- **Explain it in the owners' terms:** long-term returns, a stable dividend policy, and why reinvestment beats a buyback when it clears the hurdle.

## 7. Banks, investors, boards and funders

Make the case in their own terms:

- **A bank:** the 13-week cash forecast, covenant headroom, base and downside scenarios, the actions each scenario triggers, and what owners and leaders are contributing. Go early.
- **A fund owner:** value at exit; a buyer pays for retained customers, recurring revenue and capable teams, and due diligence finds cuts that cost customers.
- **Listed shareholders:** long-term returns, honest guidance, and investment that clears the hurdle rate.
- **A family owner:** the next generation and the firm's name in its region.
- **Funders of a non-profit or public body:** full-cost budgets; underfunded overhead weakens the mission ([company-types.md](company-types.md)).

## 8. AI business cases

- **Size tasks, not jobs:** which tasks the AI does today at the required quality, measured in a pilot against a baseline.
- **Count the full cost:** licences and usage, integration, data work, controls, monitoring, error handling and the people who review the output.
- **Book the benefit as capacity first:** hours freed, then where they go (backlog, better service, new work), before any headcount effect. Book no payroll saving before the pilot gate has shown the hours are really freed.
- **Cost the staged plan like the alternatives:** it carries its own licences, data work, controls, review and exception handling too.
- **Let the evidence set the number:** don't hand the board's percentage back with a later date. Give a range from the task sizing, and name the gate at which it becomes a commitment.
- **Keep the controls:** segregation of duties, audit trails, and a human who signs off on what affects money or people.
- **Review on real results,** quarterly, and stop or scale on evidence.
