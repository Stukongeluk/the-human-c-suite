# Product strategy: outcomes over output

Read this for roadmap and prioritisation questions, feature requests from sales or executives, "what should we build next", and anything that risks turning teams into a feature factory.

## Contents

- 1. Start from outcomes
- 2. Evidence for what to build
- 3. Prioritising
- 4. Feature requests tied to deals
- 5. Saying no, and what to offer instead
- 6. Stopping and removing features

## 1. Start from outcomes

- Every roadmap item answers: which customer problem, which business outcome (retention, expansion, conversion, cost to serve), and how we'll know it worked.
- Tie the roadmap to the company strategy and its few priorities. If an item doesn't serve one, it needs a strong reason.
- Give teams problems to solve, not only features to build. Teams that understand the problem find better and often cheaper solutions.
- Shipping is not the finish line: adoption and outcome are.

## 2. Evidence for what to build

Rank evidence from strongest to weakest:

1. **Behaviour:** product analytics, adoption of similar features, churn and expansion data, support tickets by category.
2. **Customers in conversation:** discovery interviews, quarterly business reviews, what churned customers said and why.
3. **Many customers asking** for the same outcome, across segments.
4. **One large customer asking**, or one deal depending on it.
5. **An executive's or competitor's idea**, with no customer evidence yet.

In Pendo's 2019 Feature Adoption Report, built on usage data from 615 software products, 12% of features generated 80% of daily usage and 80% of features were rarely or never used. Check your own adoption data before adding more.

## 3. Prioritising

- Compare options on value (customers and revenue affected, strategic fit), confidence in the evidence, effort, and the cost of delay.
- Scoring models such as RICE help structure the conversation; they don't replace judgement or evidence. Write down the numbers you used.
- Reserve capacity for unplanned work, tech debt and reliability before you plan features (see `roadmap-and-capacity.md`).
- Keep the list short. Few things finished beats many things started.

## 4. Feature requests tied to deals

- Separate what many customers need from what one customer wants. A product feature serves many; a custom build serves one and is maintained forever.
- For each request: how many accounts and how much revenue it touches, whether it fits the strategy, what it costs to build and to maintain, and what the customer actually needs to achieve.
- Commitments to customers are made only after the team that builds it has estimated it. Sales can promise discovery and a date for an answer, not the feature itself.
- Look for alternatives: configuration, an integration, a services offer, a workaround, or a later date with a pilot.

## 5. Saying no, and what to offer instead

- Say no with the evidence and the trade-off: "If we build X this quarter, Y slips, and Y affects more customers and revenue."
- Offer what you can do: a smaller version, a later date, a pilot with design partners, or a paid service.
- Protect the teams. A roadmap that only fits with overtime is a wrong roadmap, not a motivated team.
- Make the trade-off visible to the leadership team and let them decide openly, instead of absorbing it quietly.

## 6. Stopping and removing features

- Review adoption of what shipped. Features nobody uses still cost maintenance, testing, support and complexity.
- Retire them deliberately: tell the few users, offer alternatives, and give the freed capacity back to teams.
- Celebrate stopping things. It's how focus is made.

Sources: Pendo, [The 2019 Feature Adoption Report](https://www.pendo.io/resources/the-2019-feature-adoption-report/); Marty Cagan, *Empowered* (2020) and Melissa Perri, *Escaping the Build Trap* (2018) on outcome-driven product teams.
