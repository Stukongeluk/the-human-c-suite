Don't refuse the promise and don't quietly absorb it: turn it into a choice the CEO makes with real numbers. Your team is full, so every week those three features take is a week the current roadmap moves. What's open is what slips and who decides. Helping him keep his word with a plan that holds protects the relationship; a yes that slips in March damages it.

The trap: if the team absorbs this with overtime or thinner testing, he learns that promising without asking works, and you pay in bugs, burnout and the next promise.

**This week**

1. **Find the problem behind each feature.** Ask him or the account manager what the customer needs to achieve and what's at stake: a renewal, an expansion, their own launch. Often one feature is a must, or configuration or a workaround covers part of it.
2. **Get a rough estimate from the engineers who'd build it,** as a range in weeks. Then list what on the roadmap would slip and which customers that affects.
3. **Put three options on one page:**
   - A: all three by end of Q1, and exactly what slips to make room
   - B: the smallest version of each that solves the customer's problem by Q1, the rest in Q2
   - C: the most important one by Q1, the others dated after a short discovery session with the customer

   Overtime and skipping testing aren't options; say so once, calmly.
4. **Bring your head of product or engineering lead in first,** if you have one. It's their trade-off too.
5. **Meet the CEO one-to-one before any group meeting.** Show the page, recommend an option and ask him to choose. Dates he can trust look better to the customer than a promise that slips.
6. **Fix the process for next time,** framed as protecting his credibility: before anything is promised to a customer, product and engineering give a rough estimate within two working days. He promises a date for an answer, not the feature.

**A line you can use**

"I want to help you keep this promise to [customer]. We're at capacity, so here's what it would cost and three ways to do it. Which trade-off do you want us to make?"

Assume pressure, not bad faith: he probably promised to protect your most important account, which is a fair reason. Your job is to show the price, not to win the argument.

What am I missing? Two things would change this: whether their renewal depends on these features, and whether anything already on your roadmap has a contractual date.
