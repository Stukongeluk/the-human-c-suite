Don't absorb this quietly, and don't fight the promise head-on. Within a week, bring the CEO a one-page trade-off: what the three features cost, what drops out of Q1 if they go in, and two or three options he can choose from. You stay on his side while the cost becomes visible instead of landing on your team.

**First steps**

1. **Find the real need.** Ask the account owner what problem each feature solves and what "end of Q1" hinges on: a renewal, a launch? Often one feature is the must-have and the others can be smaller, later or a workaround.
2. **Get an estimate, not a guess.** With your engineering lead, get rough ranges based on how long similar work took. Keep time for incidents, support and tech debt in; it doesn't disappear because Q1 got busier.
3. **Sort product from custom.** A feature other customers would use is roadmap work. One only this customer needs is maintained forever, so it deserves a harder look or a paid services option.
4. **Build the options**, for example:
   - **A:** all three by Q1, naming what slips and which customers and revenue that hits
   - **B:** the most important one by Q1, the other two dated in Q2 with early access
   - **C:** smaller first versions of all three by Q1, completed in Q2

   Leave out "the team works harder": overtime and skipped testing come back as incidents and people leaving.
5. **Talk to him privately first**, then confirm in writing. Bring your engineering lead; the estimate is theirs.

**A line you can use**

"I want to help you deliver what you promised [customer]. The team is fully booked for Q1, so adding three features means something else moves. Here are the estimates and three options. Which trade-off do you want us to make?"

**Protecting the relationship**

- Assume pressure, not bad intent. He likely said yes to keep a big account happy.
- Let him save face: a dated plan shared now usually lands far better with the customer than a missed date in March.
- Afterwards, propose one rule: customers get an answer within two weeks, but feature dates only after the team has estimated. Pitch it as protecting his credibility.
- When he has chosen, tell your team what changed and why. Don't hand them "find a way".

What am I missing? Two things would change my advice: is a signed contract or renewal tied to these dates, and does your engineering lead see capacity as you do?
