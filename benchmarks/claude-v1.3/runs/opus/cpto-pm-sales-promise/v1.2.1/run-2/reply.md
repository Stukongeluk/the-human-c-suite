Don't say yes and don't say no yet. Within a week, bring the CEO a one-page choice: what you can deliver for this customer by end of Q1, what it pushes out of the current roadmap, and which option you recommend. Then let him decide. That makes you the person who rescued his promise, not the one who blocked it.

Timing matters most. If your Q1 ends in March, you have about 18 weeks, minus the holidays. Rescoping now is a conversation; finding out in February that it doesn't fit is a broken promise.

The trap is absorbing it quietly: saying yes, squeezing it in with overtime and letting the roadmap and your team pay. Something usually slips anyway, just later and with less warning.

**1. Reply now, briefly, without committing**

> Hi [name], thanks for letting me know about the three features for [customer]. I want to make sure we deliver what they really need by end of Q1, on a date you can stand behind. By [day] I'll have estimates from the team and a short options sheet: what we can deliver by then, what it would move out of the current roadmap, and how we could cover the rest. Could we take 30 minutes on [day] to decide together? It would help to know what exactly was agreed with them, and what's behind the Q1 date on their side.

**2. Find the problem behind the three features**

Ask the CEO or account manager, and the customer if you can: what are they trying to achieve, and what happens if it isn't there by Q1? Was anything put in writing? Often one of the three matters far more than the others.

**3. Let the team estimate, and make "at capacity" a number**

- Ask the engineers who would build it for ranges, based on how long similar work took before, not numbers made up under pressure.
- Show where the team's time already goes: current roadmap, unplanned work and incidents, support, tech debt.
- Then say it plainly, for example: "We have no spare team-weeks this quarter. These three need 8 to 12, so 8 to 12 weeks of something else moves."

**4. Bring options, not objections**

| Option | What it means |
|---|---|
| A. All three, roadmap slips | Name exactly which items move and who that affects: other customers, revenue, earlier commitments |
| B. Smallest version that meets their goal | The feature that matters most in full by Q1, the others smaller or as a pilot with this customer |
| C. Sequence | One or two by end of Q1, the rest in Q2 on a date the team has estimated |
| D. Not all of it in code | Configuration, an integration, a workaround or a services offer for part of it |

Say which one you recommend and why, then let him choose. Explicitly rule out overtime, cut testing and skipped security review: that debt comes back as incidents, bugs at this very customer and people leaving. Contractors rarely help within one quarter, because onboarding them eats the same team's time.

**5. How to have the conversation**

- Private and one-on-one first, not in a leadership meeting.
- Start from his goal: "You've made a promise to our most important customer. I want to help you keep it in a way that holds."
- Assume pressure, not disregard. He may know things about the account that you don't, such as a renewal at risk. Ask what it solves for them, and what's fixed versus still open.
- Let him save face. If the customer's expectations need resetting, offer him a short script and offer to join the call. "We've looked at this properly; here's exactly what you'll have in March, and the rest by May" often lands better than the original promise.
- If you report to a head of product or a CPTO, involve them before the meeting. This trade-off belongs at their level too.

**6. Write the decision down**

Send a short note afterwards: what was decided, what moves, why, and when you'll review it. Share it with your team and the owners of whatever slipped, so nobody hears it secondhand.

**7. Later, fix the process, not the person**

Once this is settled, propose a simple rule: before anyone commits features to a customer, product and engineering get a few working days to estimate. Anyone can promise "we'll come back to you by Friday", just not the feature itself. Pitch it as protecting him from walking back promises.

**What would change this:** if the promise is in a contract with penalties, or this account is a large share of revenue and at risk, the balance tilts towards option A, with bigger roadmap slips or people moved temporarily from other teams, with their agreement.
