**Don't say no, and don't quietly absorb it. Within the next week or two, turn the promise into a sized, visible trade-off and let the CEO choose: "Here's what it takes to keep your promise, here's what has to move to make room. Which do you want?"** If Q1 ends in March, you have about 19 weeks left, roughly 17 working weeks once the holidays are out. That's enough time to make a real choice now, and not enough to hope three features fit on top of a full plan.

The trap is the silent yes. For a team at capacity, "on top of the roadmap" can only mean overtime, cut testing or a missed date, and the conversation with your CEO in March would be far worse than the one you can have now.

### 1. Find out what was actually promised
Ask the CEO or the account lead, privately:
- What exactly did the customer ask for, and what problem are they trying to solve?
- Did he promise named features or an outcome? In writing or in a meeting? Is it tied to a renewal?
- What's at stake: how much revenue, when they renew, how likely they are to leave?

Customers often ask for a feature when a smaller version would solve their problem. If you can, join a call with the customer, framed as "making sure we build exactly what they need."

### 2. Size it with your team
Give your engineers a few days to estimate the three features as ranges, and work out what Q1 capacity is really free after leave, on-call, support and unplanned work. Use their past pace on similar work, not estimates made under pressure. Also check whether other customers want each feature. One that many accounts need is a product investment; one only this customer will use is a custom build you maintain forever.

Bring your own manager and your engineering lead in at this point. This shouldn't be you alone against the CEO.

### 3. Bring options, not a complaint
Meet the CEO one-on-one and lead with his goal, not his mistake:

| Option | Customer gets | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| A. All three by end of Q1 | Everything promised | Named roadmap items move to Q2, with who and what revenue they affect |
| B. The most important one, or slim versions of all three, by Q1; the rest on a firm Q2 date | The core of the promise, as a pilot partner | A smaller roadmap slip |
| C. Roadmap stays; alternatives now (configuration, a workaround, a services offer), features on later committed dates | A plan with dates | He resets expectations with the customer |

Say plainly what isn't an option: overtime, or skipping testing or security review. Then let him decide, and send a short note afterwards: what was decided, what moves, who tells whom. That makes it a visible leadership decision rather than a burden your team carries quietly.

### 4. Help him with the customer
If he picks B or C, offer to join the customer conversation and give him honest wording: what's coming, when, and how they can shape it. A clear plan with firm dates usually goes down better than a missed promise.

### 5. Suggest one habit for next time
"When a big customer asks, give me 48 hours and I'll get you a sized answer, so you can promise with confidence." Pitch it as protecting his credibility, not as a rule for him.

### Why this protects the relationship
Assume pressure, not bad intent: he probably did it to keep or grow your most important account, which is a fair goal. So never correct him in front of others, skip "you should have asked", and arrive with options. CEOs remember who helped them keep a promise, and who said yes and then missed the date.

Meanwhile, tell your team what's going on, involve them in the estimates, and keep the time for tech debt and reliability in the plan.

A note you could send today:

> Hi [name], I want to make sure we land the three features for [customer] well. My team is fully booked for Q1, so we'll need to make some choices together. Could we take 30 minutes this week or next? I'll bring rough estimates, what would have to move on the current roadmap, and two or three options, including one that gets them all three. It would also help to know exactly what [customer] was told, so we build what they really need.
