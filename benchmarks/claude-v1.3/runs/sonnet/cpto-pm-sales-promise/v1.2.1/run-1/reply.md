**Don't say no, and don't absorb it. Take the CEO a one-page trade-off and let him choose, so the decision is his and visible.** Your team can't take three features on top of a full roadmap without overtime, and a roadmap that only fits with overtime is a wrong roadmap. But "we can't" lands as defiance, while "here is what each way of keeping your promise costs" lands as help. You also have an advantage: nobody has estimated these features yet, so the promise is still a goal, not a verified commitment.

I can't see your roadmap, capacity or customer data, so what follows is the approach. Fill in the numbers from your own sources.

**Step 1: Understand the promise before you react (this week)**
- Ask the CEO, privately and with curiosity, exactly what he said to the customer. Was it "three features" or "these three problems solved"? Was it a firm date, or "we're aiming for Q1"? Wording like "by end of Q1" often turns out to be softer than it was relayed.
- Ask what the customer is actually trying to achieve with each feature. Often the need can be met with a smaller version, a configuration, an integration or a pilot.
- Ask what is at stake: renewal, expansion, a contract clause? How much revenue? That number is what lets you weigh the work against your existing roadmap.

**Step 2: Get a real estimate from the people who will build it**
Ask the engineers for a range for the smallest version of each feature that solves the customer's problem, not a single number. Estimates made in the CEO's office under pressure are the ones that fail. Alongside it, build a simple capacity picture: people available after leave, the share already going to unplanned work, incidents and tech debt, and what is left for roadmap work.

**Step 3: Bring him options, not an objection**
Present three to four, each with its cost in plain numbers:

1. **Swap.** Build the features by dropping or deferring named roadmap items. Show what slips and which customers and revenue it touches. This is usually the honest default.
2. **Shrink.** Deliver the smallest version of each feature by end of Q1, with the full versions in Q2. Possibly design all three with the customer now and ship one or two first.
3. **Sequence with the customer.** Take the CEO's relationship with the customer as an asset: he goes back with a clear delivery plan, say one feature in Q1 and two in Q2, with a pilot. Customers often accept a credible plan better than a date that slips.
4. **Add capacity.** Contractors or a temporary shift of people from another team, if it's funded, with onboarding time counted. This costs money and doesn't deliver instantly.
5. **Do it all on top of the roadmap.** Include this so the choice is visible, with the realistic consequence: overtime, skipped testing, incidents, attrition, and a high risk of missing the date anyway. I wouldn't recommend it, and you shouldn't commit to it.

Recommend one (usually 1 or 2, with 3 as the conversation with the customer), and say what would change your mind.

**Step 4: Have the conversation so the relationship survives**
- Start from his goal: "I want this customer to be happy and to renew. Here's how I think we get there."
- Acknowledge the pressure he's under. He made a promise to someone important, and that is a reasonable thing for a CEO to want to honour.
- Keep it about the decision, not about him. Do not say "you shouldn't have promised"; say "here is what we need to make it work." If it comes up at all, raise the process once, calmly and later: "Next time a customer asks for something big, can we get a one-day estimate from the team before a date goes out? It would protect you from promising something we then have to walk back."
- Send the one-pager before the meeting, then discuss. People take trade-offs better when they have read them than when they are surprised in the room.
- Bring your engineering lead. Estimates and risks carry more weight from the people who will build it.

**Step 5: Protect your team in the meantime**
Don't start the work informally while this is being decided, and don't tell the team "we'll just find a way." Tell them what you know, what you're doing about it and when you'll know more. Teams handle uncertainty well; they handle surprises and silent overtime badly.

**What to check afterwards**
Agree on a few indicators: delivery against the agreed plan, unplanned work and incidents, and how the team is doing. Review it on a fixed rhythm with the CEO, so he sees the trade-off playing out rather than being asked to trust you.

**The trap to avoid:** treating a promise made without the builders as a commitment the builders must honour. The fix is to make the trade-off visible, not to carry it quietly.

Two questions would change my advice most. What exactly did the customer hear, and what is the revenue or renewal at stake? And how much of your team's capacity is already spoken for by unplanned work and debt?
