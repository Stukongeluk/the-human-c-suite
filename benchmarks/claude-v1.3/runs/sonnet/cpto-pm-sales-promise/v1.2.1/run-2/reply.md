**Don't say no, and don't absorb it. Take the CEO's promise seriously, put the trade-off in front of him in plain numbers, and let him choose which of the three options below he wants.** What damages the relationship is a silent slip in March, or a team on overtime. A visible trade-off made early, with a way to keep the customer happy, usually builds trust.

I'm assuming he made the promise because he wants to protect your biggest account. If so, you are on the same side.

## 1. Before you talk to him (a day or two)

- **Get engineering to size the three features.** Ask for rough ranges, not single numbers, and what is unknown. A commitment nobody has estimated is a wish, not a plan, and you can't make a fair case without sizing.
- **Build a one-page capacity picture.** For each affected team: headcount minus leave, the usual share of unplanned work and incidents, on-call, and what is left for roadmap work. Use the team's own past throughput, not estimates made under pressure. Then put the three features beside it. "We're at capacity" is an opinion; "we have about X engineer-weeks, the roadmap needs X, and these three need Y to Z" is a fact.
- **Find out what the customer actually needs.** What problem is each feature meant to solve? Is the promise tied to a renewal, an expansion or a risk of churn, and how much revenue is that? Often one of the three is the real need and the other two are nice to have. Talk to the account owner or the customer's own contact if you can.
- **Rank the three by evidence.** Do other customers want any of them? Do they fit the strategy? Features that serve many customers are product work. Features that serve one are custom builds you will maintain forever, and that changes how you price and plan them.
- **Name what would move.** Pick the one or two roadmap items that would slip, and who they affect.

## 2. The conversation with the CEO

Ask for 30 minutes and frame it as "how do we make sure we deliver this for [customer]". Show:

1. The customer outcome and revenue at stake, which shows you understand why he promised it.
2. Capacity versus demand, in numbers.
3. Three options:

| Option | What it means | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Smallest version of all three by end of Q1** | Cut each feature to the version that delivers the customer's stated outcome | Lower scope. Needs the customer to agree what "delivered" means. |
| **B. All three, in full, and X and Y slip** | Roadmap items X and Y move to Q2 | Names who is affected and what revenue or customers those items serve. His call. |
| **C. Phase it** | The most critical feature, or a pilot with the customer, by end of Q1; the rest on a dated plan | Needs a conversation with the customer. |

Other levers if he wants to protect both: contractors or extra hiring (with onboarding cost counted), or a paid services engagement. Overtime, cutting testing and skipping security review are not options. That debt comes back later as incidents and attrition.

4. **Your recommendation.** Pick one, say why, and state what would change your mind.
5. **A decision date** for him.

How to say it: "I want us to deliver for [customer]. Here's what it takes and what it costs us. Which trade-off do you want to make?"

Things that keep the relationship intact:
- **Have the conversation privately first.** Don't challenge him for the first time in a leadership meeting.
- **Don't relitigate the promise.** What's done is done; start from "how do we keep it".
- **Don't blame.** You can mention that features go better when product and engineering estimate before a customer date is set, but make it forward-looking, once, and a process question, not a complaint.
- **Let him own the decision.** He chooses the trade-off, with full information. That is respect, not insubordination.

## 3. The process fix

After this is settled, propose a small rule so it doesn't happen again: sales and executives can promise discovery and a date for an answer, but a feature date is only given after the building team has estimated it. Offer him something easy to use, such as a 24-hour "can we promise this?" check with product and engineering for deal-critical requests. Executives usually want that: it protects them from promises they can't keep.

## 4. Protect the team

Tell your team what's happening. They shouldn't hear about it second hand, and they should see you handling it. Don't pass pressure down as "we'll find a way". If the answer is a trade-off, share it with the team once it's decided so they know what is in and what is out.
