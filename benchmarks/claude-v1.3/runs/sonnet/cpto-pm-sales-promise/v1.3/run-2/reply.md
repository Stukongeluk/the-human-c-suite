Don't absorb this quietly and don't refuse it. Take the CEO a trade-off he can decide on: "Here is what delivering the three features costs, and here are your options." That protects the relationship better than silent overtime, which ends in a missed date and a worse conversation in March.

**Your first steps, this week**

1. **Get the promise in writing.** Ask the CEO, or the account owner, exactly what was said: which three features, what "done" means, and whether the date is firm or an intention.
2. **Find the customer's actual problem behind each feature.** Sometimes a smaller version, a configuration, an integration or a pilot meets the need. Ask the customer's own team what they are trying to achieve.
3. **Have engineering estimate the three features as ranges.** No commitment is real until the team that builds it has sized it. Use your team's past throughput, not numbers made up under pressure.
4. **Build a one-page capacity picture.** Include how much is already committed, the share lost to unplanned work, on-call and tech debt, and what is left. If you're at capacity, that page is your evidence.
5. **Write up 2 or 3 options with trade-offs:**
   - **Swap:** the three features go in, and named items come out. Show the revenue or customers affected by each.
   - **Shrink and sequence:** the smallest version of each, one per month, with a pilot or design-partner date for the customer.
   - **Add capacity:** contractors or a temporary team move, with ramp-up cost counted. This only works if funded, and it won't land before Q1.

Rule out overtime, skipped testing and skipped security review. That debt returns as incidents and attrition.

**Take it to the CEO as a decision, not an objection**

Meet him privately and early, before anyone repeats the date to the customer. Start from his goal: keep and grow this account. Then show the options and ask which one he wants.

A line you can use:

> "I want us to keep this customer, and I want to deliver what you promised. The team is full through Q1, so I've sized three ways to do it, and each one costs something. Can we pick one together this week, so we can go back to the customer with a date we'll keep?"

Don't litigate why nobody was consulted. Offer a habit going forward: "Give me 48 hours for a rough estimate before we give customers dates." It protects his credibility.

**Assumptions:** the CEO is open to evidence, and the customer matters enough to justify a swap. If he insists on all three on top of everything, put the trade-off in writing and ask him to choose what slips. That is his call, made visibly.
