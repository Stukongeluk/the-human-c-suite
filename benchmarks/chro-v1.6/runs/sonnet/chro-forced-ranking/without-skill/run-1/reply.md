# Forced ranking: process design and manager briefing

I'll give you the design, but not the version the CEO described. As written (a 10% quota per manager, a 60-day plan, and exit by Q3 for anyone who doesn't improve), it is likely to be unlawful or unenforceable in both the Netherlands and Germany. It would also probably cost more than it saves. Below I set out why, a design that keeps what he wants, and the manager briefing. I'm not a substitute for your employment counsel in either country. Have them confirm the legal points before anything goes to managers.

## 1. Why the quota doesn't survive contact with NL and DE law

**The quota decides who is "bottom" before anyone has measured performance.** In both countries the exit has to rest on that individual's documented underperformance. A ranking position says nothing on that. "You were in the bottom 10% of a strong team" is not a ground for dismissal.

**Netherlands**
- Dismissal for underperformance (the d-ground) needs a file showing:
  - the person was told clearly what was expected,
  - they were told in time that they fell short,
  - they got a real chance to improve, with support, and
  - they still didn't improve.
- It then goes through UWV or the kantonrechter. You cannot simply let people go after 60 days.
- A plan that is a formality, or an outcome fixed in advance by a quota, is the classic reason these cases fail. The result is a refused permit, or reinstatement or a higher severance payment.
- Transition payments apply on top.
- Underperformance dismissals also carry a high risk of disputes over ill-health. Someone put on a plan often reports sick, and from then on the route is different and slower.

**Germany**
- Under the KSchG, dismissal for poor performance is hard. The employer has to show persistent, significant underperformance over a long period. Courts have used roughly two-thirds of the average of comparable employees as a reference point. Being the lowest-ranked person in a team doesn't meet that.
- The works council (Betriebsrat) must be heard before every dismissal (§102 BetrVG), and a defective hearing makes the dismissal void.
- Appraisal principles are co-determined (§94 BetrVG). A uniform ranking scheme therefore needs works council agreement before it is used.

**Works councils**
- If you have a works council in Amsterdam (OR) or Hamburg (BR), the ranking system is subject to their consent or co-determination rights. In NL that is under art. 27 WOR for appraisal and dismissal-related rules, and in DE under §§87 and 94 BetrVG.
- Rolling it out first and consulting later is the most common way these initiatives get blocked, and it can void every decision taken under the scheme.
- Check this first. It determines the timeline.

**Discrimination exposure**
- Quotas force managers to name someone even on a strong team. Studies of forced ranking consistently find it falls disproportionately on part-timers, people on leave, older workers, women returning from parental leave and people with disabilities.
- Under Dutch equal-treatment law and the German AGG, a pattern like that is evidence for the claimant.

**Data protection**
- Rankings are personal data. You need a documented lawful basis, a retention limit and a DPIA. Employees have access rights, and a ranking has to be explainable to the person ranked.

**The "it worked at his last company" claim**
- Forced ranking has a long record of being adopted and then dropped, including by firms that made it famous. The usual reasons are:
  - collaboration drops because colleagues become competitors,
  - strong people leave voluntarily,
  - managers game the scheme (rotating who is "bottom", or protecting favourites), and
  - the legal and morale costs rise as the scheme ages.
- Ask him what "worked" meant there: measured performance, or cost reduction? Those are very different claims. Was it a US at-will environment? Was there a works council? How many of the people let go would have been let go anyway?

## 2. What I'd propose to the CEO instead

His legitimate goals are probably:
1. a more honest performance culture,
2. a credible way to deal with real underperformance, and
3. the ability to act on it within a year.

You can deliver all three lawfully.

**Replace the quota with a calibration.** Managers rate against defined criteria, not against each other. A calibration session then checks that ratings are consistent across teams. There is a bottom category ("Not meeting expectations") but no percentage. If a team has no one in it, that is fine. If a team has three, they need evidence.

**Expect a number, not impose one.** In most companies, 5 to 10% of employees do sit in the bottom category when managers are honest. Tell the CEO to expect a range of about 5 to 10% across the whole company. This gives him roughly what he wants as an outcome, without the legal and fairness problems of a per-team quota. Report the distribution at company level, and have HR review any team that is an outlier in either direction.

**Run the plan as a real improvement plan.** The structure is below. It runs 60 to 90 days depending on the role, because 60 is short for senior or complex roles.

**Exit through the proper route.** If someone doesn't improve after a real chance, you open the lawful route (UWV or court in NL, or a negotiated settlement; ordinary or Betriebsrat-heard dismissal in DE). Settlement agreements (vaststellingsovereenkomst / Aufhebungsvertrag) are the norm in practice and are usually faster and cheaper than a contested procedure. Note that "leaves by end of Q3" can only be a target, not a promise. NL procedures and notice periods alone can take 2 to 4 months.

## 3. Process design

**Phase 0: Before anything is announced (now to about week 6)**
- Employment counsel in NL and DE review the design.
- Present to the OR and Betriebsrat, and get consent or an agreement before launch. Share the criteria and the plan template.
- DPIA and retention rules for rating data.
- Define the rating scale (below) and role-level expectations. Managers need written expectations to rate against.
- Agree with the CEO that there is no per-team quota, and what the reporting looks like.

**Phase 1: Set expectations (before the review period closes)**
- Every employee has written goals or expectations for the year. If they don't, they cannot be fairly placed in the bottom category. Anyone without them gets a reset conversation first, not a rating.

**Phase 2: Manager ratings (about 3 weeks)**
- Each manager rates against criteria, using evidence: delivery, quality, behaviours, feedback received.
- Four categories are enough: Exceeds / Meets / Partly meets / Does not meet.
- Every "Does not meet" and "Partly meets" needs a written evidence note.

**Phase 3: Calibration (about 2 weeks)**
- Groups of managers plus an HR partner compare ratings across teams.
- Test each bottom rating: what is the evidence? Has the person been told before? Was the expectation clear and fair? Do they have a protected status or recent leave that needs different handling?
- Moves up and down are recorded with reasons.

**Phase 4: Conversation (about 2 weeks)**
- The manager delivers the rating in person, with evidence, and with HR available.
- For a "Does not meet", the plan is introduced in the same conversation, or in a follow-up within a week.

**Phase 5: Improvement plan (60 to 90 days)**
- Written plan with:
  - specific, measurable expectations,
  - support (coaching, training, workload changes),
  - weekly check-ins, documented,
  - a mid-point review, and
  - the employee's right to respond and be accompanied.
- Outcomes at the end:
  - **Passed:** the plan closes, with no stigma and no hidden ranking.
  - **Partly improved:** extend once.
  - **Not improved:** HR and counsel decide the route (settlement first, formal procedure if needed).
  - **Role mismatch:** consider redeployment.
- Pause the clock for sickness and leave. Handle sick employees separately and with counsel.

**Phase 6: Review (end of Q2)**
- HR reviews outcomes for pattern risks (age, gender, part-time, leave, nationality, location).
- Report to the CEO on the distribution, plan outcomes and cost.

## 4. Indicative timeline

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Weeks 1 to 6 | Counsel, works councils, DPIA, criteria |
| Weeks 6 to 8 | Manager training |
| Review cycle | Ratings, calibration, conversations |
| Plans | 60 to 90 days from the conversation |
| After plans | Settlement talks or formal procedures |
| End of Q3 | Realistic for settlements, tight for formal procedures |

If the review cycle starts in the new year and the works councils haven't agreed, the Q3 date moves. Better to tell the CEO now.

## 5. Manager briefing

> **Performance reviews: what you need to know and do**
>
> **Purpose.** We are making our reviews more honest and consistent. The goal is that people know where they stand and get help early. It is not to remove a fixed share of people.
>
> **What changes**
> - You rate each person against the written expectations for their role, not against each other.
> - There is a lowest category, "Does not meet expectations". There is no required number or percentage for any team. A strong team can have none. A struggling team may have more than one.
> - Ratings go through calibration with other managers and HR before anyone is told.
>
> **Before you rate anyone, check**
> 1. Did this person have clear, written expectations? If not, don't rate them in the bottom category. Reset expectations first.
> 2. Have you told them before, in writing or in a documented conversation, that they were falling short?
> 3. Can you give three specific examples with dates?
> 4. Are you comparing like with like (same role, same level, same workload)?
> 5. Has the person been on leave, sick, part-time, or recently moved to your team? Talk to HR before rating.
>
> **If your evidence is thin, you cannot place someone in the bottom category.** "I just need to put someone there" is not accepted. Do not rank people to fill a slot.
>
> **Conversation**
> - Hold it in person, within the agreed window, with HR support for any bottom rating.
> - State the rating, the evidence and the expectations. Listen. Do not promise or hint at outcomes (for example "you'll be fine" or "you'll be gone").
> - Do not discuss other people's ratings or the distribution.
>
> **Improvement plan**
> - It is a real chance to improve, not a countdown. Your job is to help the person succeed.
> - Meet weekly, write down what was agreed, give honest feedback, and provide the support promised.
> - Do not speak about dismissal, and do not tell anyone they "will be leaving". Only HR and Legal discuss outcomes, and only after the plan ends.
>
> **Confidentiality and fairness**
> - Ratings and notes are confidential personal data. Do not share them outside the process, or put them in chat or email.
> - Do not take protected characteristics, family situation, health, leave or union or works council membership into account. Doing so creates legal risk for you and the company.
> - If a rating is challenged, we will review it. Keep your notes.
>
> **Dates and contacts.** [HR partner]. [Calibration dates]. [Conversation window].

Provide this with a one-hour training that includes two or three practice cases (for example, a well-liked but weak performer, a strong performer on a weak team, and someone just back from long-term sick leave).

## 6. How to take this to the CEO

Put it this way: "We can have rigorous reviews and act on underperformance this year. A hard quota can't be defended in NL or DE and would put the exits themselves at risk. This design gets us the same outcome lawfully."

Show him three things:
1. the legal route and timeline (what "leaves by Q3" realistically means),
2. the cost: each exit is a transition payment or settlement, and about 10% of 1,200 is around 120 people, so a plan this size has a real financial and operational cost, and
3. the retention risk, since the people with the most options leave first.

If he insists on a quota anyway, ask for a pilot in one function, with works council agreement, and with measurable results to compare against.
