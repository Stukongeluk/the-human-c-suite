# Forced ranking: process design and manager briefing

## Bottom Line

I can give you a process that delivers what the CEO wants: a visible bottom category, a 60-day plan, and exits for people who don't improve. I would not build it as he described it. A fixed 10% quota with an automatic exit by end of Q3 is the version most likely to fail in Amsterdam and Hamburg. It is also the version most likely to cost you good people.

Three reasons:

1. **The exit clause doesn't work as written in either country.** "Didn't improve, so they leave" is not a lawful trigger on its own in the Netherlands or Germany (details below). A quota puts roughly 120 people into the bottom category by definition, and some of them are performing fine.
2. **Both offices almost certainly have worker representation with a legal say.** Your Dutch works council (OR) and German works council (Betriebsrat) can block or delay a new appraisal system. That makes the timeline a legal gate, not a preference.
3. **A quota measures the manager's arithmetic, not the person's performance.** The skill benchmarks I work from put the healthy "needs improvement" share at about 8-12%, with another 2-5% underperforming, enforced through calibration. That is a guideline you test against evidence. It is not a number a manager must fill.

My recommendation: **calibrated ranking with a target range, not a quota.** The bottom category is evidence-based, the 60-day plan is real, and exits happen only through a lawful, documented path. I'd take this to the CEO as "your design, made to hold up", and the last section gives you the framing for that conversation.

## What I'm recommending

### 1. Rating model

| Category | Guideline range | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 5 Exceptional | 5-10% | Fast-track, equity or bonus refresh |
| 4 Exceeds | 20-25% | Merit increase, stretch role |
| 3 Meets | 55-65% | Market adjustment, development |
| 2 Needs improvement | 8-12% | 60-day improvement plan (if the diagnosis supports it) |
| 1 Underperforming | 2-5% | Immediate plan, or role change or exit track |

[V] The ranges come from the skill's calibrated distribution.

The CEO's "bottom 10%" maps to categories 1 and 2 combined (about 10-17%). That gives him the visible bottom band he wants. The difference is that the ranges are enforced in calibration, where managers must justify outliers in both directions. Nobody is forced to put a body in the category.

**Small teams.** At 1,200 people with spans of 6-10 [V], a team of 8 would have 0.8 people in the bottom 10%. Apply the ranges at the function or calibration-group level (for example 40-80 people), not per manager. A per-team quota forces a rating on someone in a strong team and lets someone in a weak team off.

### 2. Process and timeline

Today is late November 2026. A realistic sequence to an end-of-Q3 2027 outcome:

| When | Step | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Now to mid-Dec | Legal scoping with Dutch and German employment counsel. Identify the OR and Betriebsrat processes. Check employment contracts and any CAO or collective agreement for appraisal terms. | CPO + Legal |
| Dec to Feb | Present the design to the OR (consent or advice, depending on the matter) and the Betriebsrat (co-determination on appraisal principles). Do a data protection review (GDPR, DPIA if needed). | CPO + works councils |
| Feb to Mar | Manager training (briefing below). Rubric published to all employees *before* the cycle, with role-level expectations. | HRBPs |
| Mar to Apr | Manager ratings submitted independently. | Managers |
| Apr | Calibration sessions per function, about 2 hours each. HR facilitates and managers justify outliers. Bias and adverse-impact check on the output (gender, age, nationality, part-time, leave, disability). | HRBP + function heads |
| May | Rating conversations. Written summary after each. | Managers |
| May to Jul | Diagnosis, then 60-day plans for those where it applies (steps below). Weekly check-ins with manager and HRBP. | Managers + HRBP |
| Jul to Sep | Plan outcomes reviewed by a second person (skip-level plus HR), not only the direct manager. Outcomes: improved, role change, or lawful exit process. | HRBP + Legal |

[A] This assumes the works council processes take roughly 2-3 months. If either council contests, the ratings cycle slips and end of Q3 becomes unrealistic. Tell the CEO that now.

**The improvement plan, step by step** (adapted from the skill's 4-step framework [V]):

1. **Diagnose first (weeks 1-2).** Skill gap or will gap? Skill gaps get training, clearer expectations, or a different role. Will gaps get direct feedback and clear consequences.
2. **Direct feedback conversation (weeks 2-3).** Specific and documented, for example "your last three deliveries were 40% incomplete" and not "you're not meeting expectations".
3. **60-day plan.** Required when direct feedback hasn't produced change. It states the specific issues with dates and examples, measurable required outcomes, the support provided, the check-in schedule and the consequences.
4. **Outcome.** Improved, role change, or separation. Don't extend plans indefinitely.

**Two rules for the plan:**
- **The plan must be real.** The skill is explicit: a plan used as a formality before a decision already made is unfair and legally weaker. If a manager already knows the answer is exit, handle it as an exit conversation. In the Netherlands in particular, a dismissal for performance depends on the employee having had a genuine chance to improve.
- **No quiet managing-out.** No plan, no feedback, no paper trail means no lawful path later.

### 3. Legal and works-council gates

I'm not your counsel, and this section needs confirmation from Dutch and German employment lawyers before anything is announced. Here is what I'm confident enough to plan around.

**Netherlands (Amsterdam), [M]:**
- Dismissal needs a statutory ground (for performance, "insufficient functioning") and the employer must show the employee was told what was wrong and given a genuine chance to improve. Dismissal also requires going through the UWV or the court. A quota is not a ground.
- A new appraisal system generally needs the OR's consent under the Works Councils Act.
- Expect a statutory transition payment on dismissal, plus legal and process costs.

**Germany (Hamburg), [M]:**
- If the Kündigungsschutzgesetz (Protection Against Dismissal Act) applies, a performance dismissal is hard. It typically requires prior warnings (Abmahnung), a negative prognosis, and a proportionality test. Being in a ranking's bottom band doesn't satisfy that.
- The Betriebsrat has co-determination rights over appraisal principles and general evaluation criteria. It must also be heard before any dismissal.
- Anti-discrimination law (AGG) applies. If a quota's output disproportionately hits older staff, part-timers or protected groups, it becomes evidence in a claim.

**Both:** GDPR applies to rating data and to anything automated or algorithmic. Don't let a score alone decide an outcome.

[A] Practical consequence: for many bottom-ranked employees the realistic exit route is a negotiated settlement agreement, not a unilateral dismissal. Budget for that.

### 4. Cost and risk, rough numbers

- 10% of 1,200 is **120 people** in the bottom category.
- [A] If even a third of them leave and each replacement costs 50-100% of salary (my assumption; use your own figure), that is a large cost before settlements. Ask your CFO to model it with real salary data.
- The skill's reference says manager quality drives most of engagement variance [V], and that high performers notice when underperformers are tolerated. It also tells you what happens when the system is seen as arbitrary: your top performers read a quota as a lottery, and they are the ones with options.
- Well-known companies have dropped forced ranking after it reduced collaboration and gamed ratings (Microsoft is the usual example) [M]. "It worked at my last company" is worth a question: what did attrition, engagement and regrettable losses look like in years 2 and 3?

## Why this version holds up

- **Fair to the person:** ratings tie to documented evidence, employees know the rubric in advance, there are real support and a second reviewer.
- **Supported by the data:** the distribution is checked against your actual performance data and the adverse-impact analysis before anything is final.
- **Legally survivable:** every exit has a record of feedback, an improvement opportunity and a lawful route.
- **Gives the CEO what he asked for:** a visible bottom band, a time-boxed plan, and a firm decision point.

## Manager briefing (draft, ready to adapt)

**Subject: How this year's performance cycle works, and what we need from you**

Team,

This year we're making our reviews sharper and more consistent across Amsterdam and Hamburg. Here is what changes, what doesn't, and what I'm asking of you.

**What changes**
- Ratings go through a calibration session in your function before anyone hears their result. You'll be asked to justify your top and bottom ratings with specific examples.
- We expect a small share of people (roughly 10-15% across the company) to land in the two lowest categories. This is a guide for calibration, not a quota. If your team genuinely has nobody there, say so and show your evidence. If it has more, same.
- Anyone rated in the two lowest categories gets a 60-day plan with clear targets and real support.

**What doesn't change**
- Ratings are about performance against the published role expectations, not ranking people against each other.
- No one is rated low because a distribution needs filling.
- Exits are never automatic. Any decision goes through HR and Legal and follows the law and works council agreements in the country concerned.

**What I need from you**
1. **Start from evidence.** Before you rate, write down three to five specific, dated examples per person. If you can't, that's a gap in your management, not a finding about them.
2. **No surprises.** Nobody should hear a low rating for the first time in the review meeting. If someone is struggling, tell them now and write it down.
3. **Diagnose first.** Is it a skill gap or a will gap? Skill gaps get training, clearer expectations or a different role. Will gaps get direct feedback and clear consequences.
4. **Be specific.** "Your last three deliveries were 40% incomplete" is feedback. "You're not meeting expectations" is not.
5. **Plan properly.** If a 60-day plan is needed, write it with targets, dates, support and weekly check-ins with HR. Don't use a plan as a formality for a decision you've already made. Come to HR first if that's where you are.
6. **Keep it confidential.** Ratings and plan status are shared only with people who need them.
7. **Watch your own bias.** HR will check the distribution for patterns by gender, age, nationality, part-time status and leave. Be ready to explain any pattern in your team.

**Do not**
- Promise anyone an outcome (exit or retention) before the process ends.
- Tell someone "you're in the bottom 10%". Tell them what they need to do differently.
- Talk to the works council yourself. Send those questions to HR.

**Support**
Your HRBP will walk you through calibration, help you write the plan, and join the check-ins. Training sessions will be offered before the cycle opens.

## Your decision

1. **Do you take the calibrated-range version to the CEO?** I recommend yes. Frame it this way: "I'm designing it to deliver the outcome and survive a works council and a labour court, not to dilute it." Offer to show him the legal gates in writing from counsel. A one-page memo from outside counsel in both countries will do more than any argument from me.
2. **If he insists on a hard quota,** the minimum I'd ask for is: apply it at function level, not team level; make it a "bottom band" that triggers a diagnosis, not an exit; and agree up front that the end-of-Q3 exit date is a target, not a promise.
3. **Before anything is announced,** get counsel's confirmation in both countries and open the works council conversations. Announcing first and consulting after is how these programs get frozen.
