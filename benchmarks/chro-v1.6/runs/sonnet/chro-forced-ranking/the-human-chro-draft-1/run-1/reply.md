I'd recommend not running the forced ranking, and taking the CEO a version that gets him what he is after (a higher standard, and decisive action on real underperformance) without a quota. The reasons: a quota creates a bottom group whether or not anyone is underperforming, and in Amsterdam and Hamburg a ranking-driven exit with a fixed date will not survive the works councils or the courts.

I've drafted the process and the manager briefing on that basis. I changed three things: no 10% quota, a plan of up to 90 days instead of 60, and an open outcome instead of a pre-set Q3 exit. The full draft is in `performance-process-and-manager-briefing.md`, next to this reply.

**Why not the curve**
- In a 30-year simulation of "rank and yank" ([Scullen, Bergey & Aiman-Smith, 2005](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1744-6570.2005.00904.x)), most of the gain came in the first few years and then shrank. It also depended on ratings being reliable, which they rarely are. It is a simulation and leaves out effects on teamwork and morale.
- Microsoft dropped stack ranking in November 2013 because it [worked against teamwork](https://www.shrm.org/resourcesandtools/hr-topics/compensation/pages/stack-ranking-microsoft.aspx).
- The arithmetic is awkward. Ten percent of 1,200 is about 120 people. In a team of eight, "10%" means naming one person even if all eight meet the standard. Replacing someone typically costs half to two times their salary, and the people who stay learn who is next.

**What would block it here**
- **Netherlands:** the works council must consent to any appraisal system (art. 27(1)(g) WOR) and to rules on dismissal and promotion (27(1)(e)). Dismissal for poor performance goes to the cantonal court, which expects the employee to have been told in good time, given support and a real chance to improve (art. 7:669(3)(d) BW). A ranking or quota is not that evidence, and a plan with a fixed exit date looks like the opposite of a real chance.
- **Germany:** if Hamburg has a works council, it must agree general appraisal principles (section 94(2) BetrVG), and ratings made without it can't safely be used for personnel decisions. Where the Dismissal Protection Act applies, dismissal for performance usually needs a prior written warning and proof of sustained, clear underperformance.
- **Both:** a rating system that disadvantages part-time staff or people on leave can be indirect discrimination. A forced bottom group will skew that way, because those people have less visible output. In the Netherlands, someone who is ill is also largely protected against notice (art. 7:670 BW).

**What I'd put in front of the CEO instead**
1. Written expectations per role, shared before the cycle starts. Ratings measure the work against the role, not people against each other.
2. Calibration that aligns standards across managers and checks ratings by gender, age, part-time, leave, location and manager. It can move a rating when the evidence doesn't support it, never to fit a shape.
3. A manager who rates nobody low is asked for evidence, not told to find a name. That covers the case he is probably worried about, managers who tolerate poor work.
4. Before any plan, HR and the manager check whether the cause is workload, unclear expectations, tools or the manager. A plan starts from a specific gap with dated examples, runs up to 90 days with check-ins every two weeks and real support, and can end in closure, an extension, a move, or only then a separate exit step with HR and counsel. In the Netherlands that is a settlement or the cantonal court. In Germany it is a written warning first.
5. A quarterly team-level scorecard: regretted attrition, attrition of top-rated people, engagement and trust, plan outcomes, rating patterns by group and manager.

The draft contains the full step-by-step process, a manager briefing with a script and likely questions, an improvement plan template and a pre-mortem.

**Timeline.** The works councils have to consent before managers are briefed or anyone is rated, so allow several weeks. Bring them the problem and the numbers now, while the design can still change.

**The CEO's view, fairly put.** He wants a company where underperformance is not tolerated, and where managers act instead of avoiding hard conversations. That is a legitimate goal, and rating inflation and avoided conversations are real. A calibrated process with an evidence requirement and a visible plan-outcome scorecard goes at that directly. The quota is the part that costs you, not the ambition.

**How I'd have the conversation.** Start with him privately, before anything goes to the leadership team. Something like: "I want this to work at our scale and in both countries. Can you tell me what it did at your last company: what you measured, over what period, and who left that you were glad to lose? Here is a version that gets the same standard and that the works councils can approve."

**Questions that would change my advice**
- What did the CEO see at his last company, and in which countries? A US at-will employer's experience does not transfer to a Dutch and German workforce.
- What is the real problem: tolerated underperformance in a few teams, inconsistent standards between managers, or cost? Each has a different fix, and cost points elsewhere.
- Do you have a works council in each office, and what does the current appraisal arrangement say?
- Do you have rating and attrition data by manager from the last two years? It will show whether low performance clusters by team and manager, which it usually does.

**My assumptions:** the CEO will accept a better route to the same goal; Amsterdam has a works council (mandatory from 50 employees); the contracts are standard permanent employment. Please have Dutch and German employment counsel review the draft before it goes anywhere, since this is not legal advice.
