# Pay gap: what to do and what to tell the CFO

**Short answer:** don't take the "quiet, five years" plan. Treat the two numbers separately. The 14% overall gap is a structural issue you can close over several years, and you can explain it openly. The roughly 6% gap between men and women in the same role and grade is the one that matters legally and ethically. Fix that one fast, in one or two pay cycles, and tell people you're doing it.

## Why the two numbers are different

- **14% overall gap.** This mostly reflects who sits where: fewer women in senior, higher-paid or high-billing roles. It is not unlawful in itself. It is a talent pipeline and promotion problem, and it takes years. A multi-year plan is fine for this part.
- **~6% like-for-like gap.** Men and women doing the same work at the same grade are paid differently. Unless you can show objective, gender-neutral reasons, this is the kind of gap equal-pay law is aimed at. A five-year glide path means knowingly paying some women less for the same work for five more years.

## Why "say nothing, close it slowly" is risky

I'm not your lawyer, and the Dutch and Belgian transposition details need checking with employment counsel. Treat the points below as the framework to verify.

1. **The EU Pay Transparency Directive (2023/970)** had a transposition deadline of 7 June 2026. Check the current status of the Dutch and Belgian laws, but plan as if the rules apply to you. With 650 staff you are in the 150+ group, which has to report gender pay data annually. The first report is expected in 2027 and covers 2026 pay.
2. **The 5% trigger.** Under the directive, a gap of 5% or more in a category of workers doing equal work, that isn't justified by objective gender-neutral criteria and isn't corrected within six months, requires a joint pay assessment with worker representatives. Your 6% is above that line. The data you already have is close to what you will have to publish and act on.
3. **Employees will get the right to ask.** Staff can request pay-level information broken down by gender for comparable roles. Pay secrecy clauses and salary-history questions are restricted, and job ads need pay ranges. Keeping this internal won't hold for long.
4. **Your own records.** You now hold a documented analysis showing an unexplained gap. If it is later shown that you knew and chose a five-year timeline, that is a bad position in a claim, a labour inspectorate or equality body complaint, or a dispute with the works council. Equal-pay claims can generally reach back years, so delay increases back-pay exposure rather than limiting cost.
5. **Works council and staff rights.** In the Netherlands the OR (works council) has information and consultation rights on pay policy. In Belgium you likely have existing pay-structure reporting duties plus a works council or union delegation, depending on size. Check how these apply to your two entities.
6. **Practical risk.** In engineering consulting you compete for scarce people. A leak, a Glassdoor post or a client tender question ("what is your pay equity position?") would hurt more than the correction costs.

## What I would do (next 90 days, then 12 months)

**Now (weeks 0-4)**
- Have employment counsel set up the analysis under legal advice, and lock down who sees it for now.
- Stress-test the 6%. Check the regression and the controls: grade, role family, experience, location (NL vs BE separately), tenure, billable rate, part-time. Find out which part is explained by objective, defensible factors (for example a documented market premium for a scarce skill) and which part is not. A figure of 6% may become 3% or 7% once checked. Don't fix the number before you know it.
- Identify the individuals driving it. Gaps usually come from starting salaries (negotiation), uneven raises, and legacy acquisition pay, and these are fixable case by case.

**Next (months 1-4)**
- Cost the fix properly. Rough illustration only, with assumptions to replace with your real data: if about 160 of your 650 people are women, the average salary is about €60k and the unexplained gap is 6%, then raising women's pay to parity costs roughly €0.5-0.6m a year. That is around 1% of a ~€40m+ payroll. Your numbers may differ, but this is the order of magnitude, and it is probably smaller than the CFO fears.
- Brief the works council or employee representatives and agree how you communicate.
- Fix the process so it doesn't reopen: pay bands per grade, structured starting-salary rules, calibrated raise and promotion decisions with a gender check before sign-off.

**By the next pay cycle (within about 12 months, ideally before the first mandatory report)**
- Make individual corrections for the unexplained part. Do not lower anyone's pay to close the gap.
- Tell employees: what you found, what you are doing, and by when. Short, factual, no spin.
- Re-run the analysis after the correction and keep it as your baseline.

**Over 3-5 years (the 14%)**
- Targets for women in senior and high-paying roles, a review of how people are staffed on high-billing projects, promotion and sponsorship programmes, and the graduate and lateral hiring pipeline. Publish progress each year.

## What to tell the CFO

You are right that this has to be affordable, and I want to protect the budget as well. The point is where the money goes.

1. **Two separate problems.** "The 14% is a structural gap and we will close it over five years, fine. The 6% is unequal pay for equal work, and that is a liability, not a target."
2. **The silent plan is not actually the cheap one.** The extra cost of fixing it now is small next to the exposure. It runs to back pay across years, legal and consultancy fees, works council conflict, attrition of women we have trained and billed out, and loss of tenders, since clients increasingly ask about pay equity. Each year we wait adds to all of these.
3. **Regulation is already arriving.** The directive's reporting duty for companies our size starts in 2027, and the 5% rule means we would have to act and report on this gap anyway. If we start now we choose the pace and the message. If we wait, someone else sets both.
4. **The cost is bounded and phaseable.** "Once the analysis is validated, my estimate is a one-off adjustment of about [X]% of payroll. We can spread it over two pay cycles, tie it to the normal annual round, and target the individual cases rather than a blanket raise." Put the real number on this slide once you've run it.
5. **Offer options, not a confrontation.**
   - A: fix the unexplained gap in one cycle (cleanest, highest short-term cost).
   - B: fix it over two cycles with a hard end date (my recommendation if budget is tight).
   - C: the five-year plan. I would not recommend it, and I would want counsel to confirm we can defend it before we adopt it.
6. **Ask for a decision on process, not only money.** Agree that counsel reviews the analysis, that the findings are shared with the works council, and that we don't make "keep it internal" a commitment we can't keep.

If the CFO still wants silence, I would escalate to the CEO with counsel's written view, and I would make sure your own advice is documented. Don't sign off on a plan you have reason to think is not defensible.

## What I'd want to confirm

- The current status of the Dutch and Belgian implementing laws and how they apply to your two entities (counsel).
- The real cost, once the analysis is validated on your own data.
- Whether the 6% differs between NL and BE, since the rules and bodies involved differ.
