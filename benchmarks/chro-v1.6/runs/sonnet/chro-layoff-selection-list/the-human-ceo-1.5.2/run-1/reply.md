I can't finalise this list, and I'd advise against announcing it on the 15th. Two of the three criteria, sick days and part-time status, are ones you can't select on. The "managers' keep list" can't come first either. In both Rotterdam and Munich the law sets how people are chosen. A list built this way is unlikely to survive a works council, UWV, a German labour court or a single employee's lawyer, and it would hurt the 260 people who stay. This isn't legal advice, so please have employment counsel in each country confirm the points below.

**Why the current method fails**

- **Sick days.** Absence data is health data (GDPR special category). Selecting on it risks disability and chronic-illness discrimination. In the Netherlands, an employee who is ill is also generally protected from dismissal. In Germany, counting sick days is a legal minefield, and employers are expected to have offered reintegration support first.
- **Part-time.** Ranking people down for working part-time is unlawful unequal treatment on working hours in the Netherlands, and the same logic applies under German part-time law. It also falls hardest on women and carers.
- **Performance rating.** For a redundancy on business grounds, ratings are not the lawful order. Dutch law uses the reflection principle (afspiegelingsbeginsel). Within each group of interchangeable jobs at each location, people are sorted into five age bands, exits are spread across the bands so the age mix stays the same, and the shortest-serving in each band go first. German law uses a social selection (Sozialauswahl) that weighs length of service, age, dependants and severe disability. Neither uses performance rankings.
- **Keep lists.** Managers can protect a few people only where the law allows it. Germany allows keeping people with a "legitimate operational interest", and counsel must check that carefully. In the Netherlands, a unique job is the main exception. A blanket list of people to keep defeats the whole mechanism.

**What decides the date**

- **Works council.** In the Netherlands it has a right to advise on a reorganisation (article 25 WOR), and that advice must be requested before the decision is final. In Germany, if you have a works council, you must negotiate a reconciliation of interests and social plan (Interessenausgleich and Sozialplan) with it. If the board resolution is already being treated as final, we need to fix that sequence now. Has either council been told yet?
- **Collective thresholds.** Dutch notification to UWV and the unions applies at 20 or more dismissals in a work area within three months. German notification to the employment agency depends on establishment size, and mistakes can void the dismissals. Each site is counted separately. I don't have your split per site, so I can't say which thresholds you hit.
- **Timing.** Dutch dismissals for business reasons need a UWV permit or settlement agreements, and notice periods follow. Announcing individual names on the 15th would almost certainly be premature. At most, the 15th can be the day you inform the works councils and the workforce about the proposal and the reasons.

I'd like to see what was already tried before cutting 13% (40 of 300): spend and vendors, hiring freeze and attrition, voluntary exits, reduced hours, redeployment, and what leadership is giving up first. 2. Check whether the work is shrinking. Decide which work stops, so 260 people aren't left with the same workload.
3. Have HR prepare the lawful ranking per site. That means function groups, age bands and tenure for Rotterdam, and social-selection data for Munich, with a documented reason for any person exempted. Performance ratings, sick days and part-time status stay out of it. HR can also use the sheet it built as a check on whether the lawful outcome leaves a skills gap.
4. Open consultation with both works councils with the real numbers, while they can still change the plan. Call for voluntary exits and redeployment first, since volunteers reduce how many involuntary exits are needed.
5. Plan the package from day one of notice: paid notice, coaching and outplacement, a training budget, and extra care for people on leave, close to retirement or on a work visa. Each person hears it from their own manager, in person, with HR present.
6. Say plainly who is accountable. If results were missed, leadership owns that, and executive pay and bonuses should be cut before frontline jobs.

Telling people the order of events honestly costs you a few weeks. A list that gets overturned costs you months, reinstatement or damages, and the trust of the people who stay.
