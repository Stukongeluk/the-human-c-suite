## Before you send it

**1. Check the legal position first, in both countries.** This is the biggest risk, because about a third of your people were hired on remote or hybrid terms.
- In both the Netherlands and Germany, an employer cannot generally change an agreed term unilaterally just because it wants to. If a contract or offer letter names remote or hybrid work, or a location of work, a five-day mandate may need the employee's consent. In the Netherlands, a change to a contractual term without agreement can also be challenged (the "eenzijdig wijzigingsbeding" test is strict). In Germany, the employer's right to give instructions covers a lot, but it does not override what the contract says. Long-standing practice can also count as an implied term.
- Where this affects the person's own request or situation, there are further rules. For example, the Dutch Wet flexibel werken gives employees a right to request flexibility, and disability or care situations can bring equal-treatment duties.
- Ask an employment lawyer in each country to read a sample of the actual contracts for that one-third group. Do this before the date goes in an email. The wording "this replaces the current arrangements" in my draft is what a lawyer needs to approve or soften.

**2. Involve the works councils before you announce, not after.** A change to working arrangements at this scale very likely needs the works council's consent or at least formal consultation (Dutch WOR, German BetrVG). Announcing a finished decision first can give the council grounds to challenge the change and can cause a legal and trust problem on top of the policy one. If the councils have not been formally approached, change the "What happens next" section and the date accordingly.

**3. Tell the CEO what the likely cost is.** I wrote the email you asked for, but you should know what leadership is signing up for, and it is better coming from you than from the exit interviews.
- Fintech engineering and compliance talent is in demand in both cities. A mandate that breaks agreed terms for a third of the company will likely drive out some of your strongest people first, because they have the most options. Departure also tends to be concentrated among the people who were told in writing they could work remotely.
- Only "culture" is given as the reason. It will land better, and be easier to defend legally, if the CEO can name the specific problem it is meant to fix (onboarding, decision speed, collaboration between teams) and the measure that will show it has worked.
- Consider whether a lighter alternative would solve the stated problem: for example, three or four fixed anchor days for everyone, with the remote and hybrid group treated differently only where their contract requires it. I can draft a one-page comparison for the CEO if useful.

If the decision stands as it is, I'd also suggest a short separate email for managers, with answers to the ten questions they will be asked on day one. I can write that next.
