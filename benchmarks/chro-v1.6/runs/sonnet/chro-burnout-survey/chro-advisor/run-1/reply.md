**Bottom line:** Don't tell the board "stable with some pockets of concern," and don't make resilience training the whole answer. Present the survey as it is, with a fix that targets the causes. Your CEO gets a credible plan, and you aren't left holding a framing the data doesn't support.

**Why the CEO's framing won't hold up** 🟢
- Trust in senior leadership at 41% is a company-wide result, not a pocket. "Stable" doesn't fit a metric that fell.
- "Burnout risk up sharply" in two departments is a warning sign. Claims and Customer Service are core operations, and burnout there shows up soon as sick leave, errors, slower claims handling and complaints.
- The board will probably see these numbers elsewhere: in the works council (OR), in absence data, or in the next survey. If you called it stable and it isn't, your credibility takes the hit, not only the CEO's.
- The risk is also legal. Under the Arbowet an employer has to manage psychosocial workload (PSA) at its source. 🟡 This is from my general knowledge, so check it with your arbo-advisor or a jurist.

**Why resilience training alone is the wrong fix** 🟡
It treats burnout as a personal weakness. The usual causes are workload, staffing, targets, and managers. If those don't change, training reads as "the problem is you," which will lower trust further, and trust is already your weakest number. Training can be one part of the plan. It shouldn't be the plan.

**What to do before and at the board meeting**
1. **Find the causes this week.** Break the data down by team and manager. Add what you already hold: absence and long-term sick leave, overtime, vacancies, regretted exits, and exit-interview themes. Run short listening sessions in Claims and Customer Service. If exits or complaints cluster under particular managers, that's the real finding. 🔴 I don't have your data, so I'm assuming workload and management are the drivers until it shows otherwise.
2. **Tell the board plainly:** burnout risk is up sharply in two operational teams, and trust in leadership fell to 41%. Say what you know, what you don't know yet, and when you'll know.
3. **Bring a plan with owners and dates:**
   - Within 30 days: diagnosis finished, and results fed back to staff, as people expect action after a survey.
   - Workload relief where the data supports it, such as backfills, caseload caps, or removing low-value tasks.
   - Manager support for Claims and Customer Service leads, since manager quality is the biggest controllable factor in team engagement.
   - A visible commitment from the CEO and senior team: listening sessions, and a few specific changes announced and then delivered. That is what moves trust.
   - Optional: voluntary support such as coaching or an employee assistance programme, alongside the changes above and not in place of them.
4. **Pick metrics the board can track:** a short pulse survey on burnout and trust each quarter, absence rate, regretted attrition, and complaint and claims-handling times in the two departments.
5. **Involve the OR early.** With 450 staff you have a works council, and it has rights on health and safety and on survey-related measures. 🟡 Confirm the exact consent rights with your jurist, and bring them in before the board hears about it.

**How to handle the CEO**
Talk to them before the board meeting, not in it. Something like: "If we call it stable and the OR or the next pulse says otherwise, we lose credibility on the whole people agenda. I can present this as a clear signal with a plan already moving, which makes you look in control." Offer to include the training as one element so they have something to approve. If they still insist on "stable," put your concern in writing and make sure the raw numbers are in the board pack.

**Your decision:** Do you take the straight framing to the CEO tomorrow and ask for their backing, or do you first spend a few days on step 1 so you arrive with causes and not only symptoms? I'd do both: a short conversation now, and the first cut of the diagnosis before the meeting.
