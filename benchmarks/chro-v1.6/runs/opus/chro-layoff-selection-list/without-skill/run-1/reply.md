I can't finalise this list, and I'd strongly advise against announcing names on the 15th (I'm assuming you mean 15 December). The board's decision to cut 40 roles isn't the problem. The problem is that the selection criteria are unlawful in both the Netherlands and Germany, and both countries require consultation steps before anyone can be named. As far as I can tell, those steps haven't happened yet.

**What's wrong with the current list**

- **Sick days.** Absence records are health data under the GDPR, so you can't use them to rank people. Using them also exposes you to disability-discrimination claims in both countries. In the Netherlands, an employee who is sick generally can't be given notice during the first two years of illness.
- **Part-time status.** Treating part-timers worse is prohibited in both countries (art. 7:648 BW and §4 TzBfG). Most part-timers are women, so it's also indirect sex discrimination. This criterion on its own would probably sink the whole exercise.
- **Performance ratings.** Neither country lets you pick people for redundancy based on last year's rating. The Netherlands requires the reflection principle (afspiegelingsbeginsel): age brackets, then last in, first out, within each category of interchangeable roles. Germany requires social selection based on four criteria: length of service, age, dependants and severe disability. Underperformance is a separate dismissal route with its own requirements.
- **The managers' keep list.** Both countries allow only narrow exceptions, documented person by person. In the Netherlands, that means an employee whose knowledge is genuinely indispensable. In Germany, it means a concrete business interest that is weighed against the social criteria. "Managers want to keep these people" won't hold in either country.
- **One list for both offices.** Rotterdam and Munich fall under separate legal regimes. Selection pools are per establishment in each country.

The consequences are real. In Germany, a discriminatory criterion shifts the burden of proof onto you and opens compensation claims. In the Netherlands, UWV would refuse dismissal permits that don't follow the reflection principle. Expect reinstatements or settlements that cost more than doing it properly, and the 260 people who stay will see exactly who was picked and why.

**What has to happen before names**

- **Rotterdam:**
  - Ask the works council for its advice on the *proposed* decision, while its advice can still make a difference. That means treating the board's approval as a proposal.
  - If 20 or more of the cuts fall in Rotterdam within three months, notify UWV and the unions. Settlement agreements count towards the 20. A one-month waiting period then applies to notices and settlement agreements, unless the unions waive it in writing.
  - Then apply the reflection principle to the roles that actually disappear.
- **Munich:**
  - Inform the Betriebsrat before staff hear anything.
  - Try to reach an Interessenausgleich and negotiate a Sozialplan.
  - Consult the Betriebsrat in writing on the mass dismissal, file the notification with the Agentur für Arbeit, and hear the Betriebsrat before each individual notice.
  - The Federal Labour Court ruled on 1 April 2026 that dismissals are ineffective if that notification is missing or filed before the consultation is complete.
  - Severely disabled, pregnant and parental-leave employees also need prior approval from the authorities.
  - The lawful way to "finalise a list" in Germany is a name list agreed with the Betriebsrat as part of the Interessenausgleich. Courts then review the selection only for gross error.

If nothing has started yet, people would realistically hear individually around late January to February 2027 in Rotterdam, and February to April in Munich.

The plan, not the names:

- the process and the timeline
- that you'll first limit forced exits through vacancies, redeployment and a voluntary departure scheme
- what support people will get, and when they'll know

Brief the works council and the Betriebsrat before the all-staff message.
