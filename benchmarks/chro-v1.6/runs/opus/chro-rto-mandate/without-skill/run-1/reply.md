Here's the email. I made two choices you should know about before you take it to the CEO:

1. **It's written as a proposal that goes to the works councils, not as a done deal.** In the Netherlands, changing or withdrawing a home-working policy needs the Ondernemingsraad's consent. Last year a Dutch court blocked Caterpillar's move to full-time office working for exactly that reason. In Germany, the Betriebsrat has comparable rights. If the email announces five days from 1 March as already decided, it hands the councils the grounds to block the decision. So the councils get the formal request first, and staff get this email the same day or right after.
2. **It comes from the CEO, not from you.** It's the CEO's decision and the CEO's argument about culture. Sent by HR, it reads as an HR policy, and you need to stay the person people can come to afterwards.

Anything in [square brackets] is a placeholder or a promise. Don't send a promise unless the CEO has said yes to it and there's budget behind it.

---

**Subject:** How we work from 1 March: a proposal, and what happens next

Hi everyone,

I want to tell you about a big change I'm proposing to how we work. I'll cover why I think we need it, what it means for you, and what happens before anything is final.

**The proposal**
From 1 March 2027, I want all of us to work from our Utrecht and Berlin offices five days a week.

**Why**
We've grown to around 800 people in four years, and most of that growth happened while we were working remotely or hybrid. A lot of that has worked: [one honest, specific thing that went well]. But some things worry me, and I believe they come from how little time we spend together:

- [Specific example with a number, e.g. "New joiners now take around X months to feel fully up to speed, against Y before 2022."]
- [Specific example, e.g. "Decisions between product, risk and engineering that used to take one conversation now take weeks of threads and calls."]
- [Specific example, e.g. "In our last engagement survey, 'I know who to go to outside my team' fell from X% to Y%."]

We've tried encouraging more office time [since …]. Today, about [X]% of us are in the office on a typical day. I don't think encouragement on its own will get us where we need to be.

This is not a judgement on how hard anyone works. Many of you have done some of your best work from home, and I know that.

**If you joined us as a remote or hybrid hire**
About a third of you joined us on a remote or hybrid basis, and some of you have built your lives around that: where you live, school runs, caring for family. This proposal goes against what we agreed when you joined, and I won't pretend otherwise.

So 1 March does not automatically apply to you. If remote or hybrid working is written into your contract, this email does not change your contract. By [date], [HR] will set up a one-to-one with each of you to talk through your situation and the options [for example a longer transition period, help with travel or relocation, or keeping your current arrangement where that genuinely works for both sides]. [If this change means [Company] is no longer the right place for you, we will make sure you leave on fair terms and with our respect.]

**If your circumstances make this hard**
If a health condition, a disability, caring responsibilities or anything else makes full-time office work difficult, please talk to [HR contact] or your manager. There will be a clear process for adjustments and exceptions, and these conversations stay confidential.

**What happens next, and what isn't decided yet**
In the Netherlands, a change like this needs the consent of our Ondernemingsraad. In Germany, our Betriebsrat has co-determination rights over how it's done. We have formally shared this proposal with both [today / on date]. Their questions may change the details, including the timing and the transition, and I want to hear them. We'll confirm the final arrangement by [date], at least [six] weeks before anything takes effect.

In the meantime:

- **[Date]: Q&A with me and [HR director]** in Utrecht, in Berlin and online. Ask anything. You can send questions in advance, anonymously if you prefer, at [link].
- **[Date]: FAQ** on [intranet], updated as your questions come in.
- **Over the next two weeks:** your manager will talk it through with your team.
- **The offices:** if we ask you to come in every day, the offices have to be worth it. We're working on [list only what's approved: a desk for everyone, quiet space for focused work, enough meeting rooms, help with travel costs, flexible start and finish times].

I know some of you will be disappointed by this, and some of you will be angry. I'd rather hear that from you directly than second-hand, so please come to the Q&A, talk to your manager or write to me.

[CEO name]
CEO, [Company]

### Before you send it

1. **Councils first, then the email.** Submit the consent request to the OR (art. 27 WOR) and inform the Betriebsrat before this goes out, or on the same day.
   - **Netherlands:** in the [Caterpillar case](https://www.rechtspraak.nl/organisatie-en-contact/organisatie/rechtbanken/rechtbank-oost-brabant/nieuws/caterpillar-had-or-niet-mogen-passeren-bij-intrekken-van-thuiswerkbeleid) (Rechtbank Oost-Brabant, 15 September 2025), the court treated ending home working as a working-conditions matter. It barred Caterpillar from going ahead until the OR consented or a court gave substitute consent. If your OR says no, getting substitute consent from the kantonrechter takes months. So 1 March only holds if the OR agrees.
   - **Germany:** the LAG München made Allianz Re [withdraw an office-attendance order](https://www.haufe.de/personal/arbeitsrecht/zurueck-ins-buero-nicht-ohne-den-betriebsrat_76_609664.html) that it had issued without the Betriebsrat (2023). The BAG has [treated ending telework as a transfer](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/de-de/wissen/publications/87798b15/termination-of-agreed-work-from-home) (Versetzung) that needs Betriebsrat consent under §99 BetrVG. If you have a Betriebsvereinbarung on mobile working, check how it can be ended. The statutory default is three months' notice (§77(5) BetrVG), which for 1 March means giving notice before December starts. If Berlin has no Betriebsrat, delete that sentence from the email. The contract point below then matters even more.
   - **Attendance tracking:** if you plan to use badge data to check attendance, that needs separate consent in both countries (art. 27(1)(l) WOR; §87(1) no. 6 BetrVG).

2. **Find out what the remote-hired third actually has in writing.** Pull contracts, offer letters and recruiter emails for everyone hired as remote or hybrid.
   - **Remote or hybrid written into the contract:** in the Netherlands you will essentially need each person's agreement. In Germany, the employer's right to direct work doesn't reach it, so you're left with agreement or an Änderungskündigung, and the hurdles for that are high.
   - **Hybrid that comes only from policy:** the council route is the main one.
   - Agree the options (transition length, travel and relocation budget, exit terms) before the email goes out, because the email promises conversations about them.

3. **Count the desks.** If either office was downsized for hybrid working, five days fails on the first Monday. In the [Booking.com ruling](https://www.rechtspraak.nl/organisatie-en-contact/organisatie/rechtbanken/rechtbank-amsterdam/nieuws/2026/10/rechtbank-stelt-bookingcom-in-het-gelijk-over-thuiswerkbeleid), the court noted that Booking must keep making sure everyone required to come in has a desk.

4. **Fill the "why" with real numbers, and treat that as a test.** I left out "to fix our culture". Staff will read it as "you broke it", and it gives nobody anything concrete. The OR, the Betriebsrat and, if it comes to it, a judge will ask what your staff will ask: which problem, what evidence, and why five days rather than fewer. If you can't fill the three placeholders, raise that with the CEO before this goes anywhere.

5. **Prepare managers for formal requests.** Expect people to use the Wet flexibel werken to formally request to keep working from home. Each request has to be considered and answered in writing. Managers shouldn't improvise answers in their team meetings.

### What I'd put to the CEO

It's the CEO's call. But you're the one who'll be asked later why so many of the remote hires left, so I'd raise three things:

- **The people who leave won't be a random sample.** A [study of 54 large US tech and financial firms](https://hankamer.baylor.edu/news/story/2025/return-office-mandates-and-hidden-cost-brain-drain) found turnover rose 13–14% on average after RTO mandates. Women, senior people and highly skilled employees were the most likely to leave, and vacancies took 23% longer to fill. A [randomised trial at Trip.com](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2024/06/hybrid-work-is-a-win-win-win-for-companies-workers) (Nature, 2024) found that two days a week at home cut resignations by a third, with no effect on performance or promotions. Both studies are from outside Europe, but the direction is clear. Your remote-hired third are the most exposed, and the people with the most options are the ones you least want to lose.
- **Five days is the hardest version to defend.** The Booking.com judge approved 40% office presence. Booking had come down from 50–60%, offered six months' transition and a hardship clause, and showed that only 27% of staff were meeting the voluntary guideline. Five days from 1 March, on about three months' notice, for people who were hired as remote, is close to the opposite case.
- **Five days doesn't fix the Utrecht–Berlin gap.** If part of the culture problem sits between the two offices, that collaboration stays remote whatever the policy says.

The alternative I'd propose: name the two or three real culture problems, then set the minimum office presence that fixes them. That could be three or four fixed team days, full-time presence for new joiners' first [three] months, and a longer runway for remote hires. You'd probably get most of the culture benefit, a much better chance of OR consent and far fewer resignations. If the CEO still wants five days, the email above is the honest way to say it.

Before anything goes out, have employment lawyers in both countries review the email and the council requests. What I've given you is the outline, not legal advice.
