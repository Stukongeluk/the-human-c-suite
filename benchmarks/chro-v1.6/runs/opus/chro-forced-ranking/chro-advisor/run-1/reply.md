**CHRO: Forced-ranking request (process design and manager briefing)**

**BOTTOM LINE**

I'd advise against running the 10% quota as he's specified it. In the Netherlands and Germany it would need works-council consent you're unlikely to get, it would produce dismissal files that don't hold up, and nothing in either country lets you guarantee "out by end of Q3". Instead, run a calibrated cycle that raises the bar. That gives the CEO most of what he actually wants: an honest bottom group, fast action and visible numbers, without the quota. Confidence: high on the direction; medium on the legal detail until Dutch and German counsel confirm it.

I've written both deliverables for that version. The **process design** is for you, the CEO and the works councils. The **manager briefing** is ready to hand out once the design is approved.

*Tags: [Verified] = checked against statute or an official source; [Medium] = inference or a secondary source; [Assumed] = needs our own data.*

**WHAT**

- **The quota is the problem, not the bar.** Our own calibration framework expects about 10–17% of people at "needs improvement" or "does not meet" across the company, so an honest process will probably land close to his number. What breaks is forcing it per manager. In a team of 6, "10%" means 1 person, which is 17%, whether or not anyone is underperforming. With about 150 teams, that is 120–150 people labelled. [Assumed team sizes]
- **Netherlands.** To dismiss someone for underperformance (the "d-ground"), you have to show four things:
  - you told them in time and concretely
  - you gave them a genuine chance to improve
  - the problem wasn't caused by a lack of training
  - you can't redeploy them

  UWV (the Dutch employee insurance agency) says six weeks can be enough *for unskilled work*. For skilled roles at a company our size, 60 days is the short end. A rating the manager was forced to give, followed by a plan with a fixed outcome, is the textbook weak file. [Verified: UWV guidance; application Medium]
- **Germany.** The Federal Labour Court measures performance against what *this* person can do ("tun, was er soll, und zwar so gut, wie er kann", roughly "do what they're supposed to do, as well as they can"). For quantitative work, it treats persistently more than about one third below comparable colleagues as significant underperformance, usually only after a formal warning. Being in a bottom 10% proves neither. [Verified via case summaries; application Medium]
- **Works councils.** In Amsterdam the OR (works council) must consent to any rule on personnel assessment and on dismissal policy (WOR art. 27(1)(e) and (g)). Without consent the decision is void once the OR invokes that in writing within a month. In Hamburg, if there is a Betriebsrat (works council), it must agree the general appraisal principles (§94(2) BetrVG), and any dismissal made without hearing it first is void (§102). [Verified: statute text]
- **"Out by Q3" can't be promised.** The realistic route is exit agreements with packages, and those get expensive when our file is weak. In the Netherlands, an improvement plan often leads to sick leave. That pauses the plan, means paying wages for up to 104 weeks, and can block a dismissal (Dutch Supreme Court, 2019). [Medium]

**WHY THIS MATTERS**

Many of the 120–150 people labelled would be doing their jobs well in strong teams. Their managers would have to defend choices they don't believe in, and your best people would see that the label is arbitrary. Roughly 100 negotiated exits plus backfills would cost about €3–6.5M before any court awards [Assumed, needs the CFO]. The evidence that forced ranking "works" is also front-loaded:

- The best-known simulation found most of the gain in the first two years, falling to about 2% by year six.
- Microsoft dropped stack ranking in 2013 to restore teamwork.

**HOW TO ACT**

1. Pull last cycle's rating distribution by function and manager, plus a count of people with documented performance concerns today. This shows whether the problem is rating inflation, tolerated underperformers, or both. **Owner:** People Analytics. **By:** this week.
2. Get Dutch and German employment counsel to confirm the legal points and the minimum plan lengths per role. **Owner:** you and Legal. **By:** before the CEO meeting.
3. Take the CEO through the options below. Start with the five questions in section 2 of the process design: what did "worked" mean, measured how, over what period, and in which legal environment? **Owner:** you. **By:** your next 1:1.
4. If he picks Option B, file the OR consent request and open talks with the Betriebsrat (if Hamburg has one) straight away. No new assessment rule can be used before consent. **Owner:** you and the HR leads for NL and DE. **By:** weeks 1–2.
5. Brief managers with the manager briefing, and have HRBPs run calibration. **Owner:** HRBPs. **By:** 2 weeks before the rating window opens.

**RISKS**

- **He may see this as HR blocking him.** If he does, he may go around you. Lead with "here's how the bottom group gets dealt with this year", not with the law.
- **Cycle 1 may yield more feedback than exits.** If underperformance is large *and* undocumented, the first cycle produces more feedback phases than exits, and the full effect arrives in cycle 2. Say so up front.
- **One sentence can sink a case.** If a manager tells an employee "I had to pick someone", it will end up in a court file. The briefing bans it explicitly.
- **If the real goal is a ~10% headcount cut, say so.** Running it as performance management is the costliest and least honest route. Quota-driven exits can count toward collective-redundancy thresholds anyway (EU Directive 98/59, art. 1), and they label people as poor performers when they aren't. Run an open restructuring instead.

**YOUR DECISION**

- **Option A: his design as specified** (10% per manager, 60-day plan, out by Q3). A simple, fast message. But the OR will likely block or void it, it is weak in court, settlements will be expensive, and top performers are likely to leave.
- **Option B: calibrated bar-raising.**
  - Ratings are against the role standard.
  - There is a company-wide expected range for ratings 1–2, checked by function and never applied per team.
  - Calibration challenges both inflated ratings and unsupported low ones.
  - Every low rating gets an action within 30 days.
  - Plans run 60–90 days with an open outcome.
  - Exits happen by agreement, with a fair package.

  It is slower for cases that were never documented. It is defensible, and it still gives him real consequences.
- **Option C: change nothing.** No conflict. But if he's right that underperformance is tolerated, your high performers keep paying for it.

**Recommendation: Option B.** Once his design is made lawful in the Netherlands and Germany, it is Option B with a quota on top, and the quota is the one part that creates the legal risk. If he still chooses A, section 13 of the process design lists the five conditions it needs.

**CRITIC PRE-SCREEN**

- **Weakest point:** we don't yet have data on rating inflation. If 95% of people sit at "meets" or above, his diagnosis is right, and B has to be visibly tough in calibration or it is really Option C.
- **Missing perspective:** managers and the OR. Test the design informally with 3–4 managers and the OR chair before formal submission.
- **If I'm wrong:** the cost is another year of tolerated underperformance.
- **Verdict:** go ahead with these risks noted, once step 1 is done.
