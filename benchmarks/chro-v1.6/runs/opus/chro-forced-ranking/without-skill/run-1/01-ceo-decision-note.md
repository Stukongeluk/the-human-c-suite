# Performance reviews 2026/27: raising the bar in a way that holds up

**To:** CEO
**From:** Chief People Officer
**Decision needed this week:** how we differentiate performance and deal with underperformance in this cycle, so we can open works council talks in December.

---

## What we agree on

- Our ratings are probably too generous, and real underperformance isn't being dealt with fast enough. (I'll bring last year's rating distribution by site and manager so we can see how big the problem is.)
- You want a higher bar, honest differentiation, and visible results by Q3.

I'm committed to all three. My concern is with the method: a fixed 10% per manager, a 60-day plan, and exit by end of Q3. In the Netherlands and Germany, that method gets us less of what you want, more slowly, at a much higher cost.

## Why the fixed 10% / 60 days / exit-by-Q3 version won't get us there

| Step | Netherlands (Amsterdam) | Germany (Hamburg) |
|---|---|---|
| **Introducing the system** | A new or changed appraisal system and dismissal policy need the works council's (OR) consent. Without it, the OR can declare the decision void within a month. | If we have a Betriebsrat, appraisal principles need its consent. If we can't agree, a conciliation board (Einigungsstelle) decides. |
| **Using the 10% label as grounds for exit** | A dismissal for underperformance needs a court to agree that the person is actually unfit for the role, was told in time, had a real chance to improve and can't be redeployed. "The manager had to pick 10%" is the opposite of that evidence. A dossier built to fill a quota also risks extra compensation on top of the statutory payment. | The courts' bar for a "low performer" is roughly one-third below comparable colleagues over a longer period, plus a formal warning. Ratings that aren't measured against the same yardstick for everyone can be challenged and removed. |
| **The 60-day plan** | The court decides what counts as a fair chance. For knowledge roles and longer-serving staff, 60 days is often judged too short. | There's no fixed period. The person needs a warning and a real chance to improve. |
| **Exit by end of Q3** | Anyone who reports sick, which is common under this kind of pressure, generally can't be dismissed while they're ill. Contested cases go through the court. | Notice periods run up to 7 months. Where there's a Betriebsrat, every dismissal needs its prior hearing, or it's void. Challenges are common, and if we lose we reinstate with back pay. Clustered exits can also trigger mass-dismissal notification. |

Two more things to weigh:

- **The gains fade fast.** The best-known simulation of rank-and-yank found improvements in workforce potential of about 16% a year in the first two years, falling to about 2% by year six. After the first round we'd be cutting solid people. It may well have worked at your last company in its early years, or under at-will employment. Neither applies here.
- **It works against how we build software.** Fixed quotas push people to compete with colleagues instead of helping them, and they guarantee that someone in a strong team of six gets labelled "bottom". Microsoft dropped stack ranking in 2013 (the memo said "no more curve" and "no more rankings") and moved to reviews focused on "teamwork that benefits the company".

## Options

| | What it is | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **A. As proposed** | Every manager puts 10% in the bottom category. 60-day plan. Exit by end of Q3. | **Not recommended.** It's likely void when introduced without works council consent, and weak in court. Most exits would only happen through paid settlements. Rough cost is €2–6M for ~60 exits before regretted attrition (illustrative, see process design §11). It would also cost us the works councils' trust for the next hard decision. |
| **B. Calibrated reviews, no quota** *(recommended)* | A clear, evidenced "does not meet" standard. Calibration panels challenge managers in both directions, including those who rate nobody low. A real improvement plan (90 days by default). Exits decided case by case. | Delivers the higher bar, with decisions by July and agreed exits in Q3. The number of exits comes out of the process rather than being set in advance. |
| **C. B plus a reference range** | B, plus an agreed department-level range (for example 5–10% below expectations in departments of 50+ people). It's used only to challenge calibration, never to set an individual rating or exit. | Workable if we present it honestly and the works councils agree. This is the right compromise if you want a number on the table. |

## What you get by Q3 with B or C

- Every employee has had an honest rating conversation, calibrated across Amsterdam and Hamburg.
- Every confirmed underperformer has had a fair plan, with outcome decisions made in July.
- Agreed exits are completed in Q3. Contested cases are in progress with files that hold up.
- We can show the bar went up: rating distribution, plan outcomes, quality of replacements, and no rise in regretted attrition.
- Managers can start individual plans **now** for underperformance that's already documented, under our current policy. We don't have to wait for the new cycle.

## If we keep a distribution (option C), these are non-negotiable

1. Works council consent before anything goes to managers.
2. The reference range applies at department level (50+ people), never per team.
3. A rating never triggers an exit on its own. The plan outcome and individual evidence do.
4. Every exit is reviewed by the HR business partner and employment counsel.
5. Ratings and exits are checked for bias (gender, age, part-time, recent leave) before they're final.

## What I'm asking for

1. Agreement on B or C this week, so we can approach both works councils in December.
2. 45 minutes with our Dutch and German employment counsel together, so you can test this with them directly.
3. A conversation about what "worked" meant at your last company: what was measured, over how many years, and under which employment law. Then we keep the parts that transfer.
