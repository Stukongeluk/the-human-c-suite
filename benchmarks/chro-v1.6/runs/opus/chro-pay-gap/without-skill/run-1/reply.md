**Short answer:** Don't accept the CFO's plan as it stands, but don't reject all of it either. Your two numbers are two different problems, and they run on different timelines.

- **The 6% gap within the same roles and grades is a legal problem.** Close it within about 12 months (one or two pay rounds), not five years.
- **The 14% overall gap is mostly a structural problem.** It comes from where women sit in the organisation. A plan over several years is right for that, and that is where the CFO's five-year horizon fits.
- **"Say nothing" isn't really an option you have.** You can choose how and when you communicate, but not whether this becomes visible.

## Why five years doesn't work for the 6%

**1. Equal pay for equal work isn't new law.** Employees have been able to enforce it against private employers since 1976 (Art. 157 TFEU, *Defrenne*). Dutch and Belgian equal-treatment law say the same. The new EU directive makes gaps visible and easier to enforce; it doesn't change the rule. Once you've checked the 6% (see below), what's left is an unlawful pay difference that you now know about. Every month of a slow phase-in adds to what women can claim back. Dutch wage claims go back five years. Under Belgium's Gender Act, someone who doesn't want to prove their exact loss can claim a lump sum of six months' pay instead ([DLA Piper](https://knowledge.dlapiper.com/dlapiperknowledge/globalemploymentlatestdevelopments/belgium-discrimination-laws-updated-with-new-compensation-rules)). In the Netherlands, an employee can also go to the College voor de Rechten van de Mens (the human rights institute) free of charge.

**2. The clock is already running.**
- **Netherlands:** The bill went to parliament in May 2026 aiming for 1 January 2027 ([Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/ja/our-thinking/blogs/risk-and-compliance/dutch-pay-transparency-legislative-proposal-submitted-to-parliament-102mxc2)). Last week the minister confirmed it won't make that date. The Tweede Kamer debates it on 14 December, and 1 July 2027 is now expected but not confirmed ([Salaris Vanmorgen, 20 Nov 2026](https://www.salarisvanmorgen.nl/2026/11/20/wet-loontransparantie-niet-per-1-januari-2027-in-werking/)). Employers your size are expected to file their first report in 2028, covering 2027 pay. So your 2027 pay is most likely what the first report will show.
- **Belgium:** The rules for private employers still haven't been written into Belgian law, and as of September there was no federal draft ([Jackson Lewis](https://www.jacksonlewis.com/insights/eu-pay-transparency-transposition-belgium-implements-public-sector-portion-private-sector-still-come)). But the 2012 pay gap law already requires every company with 50+ employees to produce a detailed analysis of its pay structure every two years and discuss it with the works council ([FOD WASO](https://werk.belgie.be/nl/themas/gelijkheid-en-non-discriminatie/gelijkheid-vrouwen-mannen-de-loonkloof?id=8486)). If this really is your *first* pay analysis, check whether the Belgian entity has been meeting that obligation. If it hasn't, fix that now whatever else you decide.
- **Once the rules apply** ([summary](https://www.addleshawgoddard.com/link/c14ed8b40a97433aa82253da343dfd0b.aspx)):
  - Any employee can ask for average pay by sex for their category of work, and you must answer within two months.
  - Suppose a category shows a gap of 5% or more that gender-neutral factors don't explain. If you haven't fixed it within six months of reporting, you must run a joint pay assessment with your employee representatives.
  - Compensation covers full back pay, with no cap, and the burden of proof shifts to the employer.

A five-year phase-in almost guarantees the following: your first report shows gaps in several categories, the six-month window runs out, and you end up doing a joint pay assessment with your works councils in the open. That is exactly what the CFO wants to avoid.

**3. You now know.** "We found it and fixed it" is a strong position. "We found it and chose to wait five years to protect the budget" is the weakest position you could take, whether in front of a court, the human rights institute, a works council or a journalist. That choice would also be written down in emails and minutes.

## What I'd do

**Next 6–8 weeks: make the numbers solid**
1. Bring in employment lawyers in both countries now.
2. Run the analysis per legal entity. The Dutch bill puts reporting with the entity that holds the employment contract ([Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/02/12/netherlands-amended-draft-bill-implementing-the-pay-transparency-directive-before-council-of-state)). If your Dutch and Belgian businesses are separate entities, the Dutch one (420 people) reports every year. The Belgian one (230) falls in the 150–249 band, which reports less often.
3. Redo the analysis the way a regulator will:
   - Use hourly or full-time-equivalent pay. This matters with Dutch part-time levels.
   - Count every part of pay: bonus, allowances, pension and company car. In Belgium, gaps often hide in cars and variable pay.
   - Group people by "work of equal value", using a gender-neutral job evaluation of skills, effort, responsibility and working conditions, not just job titles.
4. Look at each category of work, not only the 6% average. The 5% test applies per category, and a 6% average usually means some categories near zero and some above 10%.
5. Go through it person by person. For each person, work out where they should be given their role, grade and legitimate, gender-neutral factors. Be strict about what counts as an explanation. Seniority and performance ratings can carry bias. "She negotiated a lower starting salary" doesn't count, and the directive bans asking candidates about previous pay.
6. The result should be a named list of adjustments with an exact cost.

**Next pay round: close the unexplained gaps between people in the same roles**
7. Raise the underpaid; don't cut anyone. You can't unilaterally cut contractual pay in either country, and trying would wreck trust.
8. Ideally do it all at once, effective 1 January 2027, even if the money (with arrears) is paid out in March or April. That keeps 2027, the likely first reporting year, clean. If the budget really can't absorb that, use two tranches and finish by mid-2027.
9. Decide with your lawyers whether to include back pay. Fixing pay from now on is the minimum. A limited back-pay amount is often cheaper than defending claims, and it is the clearest proof of good faith.
10. Tell people honestly why their pay is changing. If you present an equal-pay correction as an ordinary raise, it will backfire once the reports come out.

**At the same time: stop the gap reopening**
11. Fix the processes that cause gaps:
    - starting salaries: set ranges per role and don't use salary history
    - raise and bonus decisions: check them for gender bias
    - promotions
    - how part-time work and parental leave affect progression

    In the Netherlands, changes to a pay or job-evaluation system need the OR's consent (Art. 27 WOR), which is another reason to involve them early.
12. Set up a process for employee pay-information requests, with an owner and a two-month deadline.

**Over 3–5 years: the 14% overall gap**
13. Set targets and track:
    - women in senior grades
    - equal promotion rates
    - diverse shortlists
    - retention of women engineers

    Report on these to the board every year.

**Communication**
14. Go to the works councils in both countries first. The law will involve them anyway, and if you consult them early they're far more likely to back the plan than fight it.
15. Then tell all employees: we did our first pay analysis, this is what we found, and this is what we're fixing and by when. You don't have to publish percentages externally before the law requires it. But decide now what you want the 2028 report to say: "found in 2026, fixed in 2027".

## Rough cost (replace with your real numbers)

Assume about 25% of your 650 people are women (around 160) on an average of €60k. Closing a 6% gap would then cost about €575k a year, or €700–750k including employer costs. That's roughly 1.4% of payroll. The real figure is usually lower, because only people who are actually below where they should be get an adjustment, and part of the 6% may turn out to have a legitimate explanation.

| Option | Cost 2027–2031 | Underpayment women keep bearing (and can claim back) |
|---|---|---|
| Fix in full, 1 Jan 2027 | ~€3.6M | €0 |
| Two tranches (Jan + Jul 2027) | ~€3.4M | ~€0.2M |
| Five-year phase-in (+20% a year) | ~€2.2M | ~€1.4M |

The five-year plan's "saving" is exactly the underpayment women would keep bearing, which is the amount they could later claim back with interest. And that's before legal costs, a joint pay assessment, or women engineers leaving once pay ranges become visible.

## What to tell the CFO

Start with what the CFO is right about: budget predictability matters, and the structural gap really will take years. Then:

1. "We aren't choosing between paying and not paying. We're choosing between a planned cost of about 1.4% of payroll now and a bigger, unplanned bill later: back pay, interest, legal costs and a joint pay assessment, after it's public."
2. "The 6% isn't an optional investment. Equal pay for equal work has been the law for decades. Now that we've measured the gap, every month we knowingly leave it adds to our exposure."
3. "Keeping quiet isn't really an option. Belgian works councils are already entitled to this analysis. Every employee will soon be able to ask for the figures and must get an answer within two months. And the first Dutch report will most likely show our 2027 pay."
4. "On paper the five-year plan saves about €1.4M, but that €1.4M is exactly what women could claim back from us."
5. "My proposal: we close the gaps within the same roles within 12 months, in two tranches if we need to, partly paid for by setting aside part of the 2027 merit budget. The structural gap gets a three-to-five-year plan with a fixed yearly budget line. That's where your five-year horizon fits."
6. "Help me cost it precisely. Then let's take it to the CEO together with the lawyers' advice. This is a legal-risk decision as well as a budget decision."

If leadership still chooses the five-year route after that, make sure the CEO or board takes that decision formally, with the legal advice in front of them, rather than settling it informally between you and the CFO.

*Dutch and Belgian rules are still changing (the Dutch date moved last week), so have your lawyers in each country confirm the specifics before you finalise the plan.*
