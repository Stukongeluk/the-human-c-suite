# Performance reviews: proposed process and manager briefing

Status: DRAFT proposal for input from the works councils (Amsterdam, and Hamburg if one exists), managers and counsel. Nothing here is decided. Not legal advice; Dutch and German counsel to review before it goes to anyone.

---

## Part 1. The process (for the CEO, the works councils and HR)

### 1. What we are solving

The CEO's goal, as I understand it: stop carrying real underperformance for years, and raise the standard. That is a fair goal. This process pursues it, without a quota.

What we need to confirm with data before launch (all aggregate, read-only):
- Where underperformance sits today: by team and manager, not only by person. Low performance clusters by manager, role design and workload more often than by individual.
- Regretted attrition, absence and engagement by team, so we have a baseline.
- Rating distributions by group (gender, age, part-time, location, manager) from the last cycle.

### 2. Design principles

1. Ratings are against written expectations for the role, not against colleagues.
2. No target share of anyone in the bottom category. If a team has none below expectations, it has none. If it has three, it has three, each with evidence.
3. Criteria and rating definitions are published before the cycle starts.
4. Calibration aligns standards and checks for bias across managers. It never forces a shape.
5. Every rating can be explained to the person, who can comment on it and appeal it.
6. A person decides every outcome. No score triggers a plan or an exit automatically.

### 3. Rating scale

Four levels, each defined in writing per role family: Exceeds expectations, Meets expectations, Partly meets expectations (development needed), Does not meet expectations.

"Does not meet" is only used with at least two dated, specific examples against the written expectations, discussed with the person during the year. A first-time surprise at review time is a process failure by the manager, not a rating.

### 4. Calendar (to be fixed with the works councils)

| Step | Who | When |
|---|---|---|
| Works council input on system, criteria, appeal route | CPO, councils | Before anything is announced to staff |
| Expectations per role family published | HR, function heads | At least 8 weeks before ratings |
| Manager briefing and calibration training | HR | Before ratings |
| Mid-year check-in with written notes | Managers | Ongoing |
| Manager ratings with evidence | Managers | Cycle window |
| Calibration sessions | Managers, HR; HR presents rating data by group | After ratings |
| Equality check on results | HR, councils informed | Before ratings are shared |
| Review conversations | Managers | Within 2 weeks of calibration |
| Appeal | Employee, skip-level manager, HR | 10 working days |

### 5. Calibration and the equality check

- Compare ratings by manager, function, location, gender, age band, part-time status and people who were on leave. Groups under five are shown in aggregate only.
- A pattern is a finding: investigate before ratings are used for pay, promotion or anything else. Part-time staff and people on parental or sick leave must not be rated down for it.
- A manager whose team has an unusual spread is asked to explain with evidence. We do not push anyone's ratings towards a shape.

### 6. When a person is not meeting expectations: the improvement plan

Start from a specific gap in the work, never from a rank.

Before any plan, the manager and HR check: unclear expectations, workload, tools, team situation, the manager's own part. Health questions go to occupational health or the company doctor, never to the manager.

Plan contents: the gap with dated examples; what good looks like by the end; the support (coaching, training, time, a buddy, adjusted tasks); check-ins every two weeks; a mid-point review; the employee's own comments; signatures by employee, manager and HR.

Length: two to four months, set by how long it realistically takes to show the change in this role. Sixty days is the shortest sensible period and is only appropriate where the gap is narrow and the support can start on day one. A fixed 60-day period for every case is hard to defend before a Dutch court or in a German dismissal dispute.

Possible outcomes: gap closed and plan ends; plan extended once, with reasons; a move to a better-fitting role; only then further steps, with HR and counsel.

### 7. Legal frame in brief (counsel to confirm)

**Netherlands (Amsterdam)**
- The works council must consent to an appraisal system (art. 27(1)(g) WOR) and to rules on dismissal, appointment and promotion (27(1)(e)). Introducing a rating system without that consent is vulnerable to challenge.
- Dismissal for performance goes to the cantonal court (art. 7:669(3)(d) BW), which expects the employee to have been told in good time, supported and given a real chance to improve. A ranking or quota is not that evidence. Employees on sick leave are generally protected against notice (art. 7:670 BW).

**Germany (Hamburg)**
- General appraisal principles need works council consent where one exists (§94(2) BetrVG); ratings made without it cannot be used for personnel decisions. Ask whether Hamburg has a works council rather than assuming.
- Under the Dismissal Protection Act, a dismissal for poor performance usually needs a prior written warning (Abmahnung) and proof of sustained, clear underperformance.

**Both:** a rating system that disadvantages part-time staff, people on leave or a protected group can be indirect discrimination. Data protection impact assessment likely needed for the rating data.

**Timing:** "improve or leave by the end of Q3" cannot be promised as an outcome. Exit after a plan is possible only where the evidence supports it, and in both countries the route and the timing (court, warning, notice period, transition payment) are set by law, not by the calendar.

### 8. What happens to the data and who sees it

Individual ratings are visible to the employee, their manager, the skip-level manager and HR. Aggregates by team of five or more go to leadership. No individual ranking tables circulate. No ranking by activity, attendance or AI-tool usage.

### 9. Measures and review

Agreed up front, at team or group level: regretted attrition, engagement and trust by team, time to fill, internal moves, rating spread by group, number of improvement plans and their outcomes (closed successfully, extended, role change, exit), appeals and their results. Review at mid-cycle and after the cycle with the councils.

## Part 2. Manager briefing (draft, for managers)

**Why you are getting this**

Leadership wants a clearer, fairer review process and a faster response to real underperformance. This briefing explains what we are asking of you and what we are not. The design is leadership's responsibility; your job is to apply it well, and we will support you.

**What is new**

- Written expectations for each role family, published before the cycle.
- A four-level scale with clear definitions.
- Calibration sessions to align standards across managers.
- A clear route for improvement plans, with HR support.
- An appeal route for employees.

**What we are not asking**

- You do not have to put a fixed share of your team in the lowest category. If nobody in your team is below expectations, say so.
- You do not rank your people against each other.
- You do not use sick days, part-time hours, leave, age or anything that stands in for them in any rating.

**Your steps**

1. Know the written expectations for each person's role. If they are unclear, tell HR before you rate.
2. Keep notes during the year: dated examples of work, good and bad.
3. Hold a mid-year conversation. Nobody should hear for the first time at review time that their work is below expectations.
4. Rate against the expectations. For "Does not meet", have at least two dated examples that the person has already heard from you.
5. Prepare for calibration: bring evidence, expect questions, expect HR to show rating patterns by group.
6. Hold the review conversation in person, within two weeks of calibration.

**If someone is not meeting expectations**

1. Talk to them early, in private. Ask what is getting in the way: workload, unclear expectations, tools, something at home or health (do not ask about health diagnoses; point them to occupational health if they raise it).
2. Check your own part: have you been clear? Have you given feedback? Have you given the support?
3. Agree the improvement plan with HR in the room if needed. Use the template. The person writes their own comments.
4. Check in every two weeks and write it down.
5. Never promise or hint at an outcome. Only HR and counsel decide next steps, after the plan ends.

**What to say in the review conversation (examples)**

- "Here is what the role requires, and here are two specific examples where your work fell short. I want to hear your view of them first."
- "I should have raised this earlier. Here is what I will do differently."
- "This rating is not a judgement of you as a person, and it is not final: you can comment on it and appeal it."
- "I am not able to say today what happens after the plan. What I can say is what support you get, and that we meet every two weeks."

**What not to say**

- "The system makes me put someone in the bottom group."
- "If you don't improve in 60 days you're out."
- Anything comparing the person with colleagues by name.

**Likely questions from your team**

- *Is there a quota?* No. Ratings are against the role's expectations.
- *Can I see my rating and challenge it?* Yes, in the review conversation and through the appeal route.
- *Does being part-time or taking leave count against me?* No. We check ratings by group to catch exactly this.
- *Is this connected to layoffs?* No. This process is about performance and development. (Confirm with the CEO before this line is used; if there are plans for cuts, managers must not be told this.)
- *Who sees my rating?* You, your manager, your manager's manager and HR.

**Support for you**

Calibration training, HR business partner for every plan, a coaching session before your first "Does not meet" conversation, and a line to the CPO if you feel pressure to rate in a way the evidence does not support.
