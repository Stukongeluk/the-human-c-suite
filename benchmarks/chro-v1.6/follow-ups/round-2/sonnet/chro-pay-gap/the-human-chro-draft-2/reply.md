Don't take the CFO's plan. Fix the 6% gap for people in the same role and grade within about six months, tell the works councils now, and treat the 14% overall gap as a separate, longer job with yearly targets. A quiet five-year catch-up is more expensive than it looks, and it probably can't stay quiet.

**Why the CFO's plan doesn't hold**

- **The 6% is the legal problem.** Equal pay for equal work is already law across the EU (Article 157 TFEU), whatever the transposition date. The Pay Transparency Directive adds a joint pay assessment with workers' representatives. That applies when a gap of 5% or more in a category of workers can't be justified by objective, gender-neutral factors and isn't fixed within six months. Your 6% is over that line if nothing explains it.
- **It can't stay internal in Belgium.** Under the 2012 pay gap law, employers with 50 or more workers give the works council a pay-structure analysis by sex every two years. With 230 people in Belgium you will have a works council, and it is entitled to this analysis.
- **In the Netherlands the works council has a say.** It has consent rights on the pay system (article 27(1)(c) WOR), and the action plan from a pay evaluation will need its consent under the Dutch bill. Dutch reporting should start in June 2028, on 2027 data, for your 420-person entity. Belgian dates aren't set, because the law isn't published. Each legal entity reports for itself, so check which entity each group of people sits in.
- **Waiting isn't free.** What five years "saves" is pay withheld from women who are underpaid. They can claim it back, and Dutch claims reach back five years (article 3:308 BW). Any employee can also ask for pay levels by sex for equal work. When hidden gaps surface, the story is about the hiding.
- **The quiet route puts you on the wrong side of the money.** Engineering consultancies compete for scarce people. Underpaid women who find out, and they will, leave, and replacing someone costs half to two times their salary.

**What I'd do**

1. **Confirm the 6% (weeks 1 to 4).** Check what explains it: grade, location, tenure, billing level, starting salary. Keep only factors that are objective and gender-neutral. Show the number of people in each cell, and report groups under five only in aggregate. Have counsel review it.
2. **Brief both works councils now (month 1).** Go to them with the real numbers while the plan can still change. Dutch consent is needed anyway, and Belgium's council is entitled to the analysis. Going to them early costs you far less than being forced to later.
3. **Correct the unexplained gap in the next pay round, or off-cycle by month 6.** Raise the people who are underpaid. Don't freeze anyone else and don't ask for a waiver in return. Budget it as a one-off correction, not as a rolling five-year line.
4. **Fix the causes (months 2 to 9).** Starting salaries negotiated case by case, bonus and promotion decisions without criteria, and penalties for part-time or leave. Without this, the gap reopens.
5. **Give the 14% its own plan.** Most of it probably comes from who sits in which job and grade (hiring, promotion, who gets the high-billing work), not from pay within a grade. That needs yearly targets for hiring, promotion and progression, and it is where a multi-year plan belongs.
6. **Tell people.** Say what you found and what you're doing. Say plainly that the gap comes from past pay decisions that leadership owns, not from anything the underpaid did. Publish pay ranges and progression criteria, and give people a route to ask about their own pay.
7. **Review on a fixed date.** Re-run the analysis after the correction and again at year end, with a named owner.

**What to tell the CFO**

- "The budget worry is fair, and I'd rather give you a number than a feeling. Finance and I will price the correction this month: the cost of lifting each underpaid person to the level of comparable peers. It will almost certainly be a small fraction of 6% of payroll, because only some people are below peers."
- "The five-year plan doesn't save that money. It defers it and adds back-pay claims, interest, attrition and a legal position we'd have to defend. I'd compare the routes as what we pay plus what the underpaid go without."
- "Staying silent isn't available. The Belgian works council must get the analysis, the Dutch council has consent rights, and employees will soon be able to ask for the numbers. We'll be better off telling the story ourselves."
- "I can offer you control over timing and cost: a dated plan, a budget you've seen, spread across two quarters if cash flow needs it, with the structural 14% on a separate multi-year path where the cost is slower anyway."

If the CFO has another way to close the gap without keeping it from the people affected, ask for it and test it against the numbers.

**What I'd check before you present this**

- Do you have the cost of the adjustment yet, and the split between the Dutch and Belgian entities?
- Is there a works council in each country, and has either seen pay data before?
- Did the 6% control for tenure and billing level, and for anything else that could be a proxy for gender?

This isn't legal advice. Get employment counsel in both countries to review the analysis, the works council approach and the wording of what you tell staff.

**Key assumptions:** the 6% survives checking and has no objective explanation; the Dutch bill passes roughly as drafted; the Belgian law arrives later. **What am I missing, and what would make this wrong?** If a large part of the 6% turns out to be objectively explained, the correction shrinks, but the explanation still has to be written down and shown to the councils.
