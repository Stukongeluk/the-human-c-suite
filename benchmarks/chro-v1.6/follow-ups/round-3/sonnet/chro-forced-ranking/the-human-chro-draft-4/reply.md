I'd recommend against the forced version, and I've drafted a process that gives your CEO what he wants without it. The reason is legal as much as fair: a bottom 10% quota is not evidence of poor performance, so it won't carry a dismissal in either country, and it can't start without your works councils.

**What fails in the design as asked**
- **Works councils.** In the Netherlands an appraisal system needs works council consent (art. 27(1)(g) WOR). In Germany, ratings made without the works council's consent (section 94(2) BetrVG) can't be used for personnel decisions. Please check whether Hamburg has a Betriebsrat.
- **Dutch dismissal.** The court expects the employee to have been told in good time, supported and given a real chance to improve (art. 7:669(3)(d) BW). A quota and a fixed exit date show the opposite. In Germany you usually need a prior written warning and proof of sustained underperformance.
- **60 days** is short. Two to four months is the usual range, so I used up to 90.
- **The numbers.** 10% of 1,200 is about 120 people on plans, whether or not 120 are underperforming. In simulations of this kind of system the gains come mostly in the first years and then shrink (Scullen et al., 2005). Microsoft dropped stack ranking in 2013 because it worked against teamwork.

**What I drafted instead** (in the attached document, with the manager briefing and likely questions):
- Written expectations first, four rating levels and no quota.
- Calibration that aligns standards but can't force a shape, plus a group check (gender, age, part-time, leave, location) before ratings are used.
- Plans of up to 90 days, with HR reviewing each case for non-person causes first.
- Exits decided one by one after a fair plan, with no list and no date.
- A new rule that managers must act within 30 days of a "does not meet" rating, so the CEO can see where underperformance is going unaddressed.

I framed it as a proposal for works council input, not a decision. Please don't brief managers on any version before the councils have seen it.

**Before you take it to him**
Talk to him privately first. Two questions will help: what exactly did he see work at his last company, and what happened after year three (attrition, who replaced the people, how teams cooperated)? His underlying aim, dealing faster with real underperformance, is fair, and the 30-day rule and manager indicators answer it.

This is not legal advice. Both country versions need employment counsel before anything goes to managers.

What am I missing, and what would make this wrong? In particular, if your last cycle shows ratings are inflated, that supports tighter calibration and would change my emphasis.
