# Performance review and improvement process: proposal for input

Status: a proposal for discussion with the works councils (Amsterdam, and Hamburg if there is a Betriebsrat), not a decision. It replaces the forced-ranking design (10% bottom category, 60-day plan, exit by end of Q3). Not legal advice: employment counsel in both countries should review it before anything goes to managers or staff.

## 1. What changed from the original design, and why

| Original design | This proposal | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 10% of every team in the bottom category | No quota. Anyone who does not meet the written expectations is named, and only them | A quota creates a bottom group whether or not anyone is underperforming. In a 30-year simulation of this kind of system, the gains came mostly in the first years and shrank after that, and they depended on reliable ratings (Scullen, Bergey & Aiman-Smith, 2005). Microsoft dropped stack ranking in 2013 because it worked against teamwork |
| 60-day plan | Up to 90 days, with a check-in every two weeks | Dutch courts expect the employee to have been told in good time, given support and a real chance to improve (art. 7:669(3)(d) BW). Two to four months is the usual range |
| Exit by end of Q3 if no improvement | Outcome decided case by case after the plan, with HR and counsel | A fixed exit date decides the outcome before the plan starts. A ranking or quota is not evidence of poor performance in a Dutch or German dismissal |

For 1,200 people, 10% is about 120 people on plans in one cycle. Each replacement costs roughly half to two times a year's salary, and the people around them see the system.

## 2. Process

**Step 0: decide with representatives (now, before any manager hears about it).**
- Netherlands: the works council must consent to an appraisal system (art. 27(1)(g) WOR) and to rules on appointment, dismissal and promotion (27(1)(e)).
- Germany: general appraisal principles need the works council's consent (section 94(2) BetrVG); ratings made without it can't be used for personnel decisions. Ask whether Hamburg has a works council before assuming either way.
- Share the real numbers and the CEO's goal with them at the problem stage, while the design can still change. Give them a date for written input.

**Step 1: expectations before ratings.** Each role family has written expectations and four rating levels (for example: exceeds, meets, partly meets, does not meet). They are published to all staff before the cycle opens. The same rules apply in Amsterdam and Hamburg.

**Step 2: manager rating with evidence.** Each rating needs examples from the period. Managers rate against the expectations, never against teammates. Part-time staff, people on leave and people with adjusted work are rated on the work they did, with no penalty for hours or absence.

**Step 3: calibration without a shape.** Managers meet with HR to compare standards and check for bias. They may move a rating only with a written, job-related reason. Nobody may move a rating to fit a distribution.

**Step 4: equality check before ratings are used.** HR looks at ratings by gender, age, part-time, leave, location and manager, with groups under five reported only in aggregate. A pattern is a finding to explain before ratings touch pay or consequences. Results go to the works councils.

**Step 5: conversation and right to respond.** Each employee hears their rating from their own manager, in person, sees the evidence, can add their own comments in writing and can ask for a review by the manager's manager.

**Step 6: improvement plan, only for "does not meet" or "partly meets" ratings that persist.**
1. HR reviews the case first. The question: is the gap about the person, or about workload, unclear expectations, tools, the team or the manager?
2. First conversation asks about causes. Health questions go to the company doctor or occupational health, never to the manager.
3. Written plan: the gap with two or three dated examples, what good looks like by the end, the support given (coaching, training, adjusted tasks, a buddy), a period of up to 90 days, check-ins every two weeks and a mid-point review. The employee signs with their own comments, and can bring a colleague or a works council member.
4. Outcomes: closed because the gap is closed; extended once with reasons; a move to a better-fitting role; only then other steps, case by case, with HR and counsel.
5. In Germany, a dismissal for poor performance where the Dismissal Protection Act applies usually needs a prior written warning (Abmahnung) and proof of sustained underperformance. In the Netherlands it goes to the cantonal court (or a negotiated settlement agreement).

**Step 7: exits are individual.** No list, no date. If an exit follows a fair plan, the person hears it from their own manager in person before any email, support (coaching, job search help) starts on the first day of notice, and nobody is locked out as the way they find out.

## 3. How we will know it works (team level, reviewed quarterly)

- Share of people with a "does not meet" rating, by team and manager, and how many plans closed because the gap closed
- Regretted attrition, and attrition within six months of a rating
- Engagement and trust results, by team (groups of five or more)
- Rating gaps by group, and time to fill roles
- Cases where a manager did nothing for more than 30 days after a rating of "does not meet"

If real underperformance is going unaddressed, these show it by manager, which is what the CEO wants to find.

## 4. Manager briefing (draft)

**Why we are doing this.** Leadership wants to deal with underperformance faster and more consistently. That is a fair aim, and it is leadership's job to give you the tools. Responsibility for the process sits with HR and leadership, not with you alone.

**What you do.**
1. Read the written expectations for each role in your team and tell your team where to find them.
2. Keep notes through the year. A rating without dated examples can't be used.
3. Rate each person against the expectations. There is no required number in any category. If everyone meets expectations, say so. If three people do not, say so.
4. If someone does not meet expectations, talk to them within 30 days. Ask what is getting in the way before deciding anything. Call HR before you write a plan.
5. Tell each person their rating yourself, in person, with the evidence.

**What you must not do.**
- Do not rank people against each other or keep a private list.
- Do not use sick days, part-time hours, parental or care leave, age or anything that stands in for those as a reason for a rating.
- Do not tell anyone they will be let go. No outcome is decided in advance.
- Do not ask about health. Send those questions to the company doctor or occupational health.

**Likely questions.**
- *"Is there a quota?"* No.
- *"What if my whole team is strong?"* Then they are rated as strong.
- *"What if someone does not improve?"* HR and counsel decide the next step case by case, after a fair plan and with the employee's comments on record.
- *"Can I disagree with calibration?"* Yes, and you can ask to see the reason for any change.
- *"What support do I get?"* A briefing session, a call with HR before every plan, and a manager-to-manager peer group.

## 5. Timeline (relative; set the dates once the councils have agreed)

- Week 0: private conversation between the CPO and the CEO; works councils informed with the real figures
- Weeks 1 to 6: works council input and consent; counsel reviews both country versions
- Weeks 6 to 8: expectations published; manager briefing sessions
- Rating cycle follows consent; no ratings are used for any decision before it
- Quarterly review of the indicators above
