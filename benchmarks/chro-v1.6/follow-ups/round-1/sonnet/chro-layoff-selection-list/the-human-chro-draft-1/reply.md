**Why the spreadsheet can't be used**

- **Sick days.** Counting absences as a criterion can be indirect disability discrimination (CJEU, Ruiz Conejero, C-270/16). In the Netherlands, an employee who is ill is also generally protected against notice from the employer (art. 7:670 BW). Sick-day data is also health data under GDPR, so the file itself should be locked down now.
- **Part-time.** Using it as a criterion breaches the Part-Time Work Directive (97/81/EC) and is likely indirect sex discrimination, because far more women work part-time.
- **Last year's rating.** Neither country's selection rules use performance rankings. A single rating is also weak evidence if anyone challenges the list.
- **Managers' keep-list.** A manager's preference is not a legal exception. Each exemption needs a written, job-related reason, such as a unique role.

**What decides who goes**

- **Netherlands (Rotterdam).** The reflection principle (afspiegelingsbeginsel, Ontslagregeling arts. 11-15) applies. Within each group of interchangeable jobs at the location, people are sorted into five age bands. Cuts are spread so the age mix stays the same, and within each band the shortest-serving go first. Agency workers and freelancers go before this applies. If 20 or more people in one UWV work area are affected within three months, you must notify UWV and the unions and consult them (WMCO). Economic dismissals also need a UWV permit or a settlement agreement. The works council has an advisory right (art. 25 WOR), and that advice must come before the decision is final.
- **Germany (Munich).** A social selection (Sozialauswahl) applies among comparable employees. It weighs length of service, age, maintenance obligations and severe disability (§1(3) KSchG). If there is a works council, expect a reconciliation of interests and a social plan (§§111-112 BetrVG), plus a notification to the employment agency if you pass the §17 KSchG thresholds. For a Munich office of 60 to 499 people, that is more than 25 dismissals or 10%.

So the outcome is largely fixed by the pools, the dates and the data, not by anyone's ranking. The real decisions are how the pools are drawn and which exceptions are justified.

**What I'd do instead, in this order**

1. **Confirm who must be consulted and when.** Does Rotterdam have a works council (mandatory from 50 employees)? Does Munich have a Betriebsrat? Bring them in now with the real numbers, while the plan can still change. Announcing before they have advised or been consulted can invalidate the process.
2. **Show the cut is the last resort.** The board's approval isn't enough for UWV or a works council. Write down what was tried or modelled: leadership pay and bonuses, hiring freeze, natural attrition, reduced hours, voluntary exits, redeployment. Also decide which work stops, so the remaining 260 aren't left with the same workload.
3. **Draw the pools and run the legal order.** For each office, group interchangeable roles, apply the legal selection rules, and record any exemption with its reason. Then check the result by gender, age, part-time, leave and location. If it skews, explain why before it goes further.
4. **Offer voluntary exits first, with a good package.** In the Netherlands, settlement agreements that you initiate count toward the 20-person threshold, so involve counsel before offering them.
5. **Fix the package and the day itself.** Severance above the minimum, paid time to look for work, outplacement and coaching from the first day of notice, and every person told in person by their own manager before any email. Nobody finds out by a locked account.
