The key to handling this without damaging the relationship is simple: **make the CEO look good, not wrong.**

If you walk in saying *"You shouldn't have promised this"* or *"We don't have capacity,"* he will hear pushback and get defensive. If you walk in saying *"I want to make sure you deliver on what you promised [Customer], and here are the trade-offs to make that happen,"* you become a strategic partner instead of a blocker.

Here is how to play it step by step.

---

### 1. Do 24–48 hours of quiet homework first
Don't react in the hallway or in Slack. Take a day to gather facts so you bring solutions, not just stress.

* **Find out what was *actually* promised.** CEOs often promise high-level outcomes (*"custom reporting," "SSO," "bulk import"*) rather than detailed specs. Ask the CEO (or the AE/CS lead) for the exact email or call context—framed as *"I want to make sure we nail what [Customer] is expecting."* Frequently, a much smaller v1 fulfills the letter and spirit of the promise.
* **Align privately with your Engineering Lead.** Don't ask *"Can we do all of this?"* Ask:
  1. What is the smallest credible version (MVP) of each feature that satisfies the customer?
  2. Roughly how many weeks/sprints does each take (MVP vs. full)?
  3. Which items on the current Q1 roadmap are the cleanest to defer to Q2 with the least collateral damage?
* **Make the capacity math visual.** Put your Q1 roadmap on a single slide or table showing fixed engineering capacity vs. committed work. Treat capacity like physics, not a complaint.

---

### 2. Bring three concrete options—never a flat "no"
Never force a CEO to accept "we can't." Instead, put the executive trade-off in his hands:

* **Option A: Hit the Q1 customer promise in full, slip existing roadmap.**
  * *What happens:* All 3 features ship by end of Q1. [Roadmap Item X] and [Roadmap Item Y] move to Q2.
  * *Risk:* Delayed value for other customers / internal goals.
* **Option B: Phase the 3 features (MVP in Q1, full version in Q2) + protect top roadmap priority.**
  * *What happens:* Deliver a scoped-down v1 (or partially manual/concierge version) of the 3 features by end of Q1 so the CEO keeps his word, while keeping [Critical Roadmap Item Z] on track.
  * *Risk:* Requires a quick alignment call with the customer to confirm v1 meets their Q1 need.
* **Option C: Renegotiate the timeline with the customer.**
  * *What happens:* Ship Feature 1 (their biggest pain point) in Q1, and commit firm dates in Q2 for Features 2 and 3.
  * *Risk:* Requires the CEO (or you + the CEO) to reset expectations—usually easier if you offer a concrete Q1 win and a written delivery plan.

Pick one as **your recommendation** before you walk into the room.

---

### 3. How to run the conversation
Book a 30-minute 1:1 with the CEO (ideally with your Eng Lead, or after aligning tightly with them).

**Start with alignment, not capacity:**
> *"I want to make sure we deliver for [Customer] and protect your credibility on this commitment. I sat down with Engineering to map out what it takes to hit end of Q1, and I want to walk you through our options so you can pick the trade-off you're most comfortable with."*

**Show the math neutrally:**
> *"Right now, the team is 100% allocated to [Item A, Item B, Item C]. Adding these three features is roughly [X] weeks of work. We can absolutely prioritize [Customer], we just need to decide what comes off the plate or how we phase the scope."*

**Present your options and recommendation:**
> *"Given how important [Customer] is, my recommendation is Option B—we ship a clean v1 of all three by end of Q1 so you keep your commitment, and we only defer [Item Y] to Q2. How does that feel to you?"*

---

### 4. If he pushes back with "Can't the team just push and do both?"
This is the most likely pushback. Don't frame your answer around burnout or fairness—frame it around **risk to his promise**:

> *"I'm not worried about the team working hard—I'm worried about us silently slipping and telling you in Week 10 that we're going to miss [Customer]'s deadline. That's the worst-case scenario for you and the account. If we try to do both, we'll likely miss on quality or miss the date. Making an explicit call now lets us guarantee we hit Q1 for [Customer]."*

If he still insists on keeping everything on the roadmap, ask him to **stack-rank** the list anyway:
> *"Understood—we'll push hard. In case we hit unexpected complexity, I want to be 100% sure we protect the right things first. If something has to slip at the end of the quarter, is [Customer] #1 above [Existing Roadmap Item]?"*

That gets his priority order on the record (follow up with a brief email summarizing what you agreed to) so you aren't blamed later when physics wins.

---

### 5. Prevent it from happening again—*after* the dust settles
Do **not** lecture him about process while you're still negotiating Q1. Wait until you've landed the plan (or delivered Q1 successfully), then introduce a lightweight norm framed around helping *him*:

> *"We pulled Q1 off for [Customer], and I want to make it even easier for you to close deals like that without scrambling the team. Next time a big customer asks for a commitment, if you ping me and [Eng Lead] before locking in a date—even just a 15-minute heads-up—we can give you a scoped version and timeline you can put your name on with 100% confidence."*

### Quick recap of what protects the relationship
1. **Align with his goal** (making the customer—and him—successful).
2. **Separate "can we?" from "at what cost?"** (let him make the trade-off).
3. **Anchor pushback in delivery risk**, not team frustration.
4. **Follow up in writing** with the agreed scope, dates, and what was deprioritized.
