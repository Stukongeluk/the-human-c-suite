The core principle here is simple: **don’t make this about process or blame—make it about helping the CEO keep their promise to the customer.**

If you walk in saying *"You shouldn't have promised this, we're at capacity,"* it turns into a power struggle. If you walk in saying *"I want to make sure we deliver for [Customer]—let’s decide which trade-offs make the most sense,"* you stay on the same side of the table and let the math—not your opinion—deliver the "no."

Here is a step-by-step playbook to handle this over the next 48 hours.

---

### 1. Do your homework first (before talking to the CEO)
Give yourself 24–48 hours to gather facts so you aren't debating feelings.

* **Find out what was *actually* promised.** Talk to the AE, CSM, or check call notes/emails. CEOs rarely promise detailed specs—they usually promise outcomes (*"you'll have custom reporting by Q1"*). That ambiguity is your biggest lever: you may be able to fulfill the spirit of the promise with a much smaller MVP.
* **Align privately with your Engineering Lead.** Don't blindside your team, and don't bring vague pushback to the CEO. Get rough estimates (sprint-weeks or T-shirt sizes) for:
  1. The **full version** of all three features.
  2. A **thin-slice / MVP version** that still unblocks the customer.
  3. What is currently in flight for Q1, and what breaks or slips if you pause it.
* **Quantify the cost of the existing roadmap.** Know the business impact of what's currently in Q1 (e.g., *"Item X is tied to $400k in renewals,"* *"Item Y is a compliance deadline"*). Executives speak in business risk, not story points.

---

### 2. Build a 3-option trade-off menu
Never walk into a CEO's office with a problem and a single "no." Bring a 1-page visual (a simple table or slide) showing current capacity vs. total ask, plus **three concrete paths** and your recommendation:

* **Option A: Ship all 3 features by end of Q1 (recommended MVP scope)**
  * *What ships:* Scoped-down versions of all 3 features that meet the customer's core need.
  * *What moves:* Explicitly list the 2–3 Q1 roadmap items that move to Q2, along with their business impact.
* **Option B: Phased delivery with the customer**
  * *What ships:* Feature #1 (highest priority) by end of Q1; Features #2 and #3 in early Q2.
  * *What moves:* Smaller disruption to the Q1 roadmap; requires going back to the customer with a milestone plan.
* **Option C: Protect the current roadmap, add temporary capacity**
  * *What it takes:* Borrowing engineers from another squad or bringing in contractors—be honest about ramp-up time (adding people late in a quarter rarely creates instant velocity).

---

### 3. Run the conversation (with a script)
Keep it calm, brief, and partnership-oriented.

> **Start with alignment:**
> *"I want to make sure we deliver for [Customer] and back up the commitment you made. I sat down with engineering to map out what it takes to hit end of Q1."*
>
> **Let the math speak:**
> *"Right now we have X weeks of engineering capacity left in Q1, and our committed roadmap takes X weeks. These three features add Y weeks—or Z weeks if we build a focused v1. That means we can't deliver both without risking quality or missing the Q1 date for [Customer]."*
>
> **Hand them the steering wheel:**
> *"Here are the three options I see, and the trade-offs for each. I recommend Option [A/B] because [business reason]. Which trade-off feels right to you?"*

#### If the CEO pushes back with *"Can't the team just push harder and do both?"*
Don't argue about burnout or hours—reframe it around **risk to the CEO's promise**:
> *"Even if the team works weekends, the real risk is that we slip on both and ship something buggy to our biggest account. I’d rather we commit to a scope we’re confident we can actually hit so you don't have to walk back a missed deadline in March."*

---

### 4. Lock in the decision and de-risk with the customer
* **Send a 5-line recap email.** Confirm what was decided, which Q1 items are officially moving to Q2, and who will notify affected internal stakeholders or customers. This prevents "wait, why didn't Feature X ship?" amnesia at the end of the quarter.
* **Get on a call with the customer early.** With the AE/CEO's blessing, validate the requirements directly. Very often, customers care urgently about *one* of the three features for Q1 and are completely fine taking the other two in Q2—or they only need a 20% version to solve their immediate pain.
* **Shield your team.** Once priorities are reset, explain the *business why* clearly to engineering so they feel respected rather than jerked around.

---

### 5. Prevent a repeat—*after* the dust settles
Do **not** lecture the CEO about process while you're still in the middle of this fire. Wait until you've landed the Q1 plan (or delivered the win), then have a light, forward-looking conversation:

> *"That Q1 pivot worked out, and I'm glad we got [Customer] taken care of. Next time a big customer asks for something on a call, if you give me a 24-hour heads-up before locking in a date, engineering and I can usually get you a quick MVP option and realistic timeline so you have more leverage in the room."*

Framed that way, a heads-up isn't red tape—it's a tool that helps the CEO negotiate from a position of strength.
