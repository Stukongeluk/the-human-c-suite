This is a classic PM bind, and the good news is you can handle it in a way that actually *builds* trust with your CEO—if you avoid making it a battle over process and instead frame it around helping them succeed with the customer.

Here is a step-by-step playbook for the next few days.

---

### 1. Reset Your Framing Before You Talk to the CEO
Do **not** walk in with *"You shouldn't have promised that"* or *"Engineering says it's impossible."* Both trigger defensiveness and cast you as a blocker.

Instead, anchor on shared intent:
> *"I want to make sure we deliver for [Customer] and protect your credibility with them. Since the team is at 100% capacity, let's look at the trade-offs so we can pick the smartest path."*

Your job isn't to say **no**—it's to make the **cost of yes** visible and let the CEO make the business call.

---

### 2. Do 24 Hours of Quiet Homework First
Before scheduling the conversation, get grounded in facts so you aren't arguing from gut feel:

1. **Clarify what was actually promised:** Get the exact email, call notes, or Slack context. Was it a fully spec'd feature, or a high-level *"we'll get you X, Y, Z"*?
2. **Meet with your Tech Lead (privately):** Don't alarm the whole team yet. Ask your Tech Lead for rough T-shirt sizing on the three features and identify:
   - What is the **leanest viable version (MVP)** of each feature that solves the customer's actual problem?
   - What on the current Q1 roadmap hasn't started yet or can be paused with the least damage?
   - Are there any hard technical dependencies or risks?

---

### 3. Bring Options, Not Just Problems
Walk into the CEO's office (or 1:1) with a **one-page menu of 2–3 concrete options**. Show the existing Q1 commitments, the new asks, and what moves under each option:

* **Option A: Protect the Customer Promise (Roadmap Swap)**
  Deliver the three features (scoped to MVP) by end of Q1 by deferring **[Roadmap Item X and Y]** to Q2. State clearly who/what is impacted by deferring X and Y (e.g., other customers, internal goals, revenue).
* **Option B: Phased Delivery (Split Q1 / Q2)**
  Ship **[Feature 1 + Feature 2]** in Q1, keep **[Top Priority Roadmap Item]**, and commit to **[Feature 3]** in early Q2.
* **Option C: Keep Everything (Explain Why It Breaks)**
  If the CEO asks *"Can't we just do both?"*, explain calmly what happens if you try: either quality drops (bugs/outages for the biggest customer—worse than a delay), or you need temporary capacity (contractors/borrowing engineers, noting that ramp-up time usually means little help inside the same quarter).

Then ask a single, empowering question:
> **"Which of these trade-offs makes the most sense for the business right now?"**

This keeps the CEO in the driver's seat while making it crystal clear that adding scope means removing something else.

---

### 4. De-Risk the Promise With the Customer
Once the CEO picks a direction, offer to join (or lead) a quick scoping call with the customer, framed positively:
> *"We're excited to build this for you—we want 30 minutes with your team to make sure we design it around your exact workflow."*

Two things almost always happen on these calls:
1. You discover that **30–40% of the perceived scope isn't actually needed** to solve their core pain.
2. If you need a phased rollout (e.g., Feature 1 & 2 in Q1, Feature 3 as a beta or early Q2), customers are usually thrilled as long as they feel heard and see a concrete plan.

---

### 5. Protect Your Team When You Roll It Out
Once the plan is locked:
* **Be transparent about the *why*:** Explain the business context (why this customer matters right now).
* **Explicitly announce what is coming *off* their plate:** Nothing destroys engineering morale faster than silently piling new work onto an existing commitment. Make the cut work visible and celebrate the clarity.

---

### 6. Fix the Root Cause *After* the Dust Settles
Don't lecture the CEO about roadmap governance in the middle of the fire. Wait until the Q1 plan is stable, then bring it up lightly in a 1:1:

> *"I want to make sure you never get put in a tough spot on a customer call. Next time you have a big pitch with a strategic account, ping me or grab 10 minutes beforehand—I'll arm you with what's in flight and realistic dates so you can promise with confidence."*

That frames process as **executive air cover**, not bureaucracy—which CEOs almost always appreciate.
