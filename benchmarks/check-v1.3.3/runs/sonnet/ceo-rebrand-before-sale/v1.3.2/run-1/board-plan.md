# Board paper: Protecting value through the sale window

**Decision needed by:** next board meeting · **Decision owner:** CEO · **Prepared:** 20 November 2026 · **Status:** draft, figures marked [FILL] to be completed by Finance, Customer Success and HR before circulation

## 1. What we are asking the board to decide

1. Replace the three agreed moves, as one package starting in Q1, with a sequenced plan: fix the customer experience first, take cost out where customers do not feel it, and decide the rebrand and the structural changes at dated gates, on evidence.
2. Agree the profit target as a bridge (cost, price, churn) with a named owner for each line, so that the plan is judged on EBITDA and on the quality of earnings a buyer will diligence.
3. Agree that leadership, including the CEO, is accountable for the result and takes the first cut in variable pay if the gates are missed.

## 2. The problem in one sentence

We need higher profit that survives a buyer's due diligence in about 18 months, while NPS has fallen from +31 to +6 this year and churn is rising: the three agreed moves would make cost visible quickly, but they land on the same customers and people who are already leaving.

Leadership is responsible for the NPS fall. Four integrations in two years were ours to run, and the customer experience slipped on our watch. This plan starts from that.

## 4. Why the three agreed moves need a second look

**Rebrand all four companies in Q1.** It spends money and management attention in the quarter when customers are least sure of us, and it tells the market "new name" before we can promise "better service". If the four brands carry trust with their customers, we may be retiring the asset that is holding churn back. A buyer pays for retained, growing revenue; a new logo adds nothing to that on day one. The rebrand can still be right. It should follow evidence on how customers see each brand, and it can be staged (for example "Brand, a [Group] company") rather than switched in one quarter.

**A fourth restructure.** Stacking reorganisations is the usual way integration programmes lose their best people. Research on downsizing found that cutting 1% of the workforce was associated with a 31% rise in voluntary turnover the following year (Trevor and Nyberg, Academy of Management Journal, 2008). In a services business that means account managers and engineers, who are the people customers rate in the NPS survey. Removing management layers can be a good idea: fewer approval steps and clearer ownership. It should be designed once, on spans and decision rights, not on a headcount number.

**Ending all contractor and junior roles.** Ending contractors still ends someone's income and takes knowledge with it; during four integrations, contractors often hold the knowledge of the systems being merged. Ending junior roles saves little (they are the cheapest people we employ) and removes the pipeline that grows the seniors of 2029 and 2030. In a 2025 interview the CEO of AWS called replacing junior staff "one of the dumbest things I've ever heard" for the same reason (The Register, August 2025); that is one executive's opinion, not data, and the Stanford Digital Economy Lab finds young workers in AI-exposed occupations already 19% below trend in the US (August 2026 revision, descriptive not causal). The risk is real: if every company stops hiring juniors, nobody grows the experienced staff of the future.

**The steelman for the owners and the CFO.** Profit has to rise, buyers like a clean single-brand story and a leaner structure, and waiting costs time we do not have. Those are fair points. The plan below answers them with cost that comes sooner and holds up better in diligence, and keeps the three moves available at gates rather than ruling them out.

## 6. Recommendation

Run one integration programme in three phases, with dates and gates, and put customers first.

**Phase 1: Diagnose and stabilise (now to month 3).**
- Call the top 50 accounts and a sample of detractors and lost customers within 6 weeks; the CEO and the leadership team do the calls themselves.
- Break NPS and churn down by company, product, segment, region and account owner.
- Name an owner for each of the top five causes and publish the dates.
- Freeze new restructure announcements; start the vendor, tool, office and parallel-system review; hiring freeze with exceptions for customer-facing and critical roles.
- Hold contractors in place until each one's work is mapped; plan handovers and offer conversion to employment where the role is permanent.
- Inform the works councils of the problem and the options now, with the real numbers.
- Leaders' variable pay is re-linked to NPS and churn.

**Phase 2: Build the single customer experience and the savings (months 3-9).**
- One support desk and status page, one invoice and one renewal process, in pilot with one company, then waves.
- Pricing and renewal alignment, with protection for at-risk accounts.
- Execute the cost savings from duplicate spend and product overlap.
- Design the organisation once: spans, layers and decision rights, with the works councils as co-designers. A pilot in one business unit comes first.
- Brand research with customers; decide the name and timing at a board gate (month 6), with endorsed-brand as the default if evidence is mixed.

**Phase 3: Settle and show it (months 9-18).**
- Roll out the structure in waves, only after the previous wave is stable.
- Launch the brand change if the gate said go, ideally after churn has turned.
- Build the diligence pack: cohort retention, NPS trend, savings delivered, attrition and regretted exits, pipeline of junior and mid-level talent.

**Gates (board reviews at months 3, 6, 9).** If NPS and churn have not turned by month 6 and the cost bridge still falls short, the board takes the next decision with the data in front of it, including any reduction in roles, and we do it once, decently and with consultation.

**What I would keep from the agreed package:** removing layers (redesigned once), a single brand (decided on evidence), and ending contractor dependence where roles are permanent (by conversion or planned handover, not by a cut date).

## 7. Human impact

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who is affected? | Managers in layers that change; contractors; junior hires; customer-facing teams in all four companies [FILL numbers] |
| What changes for them? | Roles, reporting lines, income for contractors, and job security in a business heading for sale |
| Disproportionate effects | Long-tenured staff from the acquired companies, contractors with one client, managers with specialist skills; selection must follow the legal criteria, not performance rankings |
| What is hard to replace? | Customer relationships and system knowledge held by a few people |
| What we offer | Paid learning time, internal moves first, coaching and job-search support from the first day of notice for anyone whose role ends, notice for contractors in line with their contracts |
| What do those who stay experience? | A fourth reorganisation: the main risk. We commit to no further structural changes after the redesign |
| Customers and quality | Named account contacts kept through every change; service levels tracked weekly |
| Legal and consultation | In Germany, where a works council exists it has co-determination rights over monitoring-capable systems (§87 BetrVG) and over operational changes through the reconciliation of interests and social plan (§§111-112 BetrVG). Collective dismissals above set thresholds must be notified to the employment agency in advance (§17 KSchG) and dismissals for operational reasons need a social selection (§1(3) KSchG). Notice periods rise with service. Counsel to confirm for each of the four legal entities; this is not legal advice. |
| Early warning | Regretted exits, absence, pulse survey, NPS by account owner; if two of these worsen for a quarter, we pause the next wave |

## 8. Communication

- Works councils first, at the problem stage, with the numbers and options.
- Managers briefed before anyone else, with answers and support for themselves.
- Affected people hear from their own manager, live, before any wider message. No mass emails, no Friday announcements, nothing just before holidays.
- Customers hear what is changing for them and who their contact is; nothing about the brand until the gate decision.
- Follow-up check-ins at two and six weeks after each wave. Employees see this plan's reasons and may challenge them.

## 10. Assumptions, and what would change my mind

- Assumption: the NPS fall comes from integration (support, billing, product changes, lost contacts), not from the market. Would change my mind: detractor calls pointing to price or a competitor's product.
- Assumption: first-year savings from role cuts arrive late and well below the annual figure (consultation, notice and severance come first, and in Germany notice can run up to seven months for long service). Would change my mind: Finance modelling that shows a cut paying back before the sale.
- Assumption: the four brands carry customer trust. Would change my mind: customer research showing they do not, or that customers want one name now.
- Assumption: the buyer will value retention and a stable organisation over a headline cost cut. Would change my mind: advice from the owners' bankers on what the likely buyers weight.

**Pre-mortem for the board.** Imagine the sale in 18 months and it went badly. Likely reasons: churn kept rising during the rebrand; the best account managers left after the fourth restructure; the junior pipeline stopped and delivery capacity fell; the works council went to court. Each one has an owner and a warning sign in this plan.

**Questions for the board.** What euro uplift do the owners need, and by which date? What am I missing, and what would make this wrong?
