# Board plan: raising exit value over the next 18 months

I have not written the plan around the three agreed moves as they stand. Two of them work against the goal your owners care about, and a board paper that repeats them unchanged will be weaker in the room than one that reshapes them. The plan below keeps the intent behind each move (one brand, a leaner organisation, lower cost) and changes sequence and scope. The first section gives you the argument to open with.

## 1. The board's real objective is exit value, not just profit

In 18 months a buyer will pay for three things: **retained and growing recurring revenue**, **margin they believe is durable**, and **an organisation that runs without the current team holding it together**. A buyer's diligence will test all three.

Profit pulled forward by cutting capability shows up in diligence as a quality-of-earnings discount. Buyers will see:

- NPS down from +31 to +6 and churn rising. This is the single most damaging fact in your data room. If it is still falling at month 12, the multiple falls with it, and that costs more than any cost saving.
- Four companies integrated for two years with no stable operating model.
- A hollowed-out engineering and delivery base, with no junior pipeline and no flexible capacity.

So I recommend the board approve a **two-part objective**: (a) stop and reverse the churn and NPS decline, and (b) deliver structural cost savings that survive diligence. Part (a) comes first, because it protects the revenue every multiple is applied to.

## 2. Diagnose before acting (weeks 1 to 6)

Nobody can yet say why NPS fell 25 points. Plausible causes include integration disruption, service quality after reorganisations, product fragmentation across four stacks, account manager turnover and pricing changes. Each calls for a different fix, and some of them are made worse by a rebrand or another restructure.

Actions:

- **Churn autopsy.** Segment lost and at-risk revenue by company, product, customer size, tenure and contract renewal date. Identify the top 30 to 50 accounts by revenue and renewal date in the next 18 months.
- **NPS driver analysis.** Read the verbatims and run 20 to 30 customer interviews, including churned customers. Separate product, service, integration and relationship causes.
- **Employee signal.** Check attrition, regretted attrition and engagement by company. Customer experience at a logistics software group depends on a small number of people (implementation, support, key account managers). Find out whether they are leaving.
- **Cost and capability baseline.** Map where cost sits (management layers, contractors, duplicated functions, four ERPs, four office footprints, overlapping tooling) and what each contractor and junior role actually does.

Deliverable at week 6: a one-page causal view of the NPS fall and a ranked list of the cost levers with the revenue risk of each.

## 3. The three agreed moves, reassessed

### Move 1: Rebrand all four companies in Q1

**Recommendation: do not run it as a Q1 big-bang. Re-sequence it.**

Why it is risky now:

- Customers are already unhappy and churning. A rebrand during that period reads as another disruption and gives dissatisfied customers a natural moment to re-open the vendor question. Contract renewals are often the point at which they leave.
- A rebrand does not fix a service or product problem, and it costs management attention and money (signage, legal entity names, contracts, domains, email, product UI, certifications, partner portals, customs and carrier integrations that reference company identifiers).
- Renaming legal entities and updating contracts, invoices, VAT details and EDI identifiers in logistics, where systems are wired to partners, carries operational risk.

What the rebrand does offer: a buyer values a coherent single platform and brand. That is a real gain, but it is worth most when it lands on top of stable customers and a converged product.

Proposed alternative:

- **Q1:** Decide the brand architecture and name, and do the legal and trademark clearance. Introduce the group name as an endorsement ("X, a [new name] company") at low cost. No forced customer migration.
- **Q2 to Q3:** Roll out in waves, starting with the company and segments where NPS and renewal risk are lowest. Hold back accounts with renewals or open escalations in the next two quarters.
- **Q4 onward:** Full rebrand once churn has stabilised, and in time to appear in the equity story with at least two to three quarters of data.

Decision gate: proceed with each wave only if the previous wave shows no spike in churn, support tickets or NPS.

### Move 2: A fourth restructure to remove management layers

**Recommendation: do a targeted delayering, once, with a clear design. Do not run another open-ended restructure.**

Why the risk is high:

- Three reorganisations in two years have almost certainly produced change fatigue, lost knowledge and regretted attrition. Each further round costs the people you most want to keep, because they have the best options.
- A fourth round with no visible result from the first three will be read by staff, customers and a buyer's due diligence team as a lack of control.
- In Germany this is not quick or cheap. At 900 employees you will have works councils (Betriebsräte), and possibly a group or central works council, to be consulted on any operational change (Betriebsänderung). That means negotiating a reconciliation of interests (Interessenausgleich) and a social plan (Sozialplan). Mass dismissal thresholds under section 17 of the KSchG trigger notification duties to the employment agency. Dismissals must be socially justified (Sozialauswahl), which typically hits younger, shorter-tenured staff first and so protects the wrong people. A realistic timeline is 4 to 9 months from announcement to exit, and severance commonly runs at around 0.5 months' gross salary per year of service as a reference point (often more in practice). Where businesses are being merged or transferred, the rules on transfer of undertakings (section 613a BGB) also apply. Please have German employment counsel confirm every point; I am flagging the issues, not giving legal advice.
- If the restructure starts in H1 and is not complete until H2, you are carrying the disruption into the sale process.

Proposed alternative:

- Design the target operating model once, with named roles and decision rights, using the diagnosis from section 2. Remove layers where spans of control are demonstrably narrow (for example, fewer than 5 direct reports) and where duplication across the four companies is real.
- Involve the works council early, before decisions are announced. In Germany, early and genuine engagement shortens the process and reduces litigation.
- Announce once, with a commitment that this is the last structural change for 12 months. Then keep that commitment.
- Protect customer-facing roles (support, implementation, key accounts) from the first wave unless the data says otherwise.
- Target: complete within two quarters, with savings visible in the run-rate before the data room opens.

### Move 3: End all contractor and junior roles

**Recommendation: do not adopt this as a blanket rule. Replace it with a role-by-role review.**

Why a blanket rule is the weakest of the three:

- **Contractors.** Many contractors in a software and services business fill specialist or peak-capacity roles. Ending all of them removes flexibility at the point the company needs delivery stability. The cost is replaced by delays, missed implementation dates and further customer dissatisfaction. On the other hand, long-running contractors working like employees are a legal risk in Germany (false self-employment, Scheinselbständigkeit, and temporary-agency rules under the AÜG). Both sides of that are real, so review each engagement on its merits instead of ending all of them.
- **Juniors.** Cutting them saves modest money (juniors are among the cheapest people you employ) and sends a signal to everyone else that the company is cutting for short-term reasons. It also empties the pipeline that feeds mid-level roles in two to three years. A buyer will see a business that cannot renew its own skills base. It is a poor equity story to tell.
- Dismissing junior staff in Germany is also the most legally exposed category. Short-tenure staff are often (not always) the first to be selected in a social selection, and fixed-term junior roles carry their own rules under the TzBfG.
- The saving is small relative to the revenue at risk. If the 18-month profit goal is, say, a few million euros, losing a handful of large accounts to churn will cost more than all of the junior and contractor spend combined.

Proposed alternative:

- **Contractors:** Classify each as (a) legally risky or non-essential, to end or convert to employment, (b) specialist capacity, to keep on a defined scope, or (c) duplicated by an employee, to roll off as the work completes. Set rate and scope controls instead of a ban.
- **Juniors:** Keep a smaller, deliberate intake tied to the roles you will need. Freeze headcount growth rather than ending the entry level.
- Report the result as "contractor spend reduced by X% and legal exposure removed", which a buyer will accept, instead of "all junior and contractor roles ended", which a buyer will question.

## 4. Where the profit improvement should come from instead

The best protected-margin levers in a group formed from four acquisitions are usually these, and they sit largely outside the three moves above:

1. **Retention and pricing.** Reducing churn by even a few points raises recurring revenue more than most cost programmes. Add structured renewal management for the top accounts, a save-desk for at-risk ones, and a review of price and contract terms for under-priced legacy accounts. (Treat price rises carefully while NPS is weak: target accounts with clear value delivered.)
2. **Product and platform consolidation.** Four stacks, four ERPs, four sets of tooling. Retire duplicates over 12 to 18 months, starting with finance, HR, CRM and support systems, which customers do not see.
3. **Procurement and vendor spend.** Cloud, licences, offices, travel and professional fees across four companies usually contain quick savings without touching people.
4. **Shared services.** Combine back-office functions (finance, payroll, legal, IT) where duplication is clear. This is where much of the management-layer saving will come from, with less customer risk.
5. **Portfolio clean-up.** Identify unprofitable customers, contracts or product lines that should be repriced or exited before the sale.
6. **Targeted headcount reductions** from the restructure in Move 2, but only after the above are in motion.

## 5. Timeline (18 months)

| Period | Focus | Key gates |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1 to 6 | Diagnosis, works council contact, customer interviews, contractor and cost baseline, top-account protection plan | Board approves the revised plan and savings target |
| Months 2 to 4 | Save-desk and renewal programme live, quick procurement wins, target operating model designed and consulted on, brand decision and legal clearance | Churn trend and NPS pulse (monthly) |
| Months 4 to 9 | Delayering executed in one round, shared services consolidation begins, brand rolled out in the first waves, contractor review completed | NPS at or above +15, churn stabilised |
| Months 9 to 14 | Platform and tooling consolidation, remaining brand waves, margin visible in the run-rate | Two clean quarters of retention data |
| Months 14 to 18 | Data room preparation, equity story, vendor due diligence | Quality-of-earnings review ready |

## 6. Measures the board should track

- Gross and net revenue retention, by company and segment
- Logo churn and top-50-account renewal status
- NPS (quarterly) plus a monthly pulse in at-risk accounts
- Regretted attrition (employees you wanted to keep who left)
- Support response and resolution times, implementation delivery against plan
- Cost run-rate versus plan, split into one-off and structural savings
- Share of savings that are repeatable and documentable for diligence

## 7. Risks and how they are handled

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| NPS keeps falling during the programme | Decision gates on each brand wave; customer-facing roles protected |
| Key people leave after announcements | Retention agreements for the 30 to 50 people customers rely on; one clear announcement; no repeated restructures |
| Works council delay or legal challenge | Early engagement, German employment counsel, realistic timeline built in |
| Contractor misclassification claims | Review all long-running engagements before any decision |
| PE owners push for faster cuts | Show the exit-value logic (section 1) and the monthly dashboard |
| Brand change disrupts partner and carrier integrations | Technical inventory of identifiers before any legal entity changes |

## 8. What I am asking the board to decide

1. Adopt the dual objective: stabilise customers and deliver durable, diligence-proof margin.
2. Approve a 6-week diagnosis before any restructuring announcement.
3. Approve the re-sequenced rebrand (decision in Q1, rollout in waves, with gates).
4. Approve a single, targeted delayering with works council engagement and a 12-month no-further-restructure commitment.
5. Replace the blanket contractor and junior ban with a role-by-role review and a controlled junior intake.
6. Approve the churn and renewal programme and the savings from procurement, shared services and platform consolidation as the primary margin levers.
