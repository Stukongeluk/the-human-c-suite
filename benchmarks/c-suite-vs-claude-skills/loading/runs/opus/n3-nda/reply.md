**Yes, it's one-sided, and it's also very broad.** It runs only one way: the Recipient protects the Discloser, never the reverse. The bigger problem is its scope. As written, the Recipient pays for almost any loss linked to the information, whether or not the Recipient did anything wrong.

## Why it goes so far

1. **"any disclosure"**: the clause doesn't require the disclosure to break the NDA. Read literally, it also covers:
   - disclosures the NDA itself allows, such as to staff or advisers who need to know, or to a court or regulator when the law requires it;
   - disclosures made by third parties or by the Discloser itself;
   - arguably, even the Discloser's original disclosure to the Recipient.
2. **"howsoever caused"**: this takes fault out of the picture. Drafters use this wording to try to cover negligence, and depending on the governing law that can include the Discloser's own negligence. A hack of the Discloser's systems, a leak by its own staff, or a third party publishing the information could all end up as the Recipient's bill.
3. **"any loss"**: there's no cap, no time limit, and no exclusion of indirect or consequential loss such as lost profits, lost deals or damage to reputation. NDAs usually have no fee attached, so there's nothing to set a cap against unless one is written in.
4. **No claims procedure**: the Discloser doesn't have to tell the Recipient promptly about a claim, let it run the defence, get its consent before settling, or keep the loss down.
5. **It comes on top of the normal remedy.** Without this clause, the Discloser can already sue for damages and get an injunction if the Recipient breaks the NDA. With the indemnity, the Discloser doesn't have to prove a breach. It only has to show that a loss "arose from" a disclosure. It may also be able to recover costs, such as legal fees and payouts to third parties, that ordinary damages might not cover.

Indemnities are unusual in NDAs in the first place. Most standard NDAs, mutual or one-way, rely on damages and injunctions alone. A clause like this also tells you how the other side is likely to negotiate the rest of the deal.

**If the NDA is mutual**, the clause can look fair because each party is the Recipient of the other's information. In practice it isn't. It hits hardest whoever receives more information, and for both parties it still applies without fault and without a cap.

## What to propose instead (best option first)

1. **Delete it.** Replace it with: *"The remedies for breach of this Agreement are damages and equitable relief, including injunctive relief."* That is what most NDAs say, and it's the easiest thing to ask for.

2. **If they insist on an indemnity, make it mutual, based on fault, and limited:**
   > "Each party (as Recipient) shall indemnify the other party (as Discloser) against direct losses the Discloser suffers as a result of any use or disclosure of the Discloser's Confidential Information by the Recipient or its Representatives in breach of this Agreement, except to the extent the loss is caused or contributed to by the Discloser or its Representatives. The Recipient's total liability under this clause shall not exceed [amount]. Neither party shall be liable under this clause for indirect or consequential loss or loss of profit."

   Then add:
   - prompt written notice of any claim;
   - the Recipient's right to run the defence;
   - no settlement without the Recipient's consent (not to be unreasonably withheld);
   - a duty on the Discloser to keep its loss down;
   - confirmation that the normal exceptions also apply to the indemnity: public information, information already known, information developed independently, information received from a third party, and disclosure the law requires (with notice).

3. **If they push back on the cap:** keep the cap but drop it for deliberate or wilful disclosure in breach. That's a common middle ground and a fair one.

If you're the **Discloser**, the clause favours you, but expect pushback. A version based on fault (option 2) is more likely to get through the negotiation and to hold up if a court ever looks at it.

## Bottom line

As the Recipient, don't sign it as written. Option 1 is reasonable and widely accepted, and option 2 is a fair middle ground. If the information or the deal is valuable, or other parts of the contract cap or exclude liability, have a lawyer in the governing-law jurisdiction check it. Courts in different countries read "howsoever caused", and indemnities that cover a party's own negligence, differently. This is a first assessment, not legal advice.
