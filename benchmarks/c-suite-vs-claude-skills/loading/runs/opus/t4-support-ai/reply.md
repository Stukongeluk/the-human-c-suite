**No, not as a replacement. Yes to an AI agent working in front of the team, measured on your own contacts, with any change in team size decided later on evidence.**

The number behind that: an AI agent's resolution rate is not the share of work it removes. Here is an illustration with assumed figures (an estimate, so swap in your own). Say the agent resolves 40% of contacts in production, and those are the short, easy ones at about half the average handling time. Then it frees roughly 20% of handling hours, about 4 of your 20 people. Subtract the new work it creates (reviewing conversations, keeping the knowledge base current, tuning, handling escalations), say 1–2 people, and you get roughly **2–3 people's worth of real capacity, not 20**. The hard contacts stay: angry customers, billing errors, problems specific to one account.

To be fair to the case for it: support is often a big cost line, and if most of your contacts are simple and repetitive, an agent can take a large share. That is exactly why you should measure it on your own contacts instead of deciding on a vendor's figure.

**What the track record says**
- In 2024 Klarna said its AI assistant did the work of 700 agents. By May 2025 its CEO said cost had been too dominant a factor and quality had suffered, and Klarna started hiring human agents again (Bloomberg, via Entrepreneur).
- In an Orgvue survey of more than 1,000 leaders (2025), 55% of those who had made people redundant because of AI said they had made the wrong decisions. In a Careerminds survey (February 2026), two in three companies that cut jobs for AI were already rehiring. Both are vendor surveys, so read them as a signal, not as precise rates.
- Gartner (June 2025) predicts that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027.
- You stay liable for what the bot says. In *Moffatt v. Air Canada* (2024) a tribunal held the airline liable for wrong fare information its chatbot gave.
- The opposite approach worked. IKEA's Ingka Group let an AI assistant handle 47% of call-centre queries and retrained about 8,500 call-centre staff as remote design advisers, a business Forrester puts at about €1.3 billion in 2022.

**The options**

| Option | Money | Risk to customers | Reversible? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do nothing | No saving; cost grows with volume | Unchanged | Yes |
| AI helps the team (drafts, summaries, search) | More contacts handled per person | Low | Yes |
| AI takes the simple contacts, people handle the rest, and the team is sized through a hiring freeze and natural turnover | Capacity first; payroll falls only as people leave on their own | Low to medium, if reaching a person is easy | Yes |
| Replace the team | Biggest on paper, minus severance, notice, vendor and oversight costs, and rehiring | High: nobody left who knows the customers or the edge cases | Barely |

**What I'd do**
1. **Measure a baseline** (2–4 weeks): contacts by type, handling time, satisfaction, repeat contacts, cost per contact.
2. **Pilot the agent** on the 3–5 simplest, highest-volume contact types, with a one-click handover to a person. Have the team help design it; they know where it will fail.
3. **Hold a go/no-go review after about 3 months.** Check each "resolved" contact against repeat contacts within 7 days and satisfaction scores, not the vendor's deflection rate. Count the full cost: licences, usage, integration, knowledge-base work, review time.
4. **Count the gain as capacity first:** faster replies, the backlog, reaching customers at risk of leaving, telling product what customers struggle with. Count payroll savings only once the review shows the hours are really freed.
5. **If you still have more capacity than demand**, adjust through a hiring freeze, natural turnover and moves into new roles (checking the AI's answers, owning the knowledge base, customer success). Commit up front that nobody loses their job because of this automation; roles will change. That commitment gets you honest help from the people who know where the AI will go wrong.
6. **Bring in employee representatives early.** In much of Europe a works council has a say on technology that changes people's work, and the EU AI Act requires telling customers when they are talking to an AI. Check both with counsel; this isn't legal advice.

**What would change my mind:** if the pilot keeps its resolution rate with steady satisfaction and few repeat contacts across most contact types, and volume isn't growing, a smaller team is right. Get there over 12–18 months through turnover and new roles, not a cut on day one.

A yes/no on one hard-to-reverse move skips the question that decides it. Two answers would change mine the most:
- What problem are you solving: cost per contact, slow responses or a backlog, or growth you can't staff?
- What share of your contacts are simple and repeatable, and what share are about one customer's account, emotional, or about money?
