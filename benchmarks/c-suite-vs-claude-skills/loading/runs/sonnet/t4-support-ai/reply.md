**Not as a straight swap. I'd put an AI agent in front of your team, let it take the easy contacts, and move your people onto work the agent can't do. Replacing all 20 in one step is a one-way door, and you don't yet have the data to justify it.**

I'm assuming you want to cut cost or handle more volume. If either is wrong, say so, because it changes the answer.

**Why not a straight replacement**
- **A resolution rate is not the share of work removed.** The agent takes the short, easy contacts. Pilots look better than production, some "resolved" tickets come back, and the agent creates new work: reviewing conversations, maintaining the knowledge base, tuning. A first estimate (label it an estimate): contacts x production resolution rate x (handling time of the AI-resolved contacts / average handling time), minus the new AI work. That share is usually well below the headline rate.
- **The hard cases stay.** Angry customers, billing disputes, edge cases, anything with legal or safety weight. Without people who know the product and the customers, those cases go unanswered, and churn follows.
- **The company is liable for what the bot says.** Air Canada was held responsible for wrong information its chatbot gave a customer. Klarna publicly credited its AI assistant with doing the work of hundreds of agents, then brought humans back after quality slipped. Check the current figures and details before quoting either to your board.
- **Layoffs are hard to undo.** Experienced support staff hold knowledge that is not written down anywhere. Rehiring and retraining cost more than the first saving.

**Options**

| Option | Money | People | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Replace all 20 now | Biggest paper saving, minus severance, rehiring and churn | 20 people lose their jobs | High. Hard to reverse |
| B. Do nothing | No change | None | Costs keep growing with volume |
| C. AI handles the easy tier; people move up (my pick) | Slower saving, mostly through hiring freeze and attrition | Roles change, jobs are protected | Low. Reversible at each step |
| D. AI assists the humans (drafts, summaries, routing) first | Modest, early | Positive | Lowest, and it gives you real data |

**What I'd do (D, then C)**
1. **Map the work.** Break the last 3 months of tickets into types. Mark what AI can do well and what needs judgement, empathy or accountability.
2. **Pilot on one or two easy ticket types.** Measure production resolution, repeat contacts, customer satisfaction and escalations, not just deflection. Keep a visible route to a human at all times.
3. **Involve the team in the design.** They know the edge cases and will trust a tool they helped shape. Pay for the learning time.
4. **Use the freed hours.** Proactive outreach, complex accounts, onboarding, feeding product the real reasons customers complain. Backlogs and churn reasons will show you the unmet demand.
5. **Reduce headcount only through attrition and a hiring freeze**, and only once the numbers hold for several months. If you can, commit that nobody loses their job because of this rollout. It buys honest cooperation.
6. **Count the whole cost.** Licences, usage fees, integration, QA, monitoring and the people who maintain the agent. Many AI projects fall short of their business case.

If cuts ever become unavoidable, do it once, decently, with notice, and with leadership taking the first and largest cut. Check employment law and works council or employee-representation duties with counsel. Consult staff representatives before you choose the tool, not after.

**Two questions that would change my answer**
1. What is the real goal: cost, response time, or growth without hiring? Is it a problem you can name with a number and a date?
2. What share of your tickets are simple and repeatable today? Do you have that breakdown?

**Key assumptions:** a customer-facing team, moderate ticket complexity, no immediate cash emergency. If you have only weeks of cash left, the order of steps changes.
