# Improvement plan for an individual performance concern

For engineering managers, with HR. Use it when you have a specific, observed concern about one person's work measured against what their role requires. A position in a ranking or on a dashboard is not a concern. Have HR and employment counsel review this template before first use; it is not legal advice.

## 1. Before you start: check the system

Many performance problems come from the situation rather than the person. Rule these out first, and write down what you found:

- **Workload and assignment:** a heavy on-call period, constant interruptions, or being the only person on an old or poorly documented system.
- **Clarity:** whether the person knows what is expected. If the expectations were never stated, start by stating them; that is not yet an improvement plan.
- **Support:** onboarding, training, tools and access the person should have had but didn't.
- **Health and personal circumstances:** if illness or a personal situation may play a part, stop here. HR and the company doctor (bedrijfsarts) take this route instead, and the details stay with them.

## 2. Evidence

- 3–5 dated, concrete examples from the work, such as design documents, code reviews, incidents, missed commitments or customer impact. For each one, say what was expected and what happened.
- Feedback from 2–3 peers or stakeholders who work with the person regularly, gathered with HR.
- Activity counts (commits, PRs, AI acceptance rate, tickets) are not evidence of performance, so leave them out.

## 3. The first conversation

- Tell the person clearly and early what the concern is and give the examples. Ask for their view and note it.
- Follow up in writing within a few days: the concern, the examples, their response, and the plan you agreed or will propose.
- They may bring someone they trust, and HR attends.

## 4. The plan

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Role expectations | The relevant parts of the job profile or level description, quoted |
| Goals | 3–5 goals tied to those expectations, each observable: what "good" looks like and how you will both recognise it |
| Support | Named mentor or pairing partner, training, protected time, any change in assignment |
| Duration | Long enough to show change in this role, usually two to four months |
| Check-ins | Every two weeks, with written notes the person can comment on |
| Mid-point review | Adjust goals or support if the first half showed they were unrealistic |
| Signatures | Manager, employee (for receipt, with room to add their view), HR |

The test of a good plan: an independent person reading it would agree the goals are fair, the support is real and success is possible.

## 5. Outcome

- **Goals met:** close the plan in writing and say so plainly.
- **Partly met:** extend once, with adjusted goals and support, if progress is real.
- **Not met:** with HR, look first for another suitable role inside the company, with training if needed. Only then consider next steps with HR and employment counsel. A dismissal for poor performance has to show that the person is unsuited to the work, was told in time, had a real chance to improve, and that the problems were not caused by illness or by a lack of training or support from the employer ([UWV factsheet](https://www.uwv.nl/werkgevers/Images/factsheet-ontslag-bij-disfunctioneren.pdf)).

## 6. Keep it fair

- No quotas: plans start from a concern about a person's work, never from a target number of plans.
- Keep the file factual and dated, and share everything in it with the employee.
- HR checks across teams, at least once a year and in aggregate, that plans are not falling disproportionately on part-timers, people returning from leave, or any particular group.
