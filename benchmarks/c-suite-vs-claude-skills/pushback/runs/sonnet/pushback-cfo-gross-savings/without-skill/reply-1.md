## What the model shows

| | Downside | Base | Upside |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average exit date | 1 Aug 2027 | 1 Jun 2027 | 1 Apr 2027 |

| Net run-rate from 2028 | €4.3m | €4.5m | €4.75m |

The assumptions behind this are a 60 NL / 20 BE split and loaded costs of €60k per FTE in NL and €70k in BE.

1. **The arithmetic doesn't reach €6m even on a full-year basis.** €6m across 80 people needs an average loaded cost of €75k per head. A logistics workforce will usually sit well below that unless the 80 are mostly senior or office roles. The role list needs to show this.
2. **Timing removes most of 2027.** Employees are paid through consultation and notice, so savings only start on exit. Mid-year exits capture about 7 of 12 months.
3. **Severance lands in the same year as the saving.** Dutch transition payments plus a social plan, and Belgian notice indemnities (which are much more expensive), consume most of the in-year gain. Expect payback about 6 to 8 months after exit. The real benefit is 2028 onward.
4. **P&L timing may be worse than the table suggests.** Under IAS 37 the restructuring provision is usually booked when the plan is announced or the representatives are informed. That can pull the cost forward and make the 2027 P&L look weaker than the cash view.

The honest framing for the board is roughly **"€4.5m to €5m annualised run-rate from 2028, and roughly break-even to modestly positive in 2027"**. If the CEO needs €6m in 2027, the model says redundancies alone can't deliver it. Getting there would take a much larger or more senior reduction, with more cost and more risk.

## Why I would also push back on the plan itself

- **You restructured last year.** Three questions need answers before this goes to the board:
  - Were last year's savings actually delivered and visible in the 2027 baseline? If the €6m partly overlaps with them, it is double-counted.
  - Is there slack left? Leakage in a second round is typically higher than in a first. Once the easily removed work is gone, what remains tends to get covered with temps and overtime, so my base of 10% could be low.
  - What has happened to attrition and engagement since? Second rounds tend to lose the people you want to keep, who leave voluntarily and then need replacing.
- **Process drives the timeline, and I'd have counsel confirm it.** My understanding is as follows, but please verify it.
  - In the Netherlands, a collective redundancy typically means notifying unions and UWV (WMCO), works council advice, a UWV dismissal permit and the afspiegelingsbeginsel. Selection then follows age cohorts and tenure rather than performance, so you can't simply choose who goes.
  - In Belgium, check whether the collective dismissal thresholds (Renault procedure) are met, plus information and consultation duties and any outplacement obligations.
  - A seven-month average exit date may be optimistic once two jurisdictions are involved. The downside column exists for that reason.
- **Cheaper routes to part of the number.** The model doesn't include these, but they should sit next to it in the pack:
  - A hiring freeze and not backfilling natural attrition. At 900 people, normal logistics turnover is likely well over 80 leavers a year.
  - Reducing temp and subcontractor spend.
  - Network, route or shift optimisation.
  - A phased approach, where voluntary exits come before any compulsory ones.
