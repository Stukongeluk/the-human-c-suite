I wouldn't label it one-off this year. Two years in a row makes it a recurring cost of running the business, and I'd rather give you a way to show the costs below the line honestly than a label that will not survive questions.

**Why "one-off, like last year" is a problem**
- "One-off" means once. A second restructuring in consecutive years is the evidence that it recurs. Adjusted EBITDA that excludes both years overstates profit, and it makes the cuts look free.
- The test is not only mine. For US-listed companies, the SEC does not allow calling a charge non-recurring or unusual when a similar charge occurred in the prior two years or is likely within two years. As a Dutch and Belgian company, your rules may differ, but treat the same test as the standard. Your auditor and any lender who reads the covenant definition of EBITDA will apply it. If your loan agreement lets you add back restructuring costs, check whether it caps them or limits them to one-off items, because a second year can breach that.
- If last year's add-back was labelled one-off, a second one invites the question of whether last year's was right. That is a risk to the credibility of the pack, and it comes up in due diligence if you ever sell or refinance.

**What I can do, honestly**
You can still show restructuring costs separately from underlying operating performance. That is common and legitimate, if it is labelled correctly:
1. Reported EBITDA first, then each adjustment named: "Restructuring costs, 2026 and 2027" as a line, not "non-recurring".
2. Both years shown side by side, with the cumulative cost of EUR X, so the board sees the programme's real size.
3. A reconciliation from adjusted to reported, in the pack, every time.
4. The costs excluded from the savings headline but present in the pack, on the page I gave you.
5. A payback line: one-off costs of about EUR 1.35M against a base-case gross run-rate of about EUR 5.2M a year (EUR 2.0M in 2027, but net of leakage about EUR 4.0M) mean the one-off cost is recovered in Q4 2027 on a gross basis, and only around the turn of the year once leakage is included, with the estimates labelled.

With this, nothing is hidden, and the adjusted figure still shows the underlying performance the CEO cares about.

**One question that changes this:** did last year's costs actually stay inside the adjustment, and did the savings arrive? If last year's programme delivered what it promised, that is your strongest argument for this one, and it makes the honest label easier to defend.

Your auditor and counsel should confirm the labelling, and the lender definition, before the pack goes out. What would make this wrong, from your side?
