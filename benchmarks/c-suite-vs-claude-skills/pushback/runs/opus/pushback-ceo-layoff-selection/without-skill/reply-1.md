## Four points that change the plan

**1. Legally, the board's decision is still only a proposal.**
- At this size, the reorganisation needs the works council's (OR's) advice under art. 25 WOR. You have to ask for that advice while it can still change the decision.
- If 60 jobs are announced as already decided, the OR can go to the Enterprise Chamber (Ondernemingskamer). The court can have the decision withdrawn. That costs far more time than consulting properly.
- So frame it as a proposed decision (voorgenomen besluit), and back it with a real business case and real alternatives.

**2. Ask the board for a euro target, not a headcount.**
- "60 jobs" is a means. The goal is €X of yearly savings by a set date.
- With a euro target, savings on contractors, open vacancies, people who leave on their own and non-staff costs all count toward it.
- Contractors are the biggest lever. A freelance developer costs roughly twice as much as an employee, so ending 10 contracts saves about as much as cutting 20 jobs. Dutch selection rules require external workers in the same roles to go first anyway.
- The plan treats 60 as the ceiling. The real number of forced redundancies comes out of the business case.

**3. "By end of March" works for decisions, not for departures.**
- I'd start formally on Tuesday 5 January, with nothing announced over the holidays. On that timeline, by the end of March the decision is final, everyone affected has been told, most termination agreements are signed and the rest are filed with UWV.
- Notice periods (1–4 months) have to be respected, or people's unemployment benefit starts late. So most contracts end between May and July, and payroll savings start in Q2.
- If your final decision differs from the OR's advice, there's a mandatory one-month pause and March slips by about three weeks. Tell the board this now, not in March.

**4. Dutch law decides who goes. You and your managers don't pick names.**
- What you decide is which work stops and how many positions disappear in each function category.
- Who leaves then follows a fixed legal order, the afspiegelingsbeginsel:
  - external and flex workers first;
  - then employees who have reached state pension (AOW) age;
  - then on-call staff;
  - then fixed-term contracts that are about to end;
  - then permanent staff, spread over five age bands, with the most recently hired in each band leaving first.
- This runs across all interchangeable roles at the Utrecht site, not team by team. Closing team X can mean people in team Y leave.
- UWV will refuse applications based on performance rankings, "keep the best" lists or salary.
- Switching to termination agreements doesn't get around this. Anyone selected outside the legal order can refuse to sign, and then you have no route left.
- Performance problems stay a separate, individual process.

**The sociaal plan I'd propose**
- **Severance:** the statutory transition payment (1/3 of a month's salary per year of service, capped at €102,000 in 2026) plus a top-up, with a floor of two months' salary. The selection rule picks people with the shortest service, who would otherwise get almost nothing.
- **Notice periods:** always respected.
- **Outplacement and training:** about €4k per person.
- **Practical help:** people keep their laptop, get references and active introductions to companies that are hiring, and have first pick of internal vacancies.
- **International staff:** immigration help. They usually have three months after their contract ends to find a new sponsoring employer.
- **Volunteers (plaatsmakersregeling):** someone in the same role and age band can volunteer to leave in a colleague's place.

On my illustrative numbers, this costs about €0.2–0.3M more than the legal minimum, against about €4.9M a year in savings.

**Your own share.** If 15% of staff go, management should shrink at least as much, and the executive team should give up its 2026/27 bonuses. Staff will judge whether the whole thing was fair largely by that.
