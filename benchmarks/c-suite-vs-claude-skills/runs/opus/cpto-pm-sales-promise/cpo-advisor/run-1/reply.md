📊 **CPO: Your CEO promised three features to your biggest customer**

**BOTTOM LINE**
Don't say no, and don't absorb it quietly. Answer "yes, and here's what it costs": bring the CEO two or three options with their costs spelled out and let him decide what moves. Confidence: 🟡 Medium. The approach is well established, but I haven't seen the features, the customer's wording or your capacity numbers.

**WHAT**
- Your team is at capacity, so three extra features mean something on the current roadmap slips. You can't prevent that. You only decide whether it gets chosen in November or found out in February. [VERIFIED: follows from your statement that the team is at capacity; adding people this late usually slows a team down rather than speeding it up] 🟢
- What the CEO actually committed to is the customer relationship (probably a renewal or expansion), not three exact specs. There's usually room to change what each feature has to do, as long as the date holds. [ASSUMED: not checked against what was said or signed] 🟡
- Nobody knows how big the three features are yet, you or him. A rough sizing from engineering is the fact that makes the conversation possible. [ASSUMED] 🔴
- If Q1 means January to March 2027, you have about 18 calendar weeks. That's roughly 15–16 working weeks after the holidays, and testing and release time come out of that too. [ASSUMED: calendar fiscal year] 🔴
- Talking about trade-offs won't damage the relationship. Surprising him or embarrassing him in front of the customer will. A missed promise in March costs him far more than a hard 30-minute conversation now. 🟡

**WHY THIS MATTERS**
If you miss a promise to your biggest customer, you lose trust with the account and with the CEO. If you hit it by slipping the roadmap without telling anyone, or by running the team on overtime, you break promises to everyone else. You also teach the company that customer commitments cost nothing, so the next one will arrive the same way.

**HOW TO ACT**
1. Get the exact promise. Find out the wording, whether it's in a contract or renewal, what problem each feature solves for the customer and what "done" means to them. → You, with the CEO or the account owner → this week
2. Get a rough size for each feature (S/M/L, with how confident engineering is) against what's already planned for Q1, and name the specific items that would have to move. → You + engineering lead → within 5 working days
3. Build three options (see YOUR DECISION) and choose the one you'll recommend. → You → within 7 working days
4. Meet the CEO one-to-one, not in a group meeting. Open with "I want to help you deliver this." Show the options, recommend one, then ask him to choose and to back the deferrals when people ask why their work moved. → You → within 2 weeks
5. Confirm the decision by email the same day: what you'll deliver, what moves, the risks, and a check-in every two weeks. Once delivery is on track, propose a 48-hour sanity check before anyone commits a date to a customer. → You → day of the meeting, then once the plan is on track

⚠️ **RISKS**
- **The contract fixes the scope.** Then option C is off the table. Step 1 tells you this before you build options around it.
- **You absorb it with overtime.** The roadmap slips anyway, likely around February, the team burns out, and the CEO learns that promises are free. Rule this out; don't offer it as an option.
- **He says "do all of it".** Put in writing what will most likely slip and how likely that is, then ask him to rank the work. Don't agree to a plan you know won't hold.
- **You go around him**, by venting to peers or escalating over his head. That's what really damages the relationship. Keep it between the two of you until he has decided.

🔑 **YOUR DECISION** (which option you recommend; the CEO decides)
- **Option A: all three in full by Q1, with named roadmap items deferred.** It keeps his promise word for word. The cost is other commitments, which he has to own.
- **Option B: the most important one or two by Q1, the rest early in Q2.** This does the least damage to the roadmap, but he has to go back to the customer.
- **Option C: a slimmer version of each by Q1 that solves the customer's core problem.** It keeps the date and most of the roadmap, but the customer has to accept the smaller scope.
- **Recommendation:** lead with C if step 1 shows the scope has room. Otherwise go with A, with deferrals he signs off on. Either way he keeps his word to the customer, and he only has to choose, never admit a mistake.
