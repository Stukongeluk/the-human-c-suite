**My advice: don't buy this system as specified, and don't promise go-live in six months. Instead, offer the alderman a six-month plan that measurably improves enforcement, with a decision point at the end.** The "90% accurate" claim shows why. Suppose 5% of recipients commit fraud (an assumption; the real rate is probably unknown). Then a model that flags nobody is 95% accurate. A 90%-accurate model that sends the top 10% to investigators flags 100 of every 1,000 recipients. Only 25 of those 100 are real cases; the other 75 committed no fraud, and half the fraud goes unflagged.

**Why**

- **That figure doesn't measure what matters.** Ask the vendor for the hit rate within the top 10%, the error rates by group (nationality, gender, age, single parents), and whether it was measured on your data. Models like this learn from past investigations, so they learn whom investigators chose to look at, not who commits fraud.
- **This exact design has already failed in the Netherlands.** From 2017 to 2021, Rotterdam scored about 30,000 recipients and sent the top 10% to investigators. Its own documents showed the model was only 50% better than random selection. The city paused it in 2021 over bias it couldn't remove. Amsterdam built a "fair" version by the book: an explainable model, no gender, nationality or age, and bias testing. It stopped the pilot in November 2023 after spending about €535,000. The bias had come back in a new direction, and the model flagged more people than caseworkers without finding the right cases any better. In 2020 a court struck down SyRI as insufficiently transparent and verifiable, and the childcare benefits scandal showed where risk profiling ends.
- **Combining the data sources is the weakest point legally.** The AI Act's ban on social scoring has applied since February 2025. The Commission's guidelines give welfare agencies estimating fraud risk from data collected in unrelated contexts as an example of a prohibited practice. Your debt-counselling and housing data were collected for other purposes. If it isn't prohibited, it is very likely high-risk, because it concerns public benefits. The high-risk obligations, including a fundamental rights impact assessment, apply from 2 December 2027, about seven months after the planned go-live. The GDPR adds a DPIA and purpose limitation on the debt-counselling data, and after the CJEU's SCHUFA ruling a score that largely decides who is investigated may count as an automated decision. This isn't legal advice: get a written view from your FG and legal team before anything is procured.
- **It hurts the people you serve, and it costs money.** If asking for debt help raises your fraud score, people stop asking for help early, and debts grow. Three in four investigations would hit people who committed no fraud, with stress, suspended payments and appeals. The Participatiewet in balans, partly in force since January 2026, puts trust and the human dimension at the centre; this pulls the other way.
- **Six months doesn't fit.** First you need a legal basis, a DPIA and IAMA, procurement and bias testing on your own data. You also need advice from the cliëntenraad and the works council, since the system would steer investigators' work. Amsterdam's participation council objected in 2022, and it was right.

**What I'd offer instead: six months, with results the alderman can show**

1. **Months 1–3: measure.** Investigate a random sample of a few hundred files. It shows the real rate and what lies behind it: deliberate fraud, honest mistakes with complex rules, or the municipality's own errors. Any tool must beat this baseline, and it is your only unbiased test data.
2. **Months 1–6: fix what causes errors.** Simplify reporting changes in income or household. Contact people as soon as an Inlichtingenbureau signal arrives instead of reclaiming months later. Use plain-language letters. Fewer errors mean fewer investigations.
3. **Selection you can explain.** Use published rules on the benefits data itself plus a random component, registered in the Algoritmeregister. An investigator always decides, and no payment is ever stopped on a score alone. Bias-test the rules too: DUO's simple rules (distance to parents, type of education) still discriminated indirectly, and DUO now checks at random.
4. **Month 6: decision point.** Only if the baseline shows that a model would beat this, run a narrow pilot on benefits data only. Fix the stop criteria before it starts, add an independent audit and involve the cliëntenraad. The contract must give you the model documentation, the test results and AI Act conformity at the vendor's cost.

**The other side.** The council has a real point: fraud erodes support for the safety net, investigators are scarce, and doing nothing isn't neutral. Finance should see the vendor route's full cost: licence, integration, investigating innocent people, appeals and compensation risk.

**How to raise it.** Talk to the alderman privately before the committee. You could say: "In six months I can give you fairer and more effective enforcement, with numbers to show for it. In six months I can't put a fraud score on every recipient without a real risk of us becoming the next Rotterdam."

Two questions would change my advice. What triggered this: an audit finding, rising amounts reclaimed, or a political incident? And has the council already committed money or signed anything?
