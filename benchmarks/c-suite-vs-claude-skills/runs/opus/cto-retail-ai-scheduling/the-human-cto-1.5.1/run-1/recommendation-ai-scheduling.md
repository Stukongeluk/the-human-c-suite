# AI workforce scheduling: pilot first, then roll out in waves

**From:** CTO · **To:** COO, CEO, CFO and the executive team · **Status:** draft for discussion · **Facts checked:** 23 November 2026

## Recommendation

Go ahead with demand-based scheduling, but not as a 140-store go-live by March. Run a 12-week controlled pilot in about ten stores, starting once the central works council has consented. Decide on rollout by early summer 2027 on measured results, then roll out in waves from September 2027, with no go-lives during the December or Easter peaks.

**Decision requested:** approve the plan below, a pilot-only contract with an option to expand, and opening talks with the central works council this month.

## Why the idea is worth pursuing

The case for the tool is real. Labour is the largest cost the stores control, margins are thin, and stores staffed partly by habit end up with too many people in quiet hours and too few at peaks. Better forecasts can fix both, which helps customers and staff. The open questions are how fast, with which safeguards, and how much of the saving we can honestly put in the budget. A phased plan can drift, so this one has fixed dates and a decision gate.

## Why not all stores by March

**1. Much of the 12% may not be available under Dutch rules.**

- On-call staff (zero-hours contracts and the flexible part of min-max contracts) must be called at least four days ahead, or they may refuse. A collective agreement can shorten this to one day. Shifts cancelled or changed within that period must still be paid. Publishing schedules three days ahead is below the statutory default.
- On-call staff working under 15 hours a week without fixed times get at least three hours' pay per call, so precision to the quarter hour often can't become shorter paid shifts.
- Youth rules: 15-year-olds may work at most 2 hours on a school day and 12 hours in a school week, between 7:00 and 19:00. 16- and 17-year-olds may work at most 9 hours per shift and no nights. School time counts as working time.
- Our collective agreement's roster rules come on top. For comparison, the CAO Levensmiddelenbedrijf requires the full roster a week ahead. The supermarket CAOs expired on 1 April 2026 and the talks were suspended on 17 November 2026, so roster terms may change and the unions are at the table now.
- From 1 January 2028, zero-hours contracts give way to bandwidth contracts (at most 30% above the agreed minimum). Pupils' and students' side jobs are exempt under conditions. The tool has to handle both groups.

Savings from same-day cuts we must pay for anyway are not savings. We need to know where the 12% was measured, under which labour rules and against what baseline.

**2. The works council must consent, and privacy law applies.**

- Rules on working and rest times, including rosters, need works council consent (article 27(1)(b) WOR). So do systems suited to monitoring attendance, behaviour or performance (27(1)(l)) and rules on processing staff data (27(1)(k)). If we go ahead without consent, the works council can invoke nullity within a month, and staff then don't have to follow the schedules.
- A data protection impact assessment is mandatory: large-scale processing or systematic monitoring of employees, and profiling, are on the Dutch Data Protection Authority's list.
- If the tool allocates shifts using individual behaviour or characteristics, it may be high-risk AI under the AI Act (Annex III). Since the AI Omnibus entered into force on 27 July 2026, those obligations apply from 2 December 2027, including informing workers and their representatives before use. A system bought now will be in scope within a year, so we should buy it ready.
- Early consultation also tends to give better results: the OECD finds that AI at work turns out better for workers where their representatives are consulted, and that consultation can keep the productivity gain while improving job quality.

**3. The timetable runs through our two busiest periods.** Live by March means eight to ten stores a week from now, through Sinterklaas, Christmas and New Year and into the run-up to Easter (28 March 2027). Each go-live needs working integrations with the tills, HR, payroll and time registration, trained store managers, and an app for thousands of students. Any pay or schedule errors would hit 9,000 people in the busiest weeks of the year.

**4. Fewer hours can cost sales and staff.**

- In a randomised trial in 28 Gap stores, more stable and predictable schedules raised median sales by 7% and labour productivity by 5%. That came on top of two weeks' notice and no on-call shifts, which Gap had already introduced in all its US stores.
- At one retailer, right-sizing staff in 168 stores, which meant adding labour where it paid off, raised revenue by 4.5% and annual profit by nearly $7.4 million after the cost of the extra labour.
- In US retail and food service, schedule instability is a strong predictor of quitting for the third of workers with the least stable schedules.
- 12% of hours is the equivalent of about 1,080 of our 9,000 people, or roughly one shift in eight for everyone. For our students, that is income.

## Proposed plan

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Dec 2026 to Jan 2027 | Share the problem, the vendor's claims and this plan with the central works council, and agree the pilot's purpose, data and safeguards. Start the impact assessment. The vendor backtests its forecasts on 12 months of our till data for the pilot stores. Reference calls with customers we choose. Pilot-only contract. No go-lives in December. |
| Feb 2027 | After consent: configure, integrate and test in about ten pilot stores, each matched with a control store. Paid training for store managers and staff. Staff hear about it from their own store manager first. |
| Mar to May 2027 | 12-week pilot, with Easter as a stress test. Monthly review with store managers and the works council. |
| June 2027 | Decision: go, adjust or stop, on the measures below. Works council consent for rollout. |
| Sep 2027 to Mar 2028 | Remaining 130 stores in waves of 20 to 25 a month, paused in December. Configured for the new flex-work rules by 1 January 2028. |

If consent takes longer, the dates move with it; the vendor's calendar doesn't set the pace of the consent process.

## Safeguards to agree with the works council

- Schedules published at least two weeks ahead, more notice than the law and the CAO require.
- Minimum shift lengths per contract type, within the youth rules.
- Same-day changes only by the employee's choice: extra shifts are offered, not imposed, and cancellations inside the notice period are paid.
- Staff set their availability and can swap shifts in the app.
- Youth working-time rules and CAO rules built in and tested before go-live.
- The tool proposes; the store manager approves every schedule and can override it.
- No individual speed, performance or reliability scores in shift allocation. Reporting at store and department level only.
- Staff can see how their schedule was made and how to challenge it.
- Data used only for scheduling, with retention, access, data location and sub-processors agreed.

## How we judge the pilot

Measures and thresholds are fixed before go-live and compared with the control stores:

- **Business:** paid labour hours, sales, shelf availability, checkout waiting times, fresh waste, customer scores.
- **People:** turnover, sickness absence, changes inside the notice period, average hours and earnings of flexible staff, a short staff pulse, store managers' time spent on scheduling.
- **Technology:** forecast accuracy per store and hour, schedules published on time, pay errors, app adoption, incidents.

We go ahead if labour hours fall while sales, availability and queues hold or improve against the control stores and the people measures are no worse. Otherwise we adjust or stop.

## Where saved hours go

We agree in advance that lower hours come first from not replacing leavers, not from cutting existing staff's contract hours, and that part of the hours freed in quiet periods moves to peaks and shelf availability where the data shows it pays. As an illustration: at 30% annual turnover among flexible staff (an assumption; HR has the real figure), not replacing leavers frees 12% of hours in about five months.

## Money

- The 12% is the vendor's claim, not our forecast. The pilot gives us a figure we can budget.
- The first-year saving will be below the annual run rate, because licences, integration, parallel running and paid training come first. One hour of training for each of 9,000 staff is already 9,000 paid hours (estimate).
- We compare total cost over three to five years: licences per store, integration, support, and the cost of leaving (data export, contract terms).
- We ask what the March date buys us. If it is a discount, we weigh it against the cost of a failed rollout in 140 stores.

## Before we sign: what we ask the vendor

1. Where the 12% was measured: countries, contract types, labour rules, baseline and period.
2. A backtest of forecast accuracy on our own data, per 15 minutes and per day.
3. Three references in European grocery of our size, chosen by us.
4. A test, not a slide, of how the rules engine handles Dutch youth rules, the call-in rules, our CAO and the 2028 bandwidth contracts.
5. Its AI Act classification and documentation, the data processing agreement, data location and sub-processors.
6. Pilot pricing with an expansion option, plus exit and data-portability terms.

To keep the decision fair, criteria and weights are fixed before anyone scores, "improve our current scheduling" and at least one other vendor are scored as options, and anyone with an interest in a vendor declares it. IT's own interest: we would carry the integration work, which could bias us towards a slower plan, so judge this proposal on its evidence.

## Assumptions and what would change this recommendation

**Assumptions:** one central employer under the CAO Supermarkten VGL; a central works council; stores schedule today with simpler tools; a large share of staff on on-call or min-max contracts.

**What would change it:** references from Dutch grocers showing measured savings under the same rules without lost sales or availability; a backtest showing large overstaffing we can remove within the law; or the works council preferring a faster pace.

The legal points are a summary for discussion, not legal advice. Employment counsel and our data protection officer should confirm them.
