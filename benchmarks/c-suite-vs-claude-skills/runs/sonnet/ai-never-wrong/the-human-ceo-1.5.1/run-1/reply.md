I wouldn't put your name on a case to cut 16 roles on this claim, so I've written the business case for something that can be defended: a six-week measured pilot with QA and support fully staffed, then a decision on the results. The draft is in `business-case.md` in the same folder.

**Why not the cut as asked**
- "Basically never wrong" has no number behind it. Which error rate, on which tasks, found by whom?
- QA and second-line support are the people who catch AI mistakes. Cut them and errors keep happening but stop being counted, so the claim can't be tested.
- The company owns what its AI says. In Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024) the airline was held liable for its chatbot's wrong answer.
- Perceived gains can mislead. In METR's 2025 trial, experienced developers using AI took 19% longer while believing they were faster. Klarna announced in 2024 that AI did the work of 700 agents, then said in 2025 that quality had suffered and began hiring humans again.

**What the draft contains**
- The decision, the facts we have and, plainly, the ones we don't (error rate, resolution and repeat-contact rates, escaped defects, fully loaded cost of the 16 roles). I didn't have your data, so there are no savings figures; inventing them would make the case look stronger than it is.
- Options side by side: do nothing, cut as proposed, pilot then decide (recommended), use AI to remove toil, attrition only if the pilot supports it.
- A pilot design with metrics and thresholds fixed in advance, a formula for sizing freed capacity in hours, a human impact section and the consultation steps (works council, employment counsel).

**Two questions that would change the answer**
1. What is the target, in euros and by when? If it is cash or covenants, we should look first at cloud, licence, vendor and AI spend, and at leadership pay.
2. What error rate is the CTO assuming, and where does it come from? If there is real evidence, share it and I'll fold it in.
