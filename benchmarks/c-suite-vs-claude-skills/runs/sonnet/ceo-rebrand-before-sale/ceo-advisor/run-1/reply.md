# Board plan: protect the exit value of the group

## Bottom Line

I would not take the three agreed moves to the board as written. Taken together, they push the group toward a lower sale price, not a higher one.

- Your NPS is down 25 points (+31 to +6) and churn is rising. That is the number a buyer's diligence team will look at first.
- All three moves add disruption at the moment customers are already leaving.
- The recommendation is a different plan with the same goal, which is higher profit and a higher multiple at exit. It does three things:
  1. It stops the churn first.
  2. It sequences the rebrand and the restructure, instead of stacking both on the same customers in Q1.
  3. It replaces the blanket ban on contractors and juniors with a capacity decision based on data.

Confidence: high on the direction, medium on the details. I have not seen your financials, customer data or contracts, so every number below that is not yours is marked as an assumption.

## What the board is really deciding

The owners want profit up and a clean sale in about 18 months. That has three consequences.

1. **Buyers pay for earnings quality, not just earnings.** [medium]
   - For a software and services group, a buyer will test recurring revenue, net revenue retention, churn by cohort, and customer concentration.
   - EBITDA that comes from cutting delivery capacity while churn is rising is read as a one-off, and it is often discounted or reversed in the price.
2. **The real window is about 9 to 12 months.** [medium]
   - Sale preparation, vendor due diligence and the trailing-twelve-month numbers a buyer sees all start well before signing.
   - Anything you start in Q1 must show results in the numbers by roughly Q3 to Q4, or it counts against you.
3. **The group is not yet one company, and that is a risk and an opportunity.** [verified]
   - Four acquired companies in two years is a story of integration, and a buyer pays for integration that works.
   - It is also a story of integration that has not finished yet, and an unfinished integration discounts the price.

### Rebrand

- [verified] You have four brands and a NPS that has fallen sharply. We do not yet know whether the brand is the cause or whether the cause is service, product or integration problems. Check this before anyone spends money.
- [medium] A rebrand fixes none of the usual causes of falling NPS, such as slow support, product gaps between the four platforms, or account managers who left.
- [medium] Companies that rebrand during a customer-trust problem often see a short-term drop in recognition, in search and referral traffic, and in renewal conversations.
- What I would do instead:
  - Do the invisible part in Q1: legal entity naming review, trademark and domain clearance for the new name, one design system, and one set of contract templates. This is cheap and reversible.
  - Move to an endorsed model first, for example "[New name] | formerly [Old brand]", and keep the old brand visible to customers for at least 12 months.
  - Make the visible switch for each company only after that company's NPS and churn have stabilised, and have the account managers brief the key accounts personally before it happens.
  - Present to the board a clear exit story: "one group brand, live before the sale process starts". That does not require every customer to see it in Q1.

### Fourth restructure

- [verified] This would be the fourth restructure in about two years.
- [medium] Restructure fatigue is a known cause of regretted attrition, and the people who leave are the ones with the best market options.
- [medium] Removing management layers can be right, since four acquisitions usually leave duplicate layers. But the way to find out is to look at spans and decisions, not to pick a number of layers to remove.
- What I would do instead:
  - Design it once, from the customer journey backwards: where does a customer problem currently wait for a decision?
  - Define the target spans of control and the number of layers, then announce the full design once, with a promise that there will be no further structural change before the sale. Only make that promise if you can keep it.
  - Run the restructure after the first retention results, not during the brand change, so that customers and staff are not hit by both together.
  - Protect the customer-facing roles first: account management, support, implementation, and the people who hold key relationships.

### Ending all contractor and junior roles

This is the move I would argue against hardest.

- [medium] Juniors are cheap, and they are the future supply of mid-level engineers and consultants. Ending junior hiring saves little now and costs you in 2 to 3 years. A buyer will see that as a missing pipeline.
- [medium] Contractors are your flexible capacity. Removing all of them removes the ability to absorb peaks or a customer escalation, which is exactly what you need while churn is rising.
- [medium] Some contractors will hold knowledge of the four old platforms that nobody else has. Check this before any notice is given.
- Germany-specific points to check with employment counsel before any announcement [medium]:
  - **Works council.** If there is a Betriebsrat, a restructure that changes the organisation or removes roles generally triggers information and consultation duties, and often negotiation of an Interessenausgleich and Sozialplan. This takes months and must be in the timeline.
  - **Collective redundancy thresholds.** The notification duties under the Kündigungsschutzgesetz depend on headcount and the number of dismissals in 30 days. Counsel should check them for each legal entity, since the four companies may still be separate employers.
  - **Social selection.** For permanent employees, dismissals need a Sozialauswahl. That can force out younger and cheaper staff and keep older, more expensive staff, which is the opposite of what the plan intends.
  - **Contractors.** Ending contracts is simple. But if some contractors have been working like employees, there may also be a misclassification question that deserves a legal review, and the review is easier to do now than in due diligence.
  - **Transferred employees.** If any units were merged or moved between legal entities, the rules on transfer of undertakings may also apply.
- What I would do instead:
  - Replace the blanket ban with a capacity plan by team: which roles are needed to run the contracts, which are not, and what each costs.
  - Keep a small, deliberate junior intake. It is cheap and it protects the pipeline.
  - Convert long-running, business-critical contractors to permanent roles where it makes sense. This lowers risk and often lowers cost.
  - Release contractor capacity only where the work has genuinely ended.

## Proposed plan

### Phase 0: now to day 30, diagnose before acting

1. **Churn diagnosis.** Look at churn and NPS by company, product, segment, customer size, tenure, and account manager. Read the verbatim NPS comments and call the 20 biggest accounts that are at risk. Establish whether the cause is service, product, price, integration, or people leaving.
2. **Revenue at risk.** List all contracts that renew in the next 18 months, with value, health score and the executive who owns the relationship.
3. **Cost baseline.** Build one view of cost across the four companies: duplicate systems, duplicate roles, overlapping vendors and real estate. The first savings should come from here, not from people who serve customers.
4. **Legal and works council check.** Employment counsel confirms thresholds, consultation duties and timing for each legal entity.
5. **Talent risk map.** Name the 30 to 50 people whose loss would hurt customers or the sale, and have a retention conversation with each.

### Phase 1: months 2 to 6, stop the leak and find quick, safe savings

- A customer recovery programme with named executive sponsors for the top accounts, fixed response times, and a visible fix list.
- Non-people savings: vendor and tool consolidation, cloud and licence costs, office space, duplicated back-office systems, and procurement across four companies.
- Pricing and contract review: renewal terms, indexation clauses, and discounts that were given during the acquisitions.
- Rebrand groundwork (invisible): trademark clearance, design system, templates, brand architecture decision, and the plan for each company.
- Restructure design finished and tested with the works council, with a single announcement date.

### Phase 2: months 6 to 12, execute once and show proof

- Announce and execute the one restructure, with consultation completed, severance and Sozialplan agreed, and a clear statement that it is the last.
- Start the visible brand change company by company, beginning with the company whose NPS has recovered most.
- Targeted capacity changes based on the Phase 0 data, not a blanket rule.
- Monthly retention and NPS reporting to the board.

### Phase 3: months 12 to 18, sale readiness

- Build the numbers a buyer will test: cohort retention, net revenue retention, pipeline, backlog and a clean EBITDA bridge with the one-off costs shown separately.
- Vendor due diligence on legal, employment and contractor status, IP and customer contracts.
- A clear one-group story: one brand, one platform direction, one operating model, and evidence that churn has turned.
