Recommend a staged rollout, not "all 140 stores live by March": sign for March, go live in about 10 stores in February or March, and expand in waves only when the pilot beats the status quo on sales and availability as well as hours. The 12% is the vendor's claim, not your number yet. The three-day publication window conflicts with Dutch on-call rules as I read them, so it has to change before any store goes live.

I'm assuming a listed or privately owned Dutch grocer with a works council (OR) and a supermarket collective agreement (CAO). Check both before anything else. I am not giving legal advice.

**Why not March for everyone**

1. **Law and consultation.** With 9,000 staff you will have an OR. A system able to monitor staff or performance needs its consent (art. 27(1)(l) WOR). Working-time and roster rules are likely to need it too, which counsel should confirm. Consent takes months and cannot be compressed to fit a vendor date. Check the CAO's roster rules as well.
2. **Three days is too short for on-call workers.** Since 2020, on-call workers must be called at least four days ahead, or they may refuse. A CAO can shorten that to no less than 24 hours. A shift cancelled or changed inside the window is still paid. Same-day adjustment of flexible staff therefore costs money unless the CAO says otherwise. Ask the vendor to show how its savings model handles this. If it ignores it, the 12% is overstated.
3. **The AI Act.** AI that allocates tasks or monitors workers is high-risk. The duty to inform representatives and workers applies from 2 December 2027, after the Digital Omnibus delay. Put it in the contract now, with vendor documentation on data, bias testing and human oversight. A GDPR data protection impact assessment is needed regardless.
4. **The 12% lands on students.** Most of the cut will come out of flexible contracts. Fifteen-minute slots, a three-day horizon and day-of changes mean shorter shifts, split shifts and less control over exams and second jobs. That predicts turnover. The one randomised trial I know of, 28 Gap stores, found that more predictable schedules raised median sales by 7% and labour productivity by 5%. Churn costs real money: recruiting, training, and the lost knowledge of who knows the fresh department.
5. **Forecasts are the weak link.** A tool that matches hours to forecast demand also drops hours when the forecast is wrong. Under-staffed stores show up as empty shelves, queues and lost sales, not on the labour line. A 15-minute match also removes the slack that good-jobs retailers deliberately keep.

**What I'd put to your COO**

- **Now to January:** brief the OR and the unions on the problem and the numbers, before the contract is final. Back-test the tool on 12 months of your till and roster data, store by store. Ask the vendor for references from Dutch grocers with similar contracts and what they actually saw.
- **February to April:** pilot in about 10 stores across formats (big, small, urban, student-heavy, rural), with matched control stores. Start in advisory mode: the tool proposes, the store manager decides and can override. Run at least 8 to 10 weeks, long enough to see rosters, swaps and exam periods.
- **Go/no-go for wave 2:** agree the indicators before the pilot, per store, never per person: hours vs forecast, sales and on-shelf availability, queue times, shrink, staff turnover, absence, swap requests, and a short pulse survey. Hours saved counts only if the others hold.
- **Waves after that:** roughly 30 stores at a time, with the pilot results shared with the OR before each. Realistically full coverage is autumn, not March.
- **Contract:** price per wave, an exit clause if the pilot misses, data ownership and no use of your staff data to train other customers' models, and no individual performance scoring.

**Guardrails to agree with the OR (my starting suggestion)**

- Publish at least four days ahead for on-call staff, and aim for a week for everyone else.
- Minimum shift length (for example three hours) and no split shifts.
- Staff availability and exam periods are inputs the tool must respect, with a simple swap function.
- Same-day changes are an offer staff can decline, with a premium, never an order.
- Store managers keep the final call. Reporting is by store and team, with no rankings of individuals.

**Where the hours should come from**

Aim the 12% at the schedule's waste first: overstaffing in quiet slots, mismatched peaks, and hours moved to where the customers are. Student turnover is high, so a lot of the reduction can come from not refilling every leaver rather than cutting anyone's hours. Dismissals are not part of this plan, and I'd tell staff so up front. Treat 12% as a hypothesis the pilot tests. If it gives 6% with better availability, that is still a good outcome.

**The other seats' views**

- **CFO:** the cost of waiting is the saving you delay, which is at most one twelfth of the annual benefit for each month. The cost of a failed national go-live is lost sales, turnover and a damaged relationship with the OR. Ask finance to price both with your own labour cost.
- **COO and CEO:** this is a roster change for 9,000 people, and the way you introduce it is what staff will remember about the company.
- **Product and customers:** availability and queue times are the customer's view of whether this worked.

**What I don't know**

Whether your OR has seen this yet, what your CAO says about rosters, how many of your 9,000 are on-call versus fixed-hours contracts, and what the vendor's savings model assumes. Each could move this plan.

**Assumptions that would change my mind:** pilot data showing equal or better availability with a saving above 8%, a vendor who can document Dutch references and legal fit, and an OR that supports a faster timetable.
