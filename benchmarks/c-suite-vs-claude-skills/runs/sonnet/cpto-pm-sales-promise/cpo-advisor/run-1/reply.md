## Bottom Line

Don't push back on the promise. Take the CEO a decision about it. Show him what delivering three features by end of Q1 costs in roadmap terms, and let him pick. Your team is at capacity, so "yes, on top of everything" can't happen. A CEO who sees the trade-off will usually pick a trade-off. A CEO who only hears "we can't" hears a PM defending a roadmap.

Confidence: medium. I don't know your CEO, your customer or your contract, and those change which option fits best. The method holds up regardless.

## What to do (in order)

**1. Find out what was actually promised (this week, before you talk to the CEO).**
- Ask the CEO or the account owner what exactly the customer heard. Get the words, in writing if it exists: email, call notes, order form.
- Separate three things: the problem the customer wants solved, the feature as they described it, and the date. "Three features by end of Q1" is often "we hear you and will address X, Y and Z soon." It is rarely a contractual deadline on three specific builds.
- Ask whether it is in a signed contract or renewal term. That decides whether this is a commitment or an expectation. [assumed: you haven't said, so check it]

**2. Do a quick sizing with engineering (2-3 days, rough ranges, not estimates carved in stone).**
- For each feature: what the customer's underlying need is, a rough size range (small, medium, large), dependencies, and the smallest version that solves their problem.
- Capacity: what is committed for Q1, and what happens to each item if people move. Name the specific roadmap items that slip, with their business reason, and not "other stuff."
- Get the engineering lead into this. You want "we looked at it together," not "product says no."

**3. Check the customer's real stakes.**
- How much revenue, what renewal date, and what happens if one of the three is late or smaller?
- Talk to the customer, or sit in on a call with the account owner. Ask what they need first and what would make them stop worrying. Customers often rank the three features very differently from how the promise was worded, and one of them is often the one that matters.
- This is the step that most often shrinks the problem. It also protects you: you're acting on customer evidence, not on your team's convenience.

**4. Go to the CEO with options and a recommendation.** Roughly:

| Option | What ships by end of Q1 | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| A. Deliver all three | All three, full scope | These named roadmap items slip to Q2 or later. Risk to X |
| B. Phase it | Feature 1 fully, smallest versions of 2 and 3 | Smaller roadmap slip. Customer must accept a phased delivery |
| C. Re-sequence with the customer | The one or two that matter most, others dated honestly in Q2 | Least disruption. CEO or account owner has one hard conversation |
| D. Add capacity | Closest to full scope | Needs budget, hiring or contractors, and onboarding time. Rarely lands in one quarter |

Lead with the one you recommend and say why. Make the cost of each option concrete (what slips, which revenue or customers that affects). Then ask him which he wants.

**5. Use language that keeps the relationship intact.**
- Start from his goal: "I want this customer to be delighted and renew. Here's how I think we get there."
- State the constraint as fact, not complaint: "The team is fully committed through Q1. For these three to ship, these items move."
- Avoid: "You shouldn't have promised that." "We can't." "Next time ask us first."
- Example opening: "I've looked at what it takes to deliver the three features for [customer] with engineering. We can do it, but not on top of the current plan. I've got three ways to do it and a recommendation. Can I walk you through them?"

**6. Deal with the "without asking us" part separately, later, and lightly.**
- Not in the same conversation as the trade-off. Raising it while he is under pressure turns the discussion into blame.
- A few weeks after this is settled, propose a lightweight process: "Before we commit dates to customers, can sales or you send me a quick message, and I'll get you a read within 48 hours?" Frame it as speed and protection for him, so he is never caught promising something the team can't meet.
- A short "customer commitments" log (what was promised, to whom, by when, status) also helps. It makes these requests visible and gets them into planning.

## Why this approach

- The CEO has already made the promise. Backing out of it costs him credibility with the biggest customer. Giving him options lets him keep his word, change it with dignity, or fund it. [verified: this is standard practice for sales-driven roadmap pressure]
- Saying "no" to a CEO rarely works. Showing the trade-off usually does, because he then decides with the real costs in front of him, and it was his call to make. [medium]
- Features requested by the biggest customer are often real signal, but they can also be that one customer's workaround. Checking the underlying need guards against building three things for one account at the expense of everyone else. The strategic question to ask him quietly is whether these three features serve the wider customer base or just this account. [medium]
- Taking on all three "on top" with a team at capacity usually means all of Q1's work slips unpredictably, or quality drops, or people burn out. An explicit, chosen slip is better than an uncontrolled one. [verified: general delivery risk, not specific to your team]
