Don't say no, and don't absorb it quietly. Talk to the CEO privately this week, before anyone else hears about it, and ask him to choose what moves instead of telling him the promise is impossible. That protects the relationship better than quiet overtime or a public "we can't."

**Why this works.** He made a commitment to your biggest customer and is probably worried about that customer, not trying to override you. "On top of the roadmap" only fits with overtime, which is a wrong plan, not a motivated team. A clear trade-off lets him stay the decision-maker.

**Before you meet him (a day or two):**
1. **Get the exact promise.** Was it a date or an intention? "Three features" often turns out to be three outcomes the customer needs, and there may be cheaper ways to deliver them.
2. **Get rough sizes from engineering.** T-shirt sizes, not commitments, based on real capacity after support, bugs and debt.
3. **Check the numbers.** What share of revenue is this customer? Would other customers use these features? Which roadmap items would slip, and what do they affect?

**Bring options, not an objection:**
- **A. Keep all three, drop something.** Show what moves out and what that costs.
- **B. Deliver what matters by end of Q1.** One feature fully, or all three in smaller form, as a pilot with the customer. The rest follows in Q2.
- **C. Add capacity.** A contractor or a paid-services engagement for this customer. Slower to start and not free, but honest.

Recommend one (usually B) with your reasoning and what would change your mind.

**A line you can use:**
"I want to make this work for [customer]. I've looked at what the team can take on, and I don't want to promise something we then miss. Here are three ways to get them what they need. I'd like your call, because each one changes something else."

**Going forward**, ask for one habit and frame it as protecting him: "Before we give the customer a date, give me two days to get an estimate." Sales and the CEO can promise discovery and a date for an answer; the team that builds it commits to the delivery date.

**Keep it clean.** Raise it with him first, in person. Don't vent to the team or sales. Afterward, send a short neutral summary of what you agreed.

**Assumptions:** he is open to trade-offs, and the customer need can be met more than one way. If he insists on all three with nothing moved, put the cost in writing ("X slips, and the team works overtime") and ask your manager or the leadership team to decide openly.
