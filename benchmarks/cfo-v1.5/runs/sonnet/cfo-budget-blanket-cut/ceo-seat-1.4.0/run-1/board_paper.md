# Board paper: Next year's opex, an 8% reduction

**Decision needed from the board:** approve the approach and the envelope, not a list of measures · **Prepared by:** CFO, with the CEO · **Status:** draft for CFO review. Figures marked (est.) are placeholders until finance, HR and delivery data replace them.

## 1. The problem in one sentence

The board wants opex 8% lower next year; we are profitable but on a thin margin, 68% of opex is payroll, and the way we get to 8% decides whether we protect that margin or erode the revenue that produces it.

Leadership owns the result. A thin margin is a management outcome, not something employees caused, so the plan starts with leadership's own pay and spending (section 5).

If the 8% is measured against this year's actual spend, we must also absorb next year's wage indexation (est. 3 to 4%; check the CAO and our own pay policy), so the real reduction in underlying activity is closer to 11 to 12%. This one question changes the whole paper.

## 3. Why not an equal 8% per cost centre

The case for it is real: it is simple, looks fair, is quick to run and is hard to argue with. Its problems:

- **Cost centres are not equal.** A cost centre that delivers billable work produces revenue. Cutting 8% of delivery capacity cuts revenue too, and with a thin margin each euro of lost revenue can cost more than the euro of cost saved. Cutting overhead 8% does not.
- **It rewards padded budgets and punishes lean teams.** A team that already runs tight has to cut into capability; a team with slack just trims the slack.
- **Mathematically it lands mostly on people.** Applied equally, it removes 5.44 points of opex from payroll and 2.56 from non-payroll (opex = 100), the equivalent of about 48 of 600 FTE if done through exits (est.). If payroll were protected entirely, non-payroll spend would have to fall 25%, which is not credible either.
- **It is mostly a one-way door.** Rebuilding lost skills, client relationships and engagement costs more and takes longer than the saving took to make.

## 4. Options

All effects are shares of this year's opex (opex = 100), so 1 point = 1% of opex. Ranges are estimates until week 1 data is in.

| Option | In-year effect (points) | Time to effect | People affected | Hidden costs | Reversible? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do nothing | 0 | n/a | None | Board expectation missed; margin exposed | Yes |
| A. Equal 8% per cost centre | up to 8.0 on paper; less in year one (notice periods, consultation, transition payments) | 6 to 12 months for payroll | Everyone, unevenly | Revenue loss in delivery, lost knowledge, leavers we want to keep | No |
| B. Targeted plan, in order (below) | 5.2 to 7.9 in year one; higher run-rate | Starts immediately | Mostly through attrition and spend | Management time; needs data discipline | Mostly yes |
| C. B plus involuntary reductions | B plus up to about 2 | 9 to 12 months | Named roles | Severance and transition payments, works council and UWV process, survivor attrition | No |

**Option B, built bottom-up (est.):**

| Step | Low | High | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Non-payroll spend: vendors, licences, travel, office, freelancers and subcontractors (32 points; about 8 to 11% of it) | 2.5 | 3.5 | Line-by-line review, not a flat percentage |
| Stop or pause low-value work and projects | 1.0 | 1.5 | Each stopped item named with its owner |
| Hiring freeze with targeted backfill: let net headcount fall 3 to 4% through attrition, protecting billable delivery | 1.5 | 2.5 | Full-year effect is 2.0 to 2.7 points; roughly half lands in year one |
| Leadership pay and bonus reduction, perks first | 0.2 | 0.4 | Mostly a signal of trust, not a saving |
| **Total in-year** | **5.2** | **7.9** | |

Honest reading: Option B probably gets us to 5 to 8 points in year one and to the full 8 on a run-rate basis. The remaining gap, up to 2.8 points in year one, is the real decision for the board. Closing it in year one would require involuntary reductions, which we do not propose now.

Not counted towards the 8%, but worth the board's attention: indexing rates on contracts due for renewal and fixing under-priced work. Opex is only half of the margin equation. If the true aim is margin protection, a combined target (opex plus pricing) may reach it with less harm; we ask the board to confirm.

## 5. Recommendation

Approve an 8% opex reduction on a run-rate basis by the end of next year, reached through Option B, with these safeguards:

1. **Leaders go first.** Executive team pay is frozen, executive bonuses are cut before any employee measure, and executive perks and travel are reviewed first. This is announced before anything else.
2. **Protect delivery capacity.** No reduction is applied to billable teams without a utilisation and pipeline check. Overhead and spend carry the first share.
3. **Quarterly gates.** At the end of Q1 and Q2, finance reports savings against plan. If we are behind, the next steps are, in this order: temporary reduced hours (leaders included, where the law and our CAO allow it), a voluntary exit programme, and only then involuntary reductions, brought back to the board with full numbers.
4. **Bottom-up budgets.** Each cost centre owner proposes where their savings come from against the same total, and defends it in a review with the CFO. Equal shares are the default only where no better data exists.

## 6. Human impact

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who is affected? | Option B: mainly through unfilled vacancies and spend. Leavers are not backfilled by default, so remaining teams take on more. |
| Who is affected disproportionately? | Teams with the highest attrition (they lose the most headcount by default); juniors, if freezes stop all entry-level hiring. We will protect a minimum intake of graduates and trainees. |
| What do the people who stay experience? | More work per person, uncertainty. We track workload, absence and regretted attrition monthly and act if they move. |
| What happens to customers? | Delivery capacity protected; we monitor utilisation, delivery quality and client satisfaction. |
| What legal and consultation steps apply? | See section 7. To be confirmed with employment counsel. |
| How will we know it is going badly? | Regretted attrition above baseline, rising absence, falling utilisation or client satisfaction, savings behind plan at a gate. |

## 7. Process and legal floor (not legal advice; confirm with counsel)

- A works council is mandatory from 50 employees, so we should have one (confirm). A major change to how we organise or budget the workforce gives it an advisory right (article 25 WOR). We brief it in week 1, with the real numbers, and request advice before any workforce measure is final. The board should approve the approach and the envelope, and leave the specific workforce measures to follow the works council's advice.
- A hiring freeze and spend cuts are mostly outside this, but any change to pay, bonus, or working hours, or monitoring tools used to track savings, may need its consent or involvement. Check.
- If involuntary reductions ever come back to the board: 20 or more dismissals in one UWV region within three months triggers notification and consultation of UWV and the unions (WMCO); statutory transition payments and notice periods apply. First-year savings are then well below the annual figure because those costs come first. Check the CAO for any additional rules.

## 9. Assumptions, and what would change our mind

- Assumption: attrition is high enough (est. 10 to 15%) to deliver a 3 to 4% headcount reduction without exits. If actual attrition is well below that, Option B delivers less and the board must decide on voluntary exits earlier.
- Assumption: non-payroll spend has 8 to 11% of fat. A line-by-line review may show more or less.
- Would change our mind towards a deeper cut: a covenant, or a cash position that is weaker than the margin suggests.
- Would change our mind towards a smaller cut or a different lever: evidence that revenue or pricing, not cost, is the real problem.
