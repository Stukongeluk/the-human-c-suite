My recommendation is to take the 8% target to the board, but not to deliver it as a flat 8% per cost centre. A uniform cut is simple to announce. It is not simple to execute, and with a thin margin it is the version most likely to cost you revenue.

## Why the blanket cut doesn't hold up

**1. It is mostly a headcount decision, whatever it is called.**
Payroll is 68% of opex. A flat cut therefore takes about 5.4 points of the 8% out of payroll. Non-payroll spend (32%) has to find the rest.

- If you tried to hit 8% by cutting non-payroll only, you would need roughly 25% out of it (8 / 32). That is not realistic, because rent, software licences and insurance are largely contractual.
- So in practice about 8% of payroll has to go. On 600 people that is roughly 48 FTE-equivalents. Your cost centres will see that very quickly even if the CEO's message does not mention it.

**2. In a B2B services company, cost centres are not equivalent.**
Billable delivery teams and sales generate the revenue. Support functions do not.

- An 8% cut in delivery capacity usually becomes an 8% or larger cut in billable hours.
- With a thin margin, a revenue loss of that size can wipe out the saving.
- A flat cut also rewards the cost centres that padded their budgets and penalises the ones that already run lean.

**3. Reducing payroll in the Netherlands is slow and expensive.**
Please confirm the points below with employment counsel. I am giving you the planning implications, not legal advice.

- Dismissal for business reasons goes through UWV (redundancy permit) and follows the afspiegelingsbeginsel (proportional selection by age group). It is not a free choice of who goes.
- A reduction of 20 or more people in one UWV region within three months triggers the collective redundancy notification rules (WMCO). That includes consulting the unions.
- With more than 50 employees you have a works council, and a reorganisation requires its advice (WOR art. 25) before the decision is made. That process alone can take longer than your three weeks.
- Transition payments, notice periods, and any collective agreement (CAO) or social plan obligations create one-off costs. These land in the same year as the savings you are trying to book.
- Your savings will not be a full-year 8% unless the measures start on 1 January. Phasing matters, and the board should see it.

**4. The baseline is undefined.**
Next year's payroll will rise for CAO or market-driven wage indexation, annual increases, and employer contribution changes, before any cut.

- Ask the board directly whether "8% lower" means 8% below this year's actual opex or 8% below next year's baseline.
- On a baseline that includes inflation, the real reduction you need may be closer to 10 to 12% of today's spend. Agree the definition first, in writing.

## What I would bring to the board instead

I would keep the CEO's principle, which is a single clear target, and change how it is allocated. The proposal is one company-wide number with three tracks.

**Track 1: Non-payroll (32% of opex). Target about 10 to 12% savings, owned by finance and procurement.**

- Vendors, software licences and SaaS consolidation, subcontractors and freelancers, travel, office space, marketing programmes, and training.
- Start with zero-based review of the top 20 vendors, because spend is usually concentrated there.
- Savings here are fastest, reversible, and do not touch the revenue engine.
- 10 to 12% of 32% is roughly 3.2 to 3.8 points of the 8%.

**Track 2: Payroll (68% of opex). Target about 6 to 7% of payroll, achieved mostly without redundancy.**

- Hiring freeze or selective approval for non-revenue roles, and not replacing leavers where capacity allows.
- Natural attrition (your actual turnover rate is the key input here; please pull it from HR).
- Reduce reliance on contractors and overtime, and review variable pay and bonus design.
- Temporary measures such as hours reduction on a voluntary basis, if the works council supports them.
- Redundancies only as a last resort, and targeted by function, not spread across all.
- 6 to 7% of 68% is roughly 4.1 to 4.8 points.

**Track 3: Revenue protection and ring-fencing.**

- Name the cost centres that are protected or partly protected (typically billable delivery and sales). They are not cut, or are cut by less.
- Cost centres with low returns or overlap (internal tooling, duplicated support roles, low-utilisation areas) take more than 8%.
- Make the rule explicit: every cut is tested against its effect on billable capacity and on retention of key staff.

The illustrative arithmetic is: 3.2 to 3.8 from Track 1, plus 4.1 to 4.8 from Track 2, which gives roughly 7.3 to 8.6 points. That brackets 8%. ## What to do in the three weeks

**Week 1: Facts**

- Get the full opex split by cost centre and by nature (payroll, contractors, vendors, facilities, other), and the fixed versus discretionary share of each.
- Pull HR data: headcount by function, attrition by function over 24 months, open vacancies, contractor and freelancer spend, and billable utilisation.
- Confirm the baseline definition with the CEO and board chair.
- Ask legal or HR to confirm the works council timeline and the redundancy cost assumptions.

**Week 2: Build**

- Build a bottom-up proposal per cost centre, with each head of function proposing savings, and test it against the targets above.
- Model revenue and margin impact for the delivery and sales functions, using utilisation data.
- Calculate phasing: savings by quarter, one-off costs (transition payments, any restructuring costs), and net in-year effect.
- Prepare three scenarios: the CEO's flat 8%, the three-track approach, and a downside case with revenue 3 to 5% lower than plan.

**Week 3: Board pack and alignment**

- Pre-wire the CEO before the board sees anything. Show the comparison on net margin, not just on opex, so the discussion stays factual.
- Pre-wire the board chair and any audit committee members.
- Finalise the pack (see below) and rehearse the questions you are likely to get.

## The board pack (suggested structure)

1. The ask and the baseline (what 8% means in euros, and against what).
2. Where the money is: opex by nature and by cost centre.
3. Option A, flat 8%: what it requires in headcount, what it risks in revenue, and the legal timeline.
4. Option B, three-track plan: savings by track, the ring-fenced areas, and the net in-year effect including one-off costs.
5. Phasing and risk: savings by quarter, and the main risks (attrition of good people, revenue loss, works council delay).
6. Decisions needed from the board, and the governance for quarterly tracking.

## One thing to settle before you present

Ask the CEO and the board what problem the 8% is solving. If it is margin, a combination of cost and revenue or pricing levers may do better than cost alone. If it is cash or covenant compliance, the timing of savings matters more than the annual total. The answer changes which scenario you should recommend.
