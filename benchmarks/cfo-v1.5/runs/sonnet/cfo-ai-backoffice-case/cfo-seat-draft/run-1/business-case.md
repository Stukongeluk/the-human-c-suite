# Board paper: AI in finance and back-office, what it can save and how fast

Status: draft for the CFO. All figures in SEK million and all are **estimates** built on placeholder assumptions (listed in section 6); none is taken from our ledger, payroll or vendor quotes yet. Replace them before this goes to the board. Not legal, tax or investment advice.

## 1. Recommendation

Approve an AI programme for accounts payable, reconciliations and reporting with a **target of 30% (27 FTE) lower finance and back-office capacity by the end of year 3, reached through a hiring freeze, natural attrition, redeployment and voluntary exits, and gated on measured results.** Do not approve 27 compulsory redundancies in year 1: on our own estimates it loses money over 24 months (-6.5 vs -4.1 for the staged path, including the AI programme's costs), the automation will not yet cover the work, and it is the option hardest to reverse.

## 2. The problem behind the 30%

"30% fewer people next year" is a solution. The underlying question is: what cost, speed and control level do we want from finance and back-office, and by when? Today the 90 people cost about SEK 72m a year fully loaded (est. SEK 0.8m each). Thirty percent is 27 people, SEK 21.6m a year gross. That is real money, and it is worth pursuing. The disagreement is about pace and method, not direction.

What this says, plainly:
- **Neither option pays back inside 12 months, and the AI programme itself is cost-negative in year 1.** Any board slide showing a year-1 net saving of SEK 21.6m is wrong; at best the 21.6 is a year-2-onwards run-rate, before AI running costs of about 5.1.
- **At full scale the prize is real:** 27 fewer FTE is SEK 21.6m gross, about SEK 16.5m a year after AI run costs.
- **B has the higher year-2 figure (7.9 vs 2.7)** if everything goes right. It has the worse 24-month total, and its year 2 only works if the automation catches up with the cuts. With half the assumed capacity released, B's year-2 net falls to 3.7; C's is unchanged at 2.7 because it only releases what the automation has already freed.
- **Rough break-even for B:** about 6 extra regretted leavers plus a 10-FTE gap already uses up more than a third of its year-2 gross saving. A missed close or a late supervisory filing would cost more than either. I have not priced that; see risks.

## 5. How the staged path gets to 30%

1. Hiring freeze in finance and back-office with a short, named exception list (controls, reporting, regulatory roles).
2. Natural attrition: at about 10% a year that is about 9 people a year, about 27 over three years, which is the 30% on its own if the capacity is there to absorb it.
3. Redeploy and reskill: move people from AP and reconciliation into exception handling, controls, data quality and analytics, with paid training.
4. Voluntary exits (for example early retirement or negotiated leaving packages) for those who want them; roughly 5 in year 2 in the model.
5. Compulsory redundancy only if the gates below are met and the rest cannot close the gap. Then once, decently, with support from the day of notice.

Gates, reviewed quarterly by the CFO and the audit committee: measured touchless rate per process, error and rework rate against baseline, month-end close days, audit and supervisory findings, regretted attrition. A gate that is missed pauses further headcount release; it does not bring forward cuts.

## 8. Legal, regulatory and control points (to be confirmed by counsel, auditors and the compliance function)

- **MBL:** negotiation with the unions before a major change, and before any decision to dismiss. Informing the unions late costs time and trust.
- **LAS:** selection for redundancy follows length of service within each operational unit and collective agreement area, not job role or performance; each employer may exempt three people. We cannot pick the people who are best at working with the new tools, and the most recently hired, often the most digital, leave first. Notice runs one to six months by service.
- **Notification to the Public Employment Service:** 26-100 people requires at least four months' notice before the dismissals (2 months for 5-25). I would not suggest splitting a reduction into smaller batches to shorten this; it is likely to be read as avoidance, so check with counsel.
- **Job security council (TRR for white-collar private sector):** support starts from the day of notice; include its role and cost in the plan.
- **Insurer-specific:** Finansinspektionen's expectations on governance, outsourcing and operational resilience, DORA obligations for ICT third-party risk (AI vendors included), Solvency II and IFRS 17 reporting deadlines, GDPR, and the EU AI Act where AI affects staff decisions. Segregation of duties, audit trails and a named human approver for anything that moves money or affects people. Brief external audit before design, not after.
- **Base rates:** one widely cited study reported about 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots with no measurable P&L impact (method criticised, so a direction only), and Gartner expects over 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by end 2027 on cost, unclear value or weak controls. Plan for a project that disappoints.

## 9. Leaders first

If the programme is justified by savings, leadership variable pay should be tied to a balanced scorecard (close quality, audit and supervisory findings, retention and customer service) over more than one year, with no bonus for headcount reduction as such, and with leaders' pay the first to be cut if the programme under-delivers.

## 10. What would change this recommendation

- A pilot showing touchless rates well above the base case (then release capacity faster, still through attrition and redeployment first).
- A funding or solvency constraint that makes year 1 cash decisive (then I would look at working capital, vendor and licence spend, and pricing before payroll).
- Attrition much lower than 10% (then voluntary exits carry more of the load, and the timeline stretches).
