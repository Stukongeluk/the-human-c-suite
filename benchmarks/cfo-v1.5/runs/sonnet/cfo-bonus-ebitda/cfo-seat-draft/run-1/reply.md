I'd draft the plan with EBITDA as the largest weight (50%) but not 100%, and with restructuring costs counted in the bonus number, not excluded. The full draft is in `plan.md`; the short version is below.

**Why not the plan as briefed.** You've restructured three years running, so restructuring is a recurring cost of running this business, not a one-off. A bonus on EBITDA that excludes it pays managers to restructure, because the savings count and the costs don't. A bonus on EBITDA alone also pays for cutting training, security and maintenance this year and leaves the bill to the next owner. In healthcare software that is a real risk: a buyer's due diligence will look at churn, regretted attrition and security findings, and those are what a pure EBITDA plan puts under pressure. (If you were SEC-registered, the non-GAAP rules would bar this exclusion outright; as a private company it is good practice, not law.)

**What I'd propose**

| Measure | Weight |
|---|---|
| Bonus EBITDA (adjusted EBITDA after restructuring costs) | 50% |
| Cash conversion | 15% |
| Net revenue retention / customer churn | 15% |
| Regretted attrition and engagement | 10% |
| Quality and safety (uptime, security incidents, open compliance findings) | 10% |

- Threshold, target and stretch pay 50%, 100% and 150% (cap), at 90%, 100% and 110% of budget. These are placeholders until I have the 2027 budget.
- **The board still gets its adjusted EBITDA** for lenders and the exit story, shown next to reported EBITDA with every adjustment named. It just isn't the bonus number. If the board won't go all the way, the fallback is a fixed, pre-agreed allowance for genuinely single events (placeholder 2% of budget EBITDA), or deducting the three-year average restructuring charge. Anything above that counts.
- Savings count only net of their full cost (severance, notice, rehiring, lost revenue).
- 30% of the executive team's bonus is deferred over 2 to 3 years, with malus and clawback for restatements or results bought at the long term's expense.
- No bonus is paid for headcount reduction as such. If EBITDA misses threshold, executive bonuses go first, before any involuntary reductions are considered.

**The steelman for the board.** A simple, single-metric plan is clear, hard to dispute and aligned with the exit multiple. Fair. The answer is that the multiple depends on retained recurring revenue and a stable team, so the other 50% protects the thing they're selling. Also, the plan is easier to defend to employees and to a works council if leaders carry the downside first.

**Two questions that would change the numbers**
1. What did the last three restructurings actually save net of their cost, and how many regretted exits or rehires followed? If they paid back, the board has a case for a capped allowance; if not, they are the argument for counting them.
2. What does your credit agreement define as EBITDA? Lender and bonus definitions can differ; they just have to be reconciled.

I've assumed: private company, roughly 25-40 plan participants, Dutch or EU employment law (works council consultation may apply, and counsel should review clawback enforceability). All figures are estimates and placeholders until I see the budget.
