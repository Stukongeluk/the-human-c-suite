**My recommendation: don't take the €300m buyback to the board as proposed, and don't use any buyback to reach this year's EPS guidance.** Put forward one capital allocation plan with three parts:

1. **Set a liquidity floor and protect it.** Size it on your seasonal cash low point, not on today's €400m.
2. **Fund the COO's €120m in stage-gated tranches**, but only once the business case holds up under scrutiny.
3. **Return the cash that is genuinely surplus**, which is probably €100–150m, through a buyback over 12–18 months. It starts *after* you have told the market about the guidance miss.

Pair it with an honest guidance update now. Missing by a few cents with a credible plan behind you is survivable. Hitting guidance by buying shares damages your credibility, and if you buy while holding inside information it is also a legal risk.

Here's the reasoning, so you can defend it.

---

### 1. The activist's plan and the COO's plan don't fit in €400m together

€300m + €120m = €420m. That's more than your net cash before any buffer. So the activist's proposal quietly decides the investment question too: it gets deferred, cut, or debt-funded.

Your real surplus is also smaller than €400m:

- **Seasonality.** A consumer electronics retailer's reported net cash is usually inflated by supplier payables at the period end. What matters is the intra-year low point, typically before peak season, plus a buffer for a bad year. That number, not the balance sheet figure, sets the size of any return.
- **Leases.** Under IFRS 16, store leases are debt in the eyes of lenders and rating agencies. "€400m net cash" may be net debt on their definition.

### 2. Buying back to hit guidance is the wrong reason, and it's risky

- **EPS accretion proves nothing.** The ECB deposit rate is [2.50%](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/stats/monetary/rates/html/index.en.html), so your cash earns roughly 2% after tax. At any plausible retail P/E, almost any buyback adds to EPS. A buyback creates value for the shareholders who stay only if you buy below intrinsic value. Otherwise it just transfers value between selling and remaining shareholders. If you believe the shares are cheap, say so and show the valuation. "It gets us to guidance" is not that argument.
- **The research describes exactly this case.** [Almeida, Fos & Kronlund (JFE 2016)](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2016/02/08/the-real-effects-of-share-repurchases) found that firms which would just miss EPS forecasts are much more likely to buy back shares. Over the following year, those firms cut capex by about 10%, employment by about 5% and R&D by about 3%. On average the market did not punish the buyback itself. Returns and later operating performance were weaker in the cases where the buyback came at the cost of investment, which is the trade the €300m version forces on you. The [Graham, Harvey & Rajgopal survey](https://www.fuqua.duke.edu/duke-fuqua-insights/graham-reform) found 55% of finance executives would delay a project to avoid missing an earnings target. The board should see that you're not doing that.
- **Inside information.** You know you'll miss guidance. That may well be inside information under MAR, and you would be buying shares from sellers who don't know it. Under the safe harbour you can't trade in closed periods or while delaying disclosure of inside information. The only exception is a time-scheduled programme or one run independently by a broker ([summary of the rules](https://finanstilsynet.no/en/supervision/market-conduct/buy-back)). Get counsel's view before anything starts. My strong expectation is that the answer is "disclose first, then buy".
- **Practicalities.** The safe harbour caps daily purchases at 25% of average daily volume. Work out how many trading days €300m takes. EPS uses weighted-average shares, so shares bought late in the year barely move this year's figure. Ask whether the "gets us to guidance" calculation assumes an accelerated buyback. Also check how much headroom is left in your AGM buyback authorisation.
- **Incentives.** If executive bonuses or LTIPs pay out on EPS, put that in the board paper yourself. Ask the remuneration committee to neutralise buyback effects. Better you raise it than the activist or a proxy adviser.

### 3. Fund the investment, but as a staged commitment, not a blank cheque

Products are a commodity in consumer electronics. What sets a retailer apart from online pure-plays and brands selling direct is advice, installation, repair, trade-in, subscriptions and an omnichannel experience that actually works. The COO's plan targets the thing that decides whether you are still relevant in five years. Best Buy's "Renew Blue" turnaround is the usual reference. It competed with Amazon by investing in price matching, ship-from-store, store partnerships with brands and its own staff, rather than leading with cuts.

The risks are real, though. Large IT projects run on average [45% over budget and deliver 56% less value than predicted](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/transform/our-insights/delivering-large-scale-it-projects-on-time-on-budget-and-on-value). So before the board meeting, ask the COO for:

- A business case with a return above WACC.
- A phasing plan and named KPIs: online conversion, service attach rate, service gross margin, NPS, staff retention.
- Explicit kill criteria.
- **Tranche 1** (around €40–50m): platform foundations plus a pilot of the service model in a set of stores. **Tranche 2** is released only when the pilot hits its metrics.

Be upfront about the accounting. Training costs are expensed under IAS 38. Configuration of a cloud (SaaS) platform is usually expensed too, following the 2021 IFRIC agenda decision. So this plan *lowers* EPS next year as well. That is the real short-term vs long-term trade-off, and the board should see it in numbers.

Don't let staff training become the soft line that gets trimmed. The service model only works if people can deliver it, and that is where the platform investment pays back.

### 4. The package (illustrative, so replace with your numbers)

| | €m |
|---|---|
| Net cash today | 400 |
| Less: seasonal low-point adjustment + liquidity buffer | (~150) |
| Less: investment programme, staged over ~2 years | (120) |
| **Surplus available to return** | **~130** |

That supports a **€100–150m buyback over 12–18 months**, run by an independent broker under a disclosed programme. Add two top-up rules. If tranche 2 isn't released, or if cash flow beats plan, the difference goes back to shareholders. Adopting a standing capital allocation framework ("cash above €X at the seasonal low is returned annually") gives the activist a lasting win rather than a one-off.

### 5. Guidance and the activist

- **Update guidance now**, in the same announcement as the capital allocation plan. Analysts will strip out a buyback-driven beat anyway. Consider moving future guidance to EBIT and free cash flow, which you can't hit by buying shares.
- **Meet the activist before the board decides.** Their point about idle cash is fair. Offer them a real return, a clear framework, and public KPIs on the investment with a promise to return more if milestones are missed. Sound out your top 10–15 holders as well. The board's duty is to the company and all its shareholders over the long term, and most long-only holders will back a plan that combines a meaningful return with disciplined investment.

### 6. Where this could be wrong

- **If the COO's case doesn't survive scrutiny**, don't fund it just to keep the peace. Return more cash instead.
- **If the shares trade well below a conservative intrinsic value and your low-point cash is high**, a bigger buyback could be right. It should be sized by surplus cash and value, never by the EPS gap.

### Before the board meeting, I'd nail down

1. The cash low point over the last three years, plus revolving credit facility headroom and covenants.
2. Net debt including leases, and how lenders and rating agencies see it.
3. An intrinsic value estimate against the share price.
4. The COO's case: return, phasing, split between P&L cost and capex, KPIs, kill criteria.
5. Legal sign-off: MAR inside information, closed periods, AGM authorisation, volume limits.
6. Any EPS-linked pay.
7. A downside scenario (say, sales down 10%), showing what each option does to liquidity.
