I've drafted the 2027 plan with EBITDA as the gate and the largest measure, but I'd take two changes back to the board: restructuring costs stay inside the EBITDA we pay on, and EBITDA carries 50% of the bonus (60% with cash conversion) instead of 100%. We've restructured three years running, so restructuring is a recurring cost. If we exclude it, a decision that lowers reported EBITDA can raise the bonus. In the draft's illustration, a mid-year restructuring cuts reported 2027 EBITDA by 0.5m and lifts the payout from 100% to 125% of target.

The draft is in `2027-management-bonus-plan-draft.md`. In short:

- **Scorecard:** Bonus EBITDA 50%, cash conversion 10%, gross revenue retention 15%, service quality and safety 15% (availability against SLA, Sev-1 incidents, security and compliance) and regretted attrition 10%. Threshold pays 50%, target 100%, and stretch pays 150%, which is also the cap.
- **EBITDA still decides:** below 95% of budget EBITDA, nothing pays. Below target, the other measures can't pay more than 100%.
- **Restructuring:** the 2027 budget carries a reorganisation line based on 2024–2026, and targets are set after it. A year that goes to plan pays the same as the board's version would. Overruns and unplanned restructurings count against the bonus. If the board wants management neutral on a restructuring it approves, it neutralises all the costs and all the savings together, never just the costs. In the illustration, the draft pays 82%, or 100% if neutralised.
- **Other guards:** capitalised development is frozen at the budgeted ratio, because in software capitalising more is the easiest way to lift EBITDA. Underspending product engineering, security, training or support earns no credit, and no measure rewards cutting headcount.
- **Multi-year:** a third of the executive team's payout is deferred two years. Malus and clawback apply to restated results, broken plan rules, or a serious quality, clinical-safety or data-protection failure.
- **Leaders first, written in advance:** if costs have to come out in 2027, the management bonus accrual is released before any role outside management is cut. In a year with compulsory redundancies, ExCo payouts are capped at target.

**The board's case, and how the draft meets it.** One number is simple. It's the number the deal is valued and financed on, and excluding restructuring is meant to stop management dodging changes the business needs. The draft keeps EBITDA dominant, and a planned restructuring costs management nothing. But think about it from the buyer's side: their quality-of-earnings review is likely to ask whether three years of "one-off" charges are really one-off. Illustratively, if the charges averaged 1.3m on 14m EBITDA, that's about 9.5% of profit, or roughly 16m of value at 12×. We'd be paying bonuses on that value and might never be paid for it. SEC rules wouldn't let a US-listed company call a charge non-recurring when a similar one occurred in the prior two years. We aren't bound by that, but a buyer will think the same way.

**If the board insists on 100% EBITDA,** the draft's fallback (option B) keeps EBITDA as the only measure that pays. Retention, quality and attrition become a modifier between 0.75 and 1.0 that can only reduce the payout, and the deferral and guards stay. The one thing I'd hold to in any version is that restructuring stays inside the number.

**To confirm before approval:**
- Counsel: whether changing past bonus terms needs notice or consent, whether deferral, malus and clawback hold up in our jurisdictions, and any say employee representatives have over pay systems.
- Tax advisers: treatment of deferred cash.
- Auditors: the Bonus EBITDA reconciliation.
- Our regulatory lead: that the quality measure can never discourage incident reporting.

None of this is legal or tax advice.

Two questions that would change the numbers:
1. What did the 2024–2026 restructurings cost, and were the savings still there a year later? The answer sets the budget line and shows the board how big the add-back really is.
2. Does management hold equity, and when do we expect to exit? That decides how much deferral we need and how it's treated on a sale.

**Key assumptions:** the illustration uses round numbers, not ours. I've assumed no other management plan already balances these measures. The 50/10/15/15/10 weights are a starting point for the remuneration committee, not a benchmark result.
