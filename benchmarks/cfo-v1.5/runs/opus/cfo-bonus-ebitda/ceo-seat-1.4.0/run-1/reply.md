My recommendation: keep adjusted EBITDA as the main measure, but stop excluding restructuring costs from it. We've restructured three years running, so those costs recur. Excluding them means the plan counts the savings and never the cost, which pays management to restructure.

**What that does in numbers** (illustrative: a €15m EBITDA target, management otherwise exactly on target, and a restructuring that costs €1.5m and saves €1.0m in 2027):

- Cost excluded, as usual: the plan measures €16.0m, and the EBITDA element pays 133% of target.
- Cost counted: €14.5m, paying 83%.
- Real 2027 EBITDA fell by €0.5m, yet the usual definition pays a third more.

There are two more problems. Severance is free in the bonus measure while retraining someone costs full price, so the plan prefers redundancy to redeployment. And a buyer is unlikely to accept a "one-off" that shows up every year: [analysts argue recurring restructuring charges shouldn't be add-backs](https://www.fticonsulting.com/-/media/files/us-files/insights/articles/2023/jun/adjusted-ebitda-eye-beholder.pdf). [US listed-company rules](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/accounting/sec/sec-reporting-interpretations-manual/roadmap-non-gaap-financial-measures/chapter-4-non-gaap-measures-that/4-7-performance-measure-prohibitions) don't apply to us, but they forbid calling a charge non-recurring if a similar one happened in the prior two years. So we'd be paying bonuses on EBITDA that due diligence will adjust back.

**The draft** is in `2027-management-bonus-plan-draft.md`, with a one-page cover note for the board. Its main points:

- **Measures:** Plan EBITDA 70%, gross revenue retention 15%, and a four-item quality, security and safety scorecard 15%. All at company level.
- **Restructuring:** planned change costs go into the budget, and the target is set after them. If the board approves a mid-year restructuring in advance, both its cost and its 2027 savings come out of the measure, so the bonus neither rewards nor punishes the decision.
- **Other adjustments:** a closed list that works both ways (one-off gains come out too), capped at [5]% of target, with a reconciliation the board and participants all see. Capitalised development is held at the budget rate, so moving cost into capex doesn't move the bonus.
- **Gates and malus:** no payout on restated accounts or a lost certification. No measure counts incidents, because that rewards not reporting them. Concealing an incident forfeits the bonus; reporting one never costs it.
- **Leaders first:** in a year with compulsory redundancies to cut cost, executive payout is capped at target.
- **Deferral:** a third of any payout above target waits a year, against 2028 retention, because churn from 2027 decisions shows up later.

**Options for the restructuring line**

| Treatment | Incentive | At exit |
|---|---|---|
| Exclude the cost (usual) | Pays for restructuring; severance cheaper than retraining | Bonus EBITDA above what diligence accepts |
| Count everything | Clean, but can deter a needed mid-year change | Aligned |
| Count planned costs; neutralise board-approved mid-year changes (recommended) | Neutral: the decision is made on its merits | Aligned |

**The board's case, put fairly.** EBITDA drives both the equity value and the debt, and a single measure keeps management focused. The exclusion exists so managers aren't put off a change that creates value: [compensation committees have shielded executive pay from restructuring charges for decades](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/40949025) for that reason (Dechow et al., 1994). The neutralisation rule keeps that protection without paying for the cost. If the board wants to keep 100% on EBITDA, the definition matters more than the weighting. In that case, keep the definition, gates and malus, and use retention and quality as a ±15% modifier.

**People.** Three restructurings in three years is a management result, mine included, and the cover note says so. Staff will compare the management bonus with what happened to their colleagues. A plan that pays more because the cost of those exits was left out would cost more trust than it buys. Survivors leave too: [a 1% cut was followed by 31% more voluntary turnover the next year](https://clipsheet.strategiccommunication.wisc.edu/?p=218008) in one study. If you have a works council, check with counsel whether it has consent or information rights over this plan (in the Netherlands, articles 27 and 31d WOR). This isn't legal advice.

Two questions would change the draft:

1. What did each of the three restructurings cost and save, and how much of 2026 adjusted EBITDA is add-backs?
2. Does the management equity plan already carry customer or quality conditions? If so, the 30% on non-EBITDA measures could come down.
