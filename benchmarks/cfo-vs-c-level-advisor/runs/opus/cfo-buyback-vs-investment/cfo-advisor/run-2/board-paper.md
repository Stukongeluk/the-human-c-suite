# Board paper (draft): Capital allocation, share buyback and the service and e-commerce programme

**From:** CFO
**Status:** draft for the board pre-read. Figures tagged [ASSUMED] are placeholders and must be replaced with actuals before this paper goes out. [GIVEN] means the figure is known internally. [VERIFIED] means it has been checked against a published source.

---

## 1. Decision required

The board is asked how to deploy €400m of net cash [GIVEN] between two proposals:

- **(a)** a share buyback that an activist shareholder wants at €300m this financial year. A buyback that size would also take EPS up to guidance, which on current trading we expect to miss by a few cents [GIVEN].
- **(b)** a €120m programme proposed by the COO to rebuild the e-commerce platform and train store staff for a new service model [GIVEN].

## 2. Recommendation

1. **Approve the €120m programme as a capped envelope released in three gates.** Release the first tranche once the business case clears the hurdle rate.
2. **Authorise a buyback of up to €150m.** Execute it over about 12 months through a broker-led programme, starting only after the market update.
3. **Adopt and publish a capital allocation policy.** It sets a liquidity floor, applies one hurdle rate to investment, and returns any cash above the floor at each full-year result.
4. **Don't size or time the buyback to meet this year's EPS guidance.** Update the market on the expected outcome together with this plan.

The €150m is conditional on the liquidity floor in section 3. If the floor has to be higher, the buyback gets smaller. The floor itself stays where it is.

**How to set the floor:**

- **Measurement date.** State when the €400m was measured. Electronics retailers typically hold peak cash just after the peak season, when sales have been collected and supplier payments haven't gone out yet [ASSUMED sector pattern]. If €400m is a peak figure, the low point in the year could be much lower.
- **History.** Use the lowest weekly net cash over the last three years, measured against the reporting-date figure.
- **Stress case.** Model a bad peak season, for example sales 10% below plan with the inventory already bought [ASSUMED scenario].
- **Leases and covenants.** Show lease-adjusted net debt (IFRS 16) alongside net cash, along with any rating or covenant thresholds. For a store estate, lease liabilities are likely larger than the net cash [ASSUMED].

**Doing both in full doesn't fit.** €300m + €120m = €420m, which is more than the €400m of net cash even before seasonality and leases. Funding both would mean borrowing at some point in the year.

## 4. The EPS case for €300m this year

**Illustrative company (replace with actuals):** share price €25; 80m shares (€2.0bn market cap); net income €140m; EPS €1.75 (P/E 14.3, earnings yield 7.0%); cash earns 2.0% pre-tax; 25% tax rate [all ASSUMED].

**Full-year run-rate.** Once all the shares are retired, €300m buys 12m shares (15% of the total) and adds about **+24 cents (+13.9%)** to EPS. €150m buys 6m shares (7.5%) and adds about **+11 cents (+6.4%)**. That's the real attraction, and the mechanics are sound. The buyback is accretive because the 7% earnings yield is well above the 1.5% after-tax yield on cash.

**This year's EPS** is what matters for guidance, and it depends on timing. The table assumes €300m is bought evenly over the months left in the fiscal year:

| Months left in fiscal year | EPS uplift this year | Trading days | Purchases per day | Average daily volume needed under the 25% cap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | +11.1c | ~252 | €1.2m | €5m |
| 6 | +5.4c | ~126 | €2.4m | €10m |
| 3 | +2.6c | ~63 | €4.8m | €19m |
| ~1.25 (about 5 weeks) | +1.1c | ~26 | €11.4m | €46m |

Under the EU buyback safe harbour, an issuer may not buy more than 25% of average daily volume on any trading day (Delegated Regulation (EU) 2016/1052, Art. 3(3)) [VERIFIED].

**What follows from the table:**

- **Timing decides it.** With six months or more left, the activist's arithmetic probably works. With a quarter or less, it probably doesn't, and the volume cap may make €300m impossible to buy this year within the safe harbour.
- **An accelerated share repurchase doesn't solve the real problem.** In an ASR, a bank delivers most of the shares upfront and buys them in the market over the following months. That fixes the timing, but not the cash shortfall or the trade-off against the investment.
- **Accretion is not value creation.** A buyback creates value only if the price paid is below intrinsic value. The board should see management's intrinsic value view before approving any size. If the shares aren't cheap, a special dividend is the better way to return the cash.

**What the research says.** Firms that would just miss their EPS forecast are significantly more likely to buy back shares. In the four quarters afterwards they cut employment, capex and R&D, by about 5% of employees, 10% of mean capex and 3% of mean R&D. On average the market doesn't punish these buybacks at the announcement. Later operating performance is weaker at the firms that cut investment to pay for them (Almeida, Fos & Kronlund, *Journal of Financial Economics*, 2016) [VERIFIED]. The argument against €300m is therefore about cash and investment, not about how the beat looks.

## 5. The programme will hit next year's EPS

**Accounting.** Training is expensed. Much of a modern platform rebuild is configuration and customisation of cloud software. Under the IFRS Interpretations Committee's 2021 agenda decision, that work is usually expensed rather than capitalised, unless it creates code the company controls [VERIFIED]. Finance and the auditors need to confirm the actual split.

**EPS drag, illustrative**, assuming the expensed part is spread evenly over two years and 80m shares:

| Share of the €120m expensed | Pre-tax P&L cost per year | EPS drag per year |
|---|---|---|
| 30% | €18m | −17c |
| 40% | €24m | −22c |
| 50% | €30m | −28c |

**What this means for guidance.** The guidance conversation is coming anyway, and it will be several times bigger than this year's few cents. The cleanest story for the market is a single message: this year slightly below guidance, next year an investment year, and here is what the investment should deliver and how progress will be reported.

**On next year's EPS alone, Option A wins.** With the investment deferred, the €300m buyback gives about +24c. Under Option B, the €150m buyback (+11c) is partly offset by the investment drag (about −22c). The case for Option B therefore rests entirely on the programme's returns. If the business case can't show a return above the cost of capital, the board should return more cash and invest less.

## 6. What the board should require from the programme

**A business case, not just a budget:**

- Incremental gross profit from the services attach rate, online conversion and online share, and store productivity.
- Payback, and return against the cost of capital.
- The cost of *not* investing: platform end-of-life, outages, the conversion gap against competitors.

**Stage gates** (illustrative split; the COO should propose the real one):

| Gate | Timing | Scope | Tranche | Release condition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | On approval | Platform foundations, service-model pilot in ~10% of stores | ~€40m | Business case clears hurdle rate |
| 2 | ~6 months | Platform build-out, regional rollout of service model | ~€40m | Pilot KPIs met (attach rate, conversion, NPS, staff retention) |
| 3 | ~12 months | Full rollout | ~€40m | Gate 2 KPIs met, platform milestones on plan |

**Contingency.** On average, large IT projects run 45% over budget, 7% over time and deliver 56% less value than predicted. That's from McKinsey's study with the University of Oxford of more than 5,400 projects [VERIFIED]. If the platform is ~€80m of the €120m [ASSUMED], an average overrun would add ~€36m. The board should decide now that overruns need fresh approval. They should come out of the buyback envelope, never out of the floor.

**People.** The service model only works if trained staff stay. The plan should include:

- time on the sales floor set aside for training;
- pay and progression for the new service roles;
- store staff retention as a gate KPI.

**Reporting.** A quarterly KPI dashboard to the board, covering both cost against budget and the value delivered.

## 7. The activist

- **Their point about idle cash is fair.** Saying so is part of a credible response.
- **Engagement.** The Chair should meet the activist before the decision is final and share the framework and the floor logic. In parallel, sound out the top ~10 holders, and prepare for escalation (public letter, AGM resolution, demand for a board seat).
- **The offer.** €150m now, plus a published policy that returns cash above the floor at each full-year result. If trading and the gates allow, total returns could approach their €300m over two years.
- **Precedent.** If the board sets this year's buyback to close an EPS gap, the activist learns how to get the next one.

## 8. Disclosure and execution (GC and brokers to confirm)

- **Inside information.** If the expected EPS shortfall is inside information, Article 17 of the Market Abuse Regulation (MAR) requires disclosure as soon as possible, unless the conditions for delaying it are met.
- **Safe-harbour trading restrictions.** For the life of the programme, the issuer may not trade during the MAR closed period or while it has delayed disclosure of inside information. The exceptions are a time-scheduled programme, or one lead-managed by an investment firm or credit institution that takes its trading decisions independently of the issuer (Delegated Regulation 2016/1052, Art. 4) [VERIFIED].
- **Volume cap.** Purchases may not exceed 25% of average daily volume per trading day (Art. 3(3)) [VERIFIED].
- **Shareholder authorisation.** Confirm how much headroom is left under the current AGM buyback authorisation: amount, percentage of share capital and expiry. Caps vary by national law.
- **Sequence.** Board approval, then the market announcement (trading update plus the plan), then the start of the buyback.

## 10. Critic screen

- **Weakest point.** The €150m and the €130m floor are placeholders. If the seasonal low turns out much lower, the buyback shrinks, and the activist will read that as backsliding. Get the low-point number before naming any figure outside the boardroom.
- **Missing perspective.** We haven't tested whether the shares are actually below intrinsic value. If they aren't, cash above the floor should go out as a special dividend.
- **Cost if wrong.** If the programme fails, the exposure is €120m plus a typical overrun (~€36m). With the gates in place, the realistic loss is limited to roughly tranche 1 (~€40m, which covers the platform foundations and the pilot).
- **Verdict.** Proceed with the risks noted, once the three checks in section 11 are back.
