**Short answer: don't announce the cuts before the bank meeting.** It probably won't fix the covenant on the test date. It puts you on the wrong side of the works council's right to advise. And it doesn't send the signal the owner wants. If you flag a 0.1x miss early, with a credible plan and visible support from the owner, it's the kind of problem banks usually settle with a waiver or a reset. What turns it into a bigger problem is a surprise, numbers that don't hold, or looking panicked. Use the three weeks to make the bank's decision easy.

If your cost base really is too big for the demand you expect, cutting capacity may still be right. But decide that on its own merits and run it properly. Don't use it as a gesture for one meeting.

## Why announcing first backfires

**1. It probably doesn't move the number that gets tested.** Leverage is normally net debt at quarter end divided by last-twelve-months (LTM) EBITDA. Savings from cuts arrive with a lag, and the costs come first. Here's an illustration; put your own figures in:

| | Q2 forecast | Cuts announced now, costs **not** added back | Cuts announced now, costs added back |
|---|---|---|---|
| LTM EBITDA | €4.50m | €4.06m | €4.76m |
| Net debt | €16.20m | €16.64m | €16.64m |
| **Leverage** | **3.60x** | **4.10x** | **3.50x** |

Assumptions: 26 roles at ~€60k fully loaded is ~€1.56m a year. Once you allow for the OR advice, the WMCO waiting period, the UWV process and notice periods, maybe two months of savings (~€0.26m) fall inside the LTM by the test date. One-off costs are ~€0.7m: about €0.35m of statutory transition payments, plus social-plan top-ups, advisers and notice periods paid but not worked. Those costs are booked, and mostly paid, before the test date. If Q2 end is further away, more of the savings land, but the pattern stays the same. The annual saving is real, the effect on the test date is small, and the costs come first. At best you scrape in at the limit with no headroom. At worst the breach gets deeper. Also note that under both IFRS and Dutch GAAP, announcing the main features of a restructuring plan to the people affected is usually what triggers the reorganisation provision. So an announcement now could put the cost into the very period being tested. Check this with your auditor.

**2. It runs into Dutch law.** With 260 staff you have a works council (OR). Cutting 26 jobs is almost certainly a "belangrijke inkrimping" under art. 25 WOR. That gives the OR a right to advise, and you must ask for its advice at a point when it can still substantially influence the decision. If you announce first, you present the decision as already made. If your final decision then differs from the OR's advice, you have to wait a month before carrying it out. The OR can appeal to the Ondernemingskamer (Enterprise Chamber), which can order you to withdraw the decision, undo its effects and stop implementing it.

On top of that, 20 or more dismissals within three months in one UWV region fall under the WMCO. You notify UWV and the unions and consult the unions. Contracts can't end until a one-month waiting period has passed, unless the unions waive it. If you skip this step, a court can overturn the dismissals. After that come the UWV permits, notice periods of one to four months depending on length of service, transition payments, the afspiegelingsbeginsel (you don't get to pick who goes) and whatever your CAO adds.

Telling the bank about a plan that then stalls with the OR, or ends up in court, is the worst possible signal about whether management is in control.

**3. Banks don't read it the way the owner hopes.** A credit committee wants answers to five questions:

- Is liquidity safe?
- Is the forecast credible?
- Is the breach temporary or structural?
- Is management in control?
- Will the shareholder back the company?

A rushed announcement about headcount answers none of them, and it can easily look like panic. The thing to avoid is being moved to the bank's special-assets team (bijzonder beheer). That means higher costs, advisers you pay for, and much less room to manoeuvre. Lost confidence is what gets you there, not a 0.1x miss.

**4. Collateral damage.**
- Credit insurers and suppliers read restructuring news too. If they cut supplier credit limits, your payables shrink and your net debt goes up.
- Your best people leave first, and skilled production staff are slow and expensive to replace.
- Customers start asking whether you can still deliver.
- In a family company, your reputation as an employer is part of what the company is worth, and you can't reverse cuts that turn out to have been unnecessary.

## What to do in the next three weeks

**Days 1–3**

- **Read the facility agreement with your lawyer.** Check:
  - the exact definitions of net debt and EBITDA: how leases are treated (IFRS 16), add-backs for exceptional or restructuring costs and any caps, and pro forma savings
  - test dates and reporting deadlines
  - any equity cure right
  - whether you must report a forecast breach
  - what a breach triggers: default, cross-default, margin ratchet
- **Pressure-test the forecast.** If 3.6x is the base case, what's the downside? Bring a number you can defend, not the hopeful one. Revising it later costs more credibility than a worse number now.
- **Call your relationship manager this week.** Keep it informal: "We see a risk of a narrow breach at Q2. We'll bring a plan and a proposal to the review." Your manager needs time to prepare the case for the credit committee, and three weeks is short. The formal review shouldn't be the first time the bank hears about this.

**Weeks 1–2: build the bridge from 3.6x to a level with headroom**

In the illustration, closing the gap to 3.5x takes ~€0.45m less net debt *or* ~€0.13m more LTM EBITDA. Getting to 3.3x, where you have some room, takes ~€1.35m less net debt or ~€0.4m more EBITDA, or a mix of the two. The levers below run roughly from fastest and least harmful to slowest:

- **Working capital.** Net debt is measured at a single moment, quarter end, so this is your fastest lever. Start with raw materials and finished goods: cut the product range, order closer to demand, clear slow movers. Then chase overdue receivables. If you carry €6m of stock, taking 10% out is worth ~0.13x on its own. Don't window-dress by squeezing suppliers or invoicing early at quarter end. The bank will see it reverse the next quarter, and credit insurers will notice.
- **Capex.** Defer anything that isn't needed for safety, maintenance or customers until after the test date.
- **Payments to the family.** Suspend dividends, management fees and other payments to the family until there's headroom again.
- **EBITDA without redundancies.**
  - Pricing and surcharges where you can pass costs on.
  - Purchasing and energy.
  - Product mix.
  - Labour: a hiring freeze and natural attrition, less overtime, and fewer temps and contractors first.
  - Don't count on short-time working. The Dutch scheme (WTV), and the crisis bill that is meant to replace it, only cover events outside normal business risk, not a drop in demand.
- **Non-core assets.** Surplus machinery or property. If leases count as debt in your covenant, a sale-and-leaseback helps less than it looks.
- **The owner's own money.** For a family business this is the strongest signal there is: a subordinated shareholder loan or new equity, or at least a written standby commitment to cure the breach if the plan falls short. That is what "showing we're serious" looks like to a bank.

Start on working capital now, because it needs lead time to show up at quarter end.

**Week 2: the bank pack** (share a draft with your relationship manager before the meeting)

1. One page: what happened, where the covenant lands, what you're doing, what you're asking for.
2. A quarterly covenant forecast for the next 4–6 quarters, base and downside cases, with headroom.
3. Liquidity: a 13-week cash flow and 12 months of headroom on the facilities. If liquidity is fine, say so first. It changes how the bank sees the breach.
4. Root cause: an EBITDA bridge from last year. Is the drop driven by the market and temporary, or structural?
5. Action plan: each lever quantified, with a person responsible, a date and its effect on the covenant. Keep what's already done separate from what's planned.
6. The owner's commitment.
7. The ask, with a preferred option and a fallback. Options include:
   - resetting the covenant for Q2–Q4 and stepping it back down to 3.5x
   - a one-off waiver for Q2
   - using the cure right

   Offer monthly reporting. Expect a waiver fee, possibly a higher margin and a block on dividends, and decide in advance what you'll accept.
8. Capacity review. If volumes look structurally lower, say you're reviewing capacity, give the scope and timeline, and confirm that any decision on jobs will go through the OR and WMCO process. You can share a proposed decision with the bank in confidence. That's lawful, and more credible than a public announcement.

**Week 3: the meeting.** Bring the owner. Their being there, with a concrete commitment, does more than any announcement.

**Alongside all this:** use your regular consultation meeting to brief the OR on the financial position and the cost review. If cuts do become necessary later, you'll need the trust you build now.

## How to put it to the owner

The owner's instinct is right: the bank needs to see that you're serious. It just judges seriousness differently. You and the owner want the same things: keep control of the company, keep the bank on side, and come out healthy. You could say something like:

"If we announce cuts now, we probably still miss the covenant on the test date. We risk a case at the Ondernemingskamer brought by our own works council. And the bank may read it as panic. If we arrive with a plan that's already moving, a stop on dividends and your personal backing, we get a waiver on our terms. Then the decision about jobs gets made on what the business needs, not for one meeting."

## Caveats

The figures above are illustrative, so rerun them using the definitions in your own agreement. Before anyone says anything about jobs, internally or to the bank, have a Dutch employment lawyer confirm the OR and WMCO route and your CAO's reorganisation clauses.
