My recommendation: don't put 120 redundancies in the board pack as €9 million of 2027 savings. On the timetable Dutch and German law allows, they save €3.3 million gross in 2027 and cost €5.1 million up front. Between December 2026 and December 2027 they leave the group €2.9 million worse off in cash.

I've built the model that way, net and by quarter, next to a staged plan that adds an estimated €4.6 million to the 2027 P&L (€3.3 million cash) with no dismissals and leaves the redundancy decision for one checkpoint in April.

- €9.0 million (120 × €75k) is a yearly run-rate, not a 2027 figure. Nobody can leave before consultation and notice are over. The model assumes exits from April to September in the Netherlands and from June to December in Germany.
- Watch the P&L. If the December announcement sets out the plan to the people affected, the auditors may put the €4.3 million provision into 2026. The 2027 P&L would then show +€1.5 million while 2027 cash is −€2.8 million. The pack should show both years and both measures.
- 2027 cash is negative in every sensitivity I ran. That includes putting all 120 roles in the Netherlands (−€1.3 million) and packages at the low end of the range (−€1.7 million). The two inputs that matter most are exit timing, mostly German consultation, and package level.
- From 2029 the redundancies are worth about €7.1 million a year.

The trap in one line: in the Netherlands and Germany, consultation and notice decide when savings start, and the costs come first.

- No option reaches €9 million inside 2027.

**Next month's announcement**

In both countries it can only be an intended decision that opens consultation:
- in the Netherlands, the works council's advice (art. 25 WOR);
- in Germany, a reconciliation of interests and a social plan (§§111–112 BetrVG);
- in both, the mass-dismissal notifications.

If it's announced as a done deal, the company risks an Enterprise Chamber appeal in the Netherlands and compensation claims in Germany (§113 BetrVG). It also shouldn't go out in the week before Christmas. If the CEO wants to say something in December, the stronger message is the savings programme, the leaders' own cut and the freeze, plus a commitment that any redundancies would be decided only after consulting the works councils.

Before the pack goes out, walk the CEO through the numbers one to one. They shouldn't see €3.3 million instead of €9 million for the first time in front of the board.

**Two questions that would change the numbers most**
1. What does the €9 million protect: a covenant test, a promise to owners, or cash? And does it have to land inside 2027, or as a run-rate by year-end? A covenant points to the bank and working capital now. A run-rate is within reach: C gets to about €8.4 million a year, B2 to about €12 million.
2. Which work stops with the 120 roles, and where? I mean the country split, the sites, and whether the roles are billable or overhead. That moves severance, timing and revenue at risk more than anything else. If a contract or site is ending, consulting on that scope now is a different and stronger case.

**To firm up the numbers** I'd pull, in aggregate: payroll for the affected pools (cost, tenure, age bands), the CAO and Tarifvertrag terms and any standing social plan, the works councils at each German site, spend by supplier, contract indexation and unbilled work, attrition history and the loan agreement.

Everything except the 1,400 headcount, the 120 roles, the €75k average and the legal rules is a labelled estimate. The key ones are a 60/60 country split, 10 years' average service, packages of 1.5 times the Dutch transition payment and 0.75 months' pay per year of service in Germany, and 7% natural attrition.

Employment counsel in both countries and your auditors need to check the timetable, the packages and when the provision is booked; this isn't legal advice.
