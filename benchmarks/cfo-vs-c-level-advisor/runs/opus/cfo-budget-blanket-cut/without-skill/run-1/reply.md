Keep the 8% and keep it simple. But make the rules simple rather than the cut uniform. With 68% of opex in payroll, "every cost centre cuts 8%" mostly means cutting the billable people who earn your revenue. On a thin margin you can hit the cost target and still leave EBIT flat. I'd take the board a targeted 8% built on five simple rules. Put the uniform cut next to it with the numbers, and be explicit about what lands in 2027 and what only shows in the run-rate.

The full board-paper draft is attached as **board-budget-approach.md**. It has the decisions to ask for, the options, a lever register, the Dutch works council and dismissal timeline, a risk table, a three-week plan, a week-one data request and a draft note to budget holders.

The numbers below and in the draft are illustrative:
- opex €70M
- revenue about €73M, EBIT about 4%
- 450 billable staff and 150 support staff

Swap in your actuals in week one; the logic doesn't change. I've also assumed "opex" means your total operating cost, billable staff included. If billable people sit in cost of sales, the revenue risk is smaller but the cut to support functions gets much steeper.

## 1. First, pin down what "8% lower" means

The two readings differ by about 40% in how much you have to find.

Settle three things with the chair before the pack goes out:
- Which of these two the board means.
- Whether the target is in-year or the exit run-rate.
- Whether the real objective is margin.

If it's margin, price counts too. Each 1% of extra price realisation is worth about €0.7M of EBIT, the same as eight or nine roles. Dutch contract wages are forecast to rise 3.8% next year, so full indexation of client contracts is one of the biggest levers you have.

## 2. Why the uniform cut doesn't work in a services business

- **It cuts revenue along with cost.** A billable person costs about €81k and brings in about €145k of revenue. 8% of billable payroll is about 36 people and €5.2M of revenue capacity. The uniform cut takes €5.8M off opex, but run-rate EBIT improves by only €0.6–3.2M, depending on how busy those people were. In 2027 itself, after one-off costs, the EBIT effect is roughly −€1M to +€1M.
- **You don't get to choose who goes.** Dutch redundancy selection follows afspiegeling: by age group and length of service within interchangeable functions. You can't cut the bench and keep the busy people.
- **The timing works against you.** Around 48 roles puts you under the collective-dismissal rules (20 or more in one UWV region within three months, settlement agreements included). That means union consultation and a one-month waiting period. Works council advice and 1–4 months' notice come on top. Exits land mid-year at best, so only about half the payroll saving shows in 2027. Against that you pay €1.2–1.7M in transition payments and social-plan costs.
- **Doing it through attrition doesn't fix it.** You'd have to leave two-thirds of all leavers unreplaced, including billable roles with client demand. Attrition also doesn't fall evenly. Uniform targets still force dismissals in some teams while others keep slack.
- **It rewards padded budgets, punishes lean ones, and hides the trade-offs from the board.** It also doesn't give you the per-role business rationale UWV or a settlement negotiation will ask for.

## 3. The approach: same 8%, five rules

1. **One company number,** owned by the whole ExCo and reported as one figure.
2. **Uniform −12% on discretionary spend** per cost centre: marketing, events, advisors, travel. Here your CEO's instinct is right. It's simple and fast, and budget holders choose what to stop.
3. **Big contracts are run centrally,** each with a named owner: contractors, IT and software, housing, lease cars, recruitment. Contractors are usually the largest quick win, especially where your own bench can take the work.
4. **A hiring freeze with a weekly 30-minute exception panel** (you, COO, CHRO). Backfill only two kinds of role:
   - billable roles with demand, in practices at or above utilisation target
   - critical control and security roles

   Natural turnover becomes the main payroll lever.
5. **Billable capacity is managed on utilisation and pyramid, not on cost %.** Each delivery unit gets a utilisation target and a headcount envelope tied to its pipeline.

Two additions:
- **A short protect list:** training, delivery quality, security and compliance, and named key talent.
- **Anti-gaming rules:** a saving counts only when the run-rate line is gone, deferrals are reported as deferrals, and nothing is reclassified to cost of sales or capex.

## 5. Be straight with the board about 2027

Neither option delivers 8% inside 2027, because people-related savings in the Netherlands land around mid-year at the earliest. Option B ends 2027 at the full 8% run-rate and delivers about 5% in-year.

If the board wants "8% below 2026", or 8% in-year, you need a second tranche. That means deeper support-function redesign (about 15 more roles), a housing step-change at the next lease break, and possibly a salary round below market drift. The draft calls this Option C: about €8.3M run-rate, about 22 forced exits, the collective-dismissal rules, and real retention risk.

I'd ask the board to approve B with a Q1 checkpoint. If less than 70% of the run-rate is secured by the end of March, tranche 2 goes to the works council in time to land in H2.

## 6. Dutch process: get the sequence right

- **The board approves the target and the framework, not the job cuts.** Specific job eliminations are proposed decisions that go to the works council for advice (art. 25 WOR), at a point where the advice can still change them. A board minute that already says "cut 48 roles" invites a challenge.
- **Brief the works council early.** It is entitled to the budget information anyway, so brief the chair informally before budget-holder instructions go out.
- **Change lease-car terms for existing staff only at renewal,** unless counsel says otherwise.

## 7. Three weeks

- **Week 1:**
  - Call the chair about the target definition.
  - Spend 20 minutes with the CEO on the one-page A-vs-B comparison.
  - Send the data request to FP&A and HR: cost cube, utilisation and bench, attrition, contractor list, contract calendar, client indexation clauses.
  - Send the discretionary-spend instruction to budget holders.
- **Week 2:**
  - Hold lever sessions with delivery and function heads.
  - Review the central contracts.
  - Get employment counsel to check the design.
  - Get the draft pack to the CEO by Friday.
- **Week 3:**
  - Send the pack out about five working days before the meeting.
  - Pre-wire key board members.
  - Have the internal and works council messages ready for the day after.

## 8. On your CEO

Your CEO is right about the two things that matter for execution: one number and simple rules. Option B keeps both. It drops only the uniform payroll cut, which is the part that costs you revenue.

Show the CEO the comparison table before anything goes to the board. If they still prefer the uniform cut, put both options in the pack with the numbers. The board should see the trade-off, and neither of you should be surprised in the room.
