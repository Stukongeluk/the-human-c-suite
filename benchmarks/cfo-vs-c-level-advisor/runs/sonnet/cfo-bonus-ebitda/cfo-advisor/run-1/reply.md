## Bottom Line

I drafted the plan as the board asked: 100% of the bonus pays on adjusted EBITDA. I did not draft "restructuring excluded as one-off as usual," because that wording no longer holds. You have restructured three years running, so restructuring is a recurring operating cost. If you exclude it, management gets paid for the savings and never bears the cost. That gives them a built-in reason to restructure again in 2027.

The draft keeps the board's structure and changes four things: a capped and pre-approved restructuring add-back, a cash-conversion gate, committee control of adjustments, and a clawback. Each is explained below, with the alternatives you can offer the board.

Confidence: **High** on the design logic. **Assumed** on every number in brackets. I have no budget, margin, cash or bonus-pool data, so those are placeholders to replace.

## Why

1. **Three years in a row is a pattern, not an exception.** An add-back is a claim that the cost will not recur. Your history contradicts that claim for 2025, 2026 and 2027 planning (**High**, from what you told me). Reported EBITDA with restructuring excluded overstates the run-rate earnings the business actually delivers.
2. **A 100% EBITDA plan with open-ended exclusions rewards the wrong move.** Cuts raise EBITDA and the cost drops out, so the cheapest way to hit the number is another restructuring. That is cost-driven, not growth-driven, and in a 350-person software company it erodes the product, support and compliance capacity that renewals depend on (**High** on the incentive, **Medium** on the size of the effect for your business).
3. **One metric invites gaming.** EBITDA alone is silent on cash, retention and growth. In software, EBITDA can be lifted by cutting R&D or customer success, capitalizing more development cost, or pushing out spend. The gate and committee discretion address that without breaking the board's 100% EBITDA instruction (**Medium**).
4. **The sponsor's own exit value is at stake.** A buyer will diligence adjusted EBITDA. Recurring add-backs get discounted or rejected in a quality-of-earnings review, so a bonus plan that rewards them pays management for earnings the exit may not recognize (**Medium**; I have not seen your exit timeline or any QoE work).
5. **Three years of restructuring also costs you the people who hold the product knowledge.** I'd expect that to show up in attrition and delivery speed. I have no figures for yours (**Assumed**).

## Your decision

Choose the restructuring treatment to put to the board:

| Option | What it does | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Capped and pre-approved add-back (the draft above)** | Keeps most of the board's "as usual" treatment, with a ceiling and approval step | Closest to what the board asked. Management still has some room to restructure with cost excluded, up to the cap |
| **B. Costs included in full, no add-back** | Management bears 100% of restructuring cost in the bonus year | Cleanest incentive. May discourage a restructuring that is genuinely needed, and the board will likely push back |
| **C. Include costs, but spread over 2-3 years** | Charges one-third per year, so cuts pay back before the cost fully clears | Middle ground. More complex to administer and track |

I recommend **A**, with the cap set at roughly the lowest of your last three years' restructuring spend. If the board will not accept any cap, fall back to **C**.

The other call is whether to keep 100% weighting on EBITDA with the gate and discretion (as drafted), or move to a split such as 70% EBITDA, 15% cash conversion and 15% retention. The split is the stronger design. The 100% version with a gate is the one I can defend if the board insists.

I would not recommend the plan as originally worded, with an unrestricted "one-off" exclusion, since your own history shows the costs recur.
