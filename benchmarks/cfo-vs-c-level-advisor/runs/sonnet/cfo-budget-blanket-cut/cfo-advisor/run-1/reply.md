# CFO — FY budget approach for the board: 8% lower opex

## Bottom line

Go to the board with one company-level target (opex down 8%) and a driver-based allocation that gets there. Do not present a flat 8% per cost centre. With payroll at 68% of opex, a flat cut mostly lands on people. It also cuts delivery capacity, and in a thin-margin services business that can lose more revenue than it saves cost. Confidence: High on the arithmetic, Medium on the recommendation until we see your cost-centre and utilisation data.

## What the numbers say

- **Payroll is 68 of every 100 euros, non-payroll is 32.** An 8% cut is 8 points of total opex. [Verified: from your numbers]
- **A flat 8% on non-payroll yields only 2.6 points.** The other 5.4 points, which is 8% of payroll, has to come from people costs. Even a 20% cut to non-payroll gets you only 6.4 points. So the CEO's "simple" rule is not a non-payroll exercise: it is a headcount and people-cost exercise. [Verified: arithmetic]
- **Illustrative size.** 600 FTE x EUR 85k = about EUR 51M payroll, so opex is about EUR 75M and the 8% target is about EUR 6.0M. A flat cut would take about EUR 1.9M from non-payroll and about EUR 4.1M from payroll, which is roughly 48 FTE-equivalents. [Assumed: EUR 85k loaded cost]
- **Check the baseline.** If "8% lower" is against this year's actual and next year carries CAO/salary indexation of about 3-4%, the real underlying cut is closer to 11-12%. If it is against the current budget, it is smaller. This is the first thing to pin down with the board. [Assumed: 3-4% indexation]
- **In-year versus run-rate.** Savings from attrition or a hiring freeze build up through the year. Leavers are spread across the months, so only about half the annualised saving lands in year one. Exiting people costs money up front (see the Dutch process point below). A plan that reaches 8% on a run-rate basis can still miss it in the P&L. [Assumed: leavers evenly spread]

## Why a flat 8% per cost centre is the wrong tool

1. **It treats unlike costs alike.** A billable delivery team, a bench, a back-office function and a software licence do not carry the same return. Equal cuts are not equal pain.
2. **It can destroy margin.** If "opex" includes delivery staff, cutting them cuts billable capacity. Illustration: if delivery cost EUR 1.00 earns EUR 1.25 of revenue, cutting EUR 8 of delivery cost loses about EUR 10 of revenue, so you net -EUR 2 and your thin margin gets thinner. Where we cut matters more than how much. [Assumed: 1.25x revenue to cost; test against actual utilisation and rate cards]
3. **It rewards padding and punishes lean teams.** Cost centres that already run tight take the same hit as ones with slack. Managers learn to inflate next year's baseline.
4. **It collides with Dutch employment process.** A company this size will have an ondernemingsraad (works council). Material reorganisations need its advice before a decision is taken. Dismissals go through UWV or settlement agreements, often with a transition payment, and collective redundancy (20 or more in a three-month period in one UWV region) triggers notification and union consultation. Spreading cuts evenly across every cost centre maximises the number of separate processes. This is general guidance: confirm timing and thresholds with employment counsel. [Medium confidence, verify with counsel]

## Recommended approach: one rule at the top, drivers underneath

The CEO's wish for simplicity is fair. Keep the rule simple at company level: "opex is 8% lower, and no cost centre is exempt from justifying its number." Then allocate using three classes:

| Class | What goes in | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Protect** | Revenue-generating delivery capacity at healthy utilisation, key account owners, sales pipeline with a short payback | 0% target, or an investment case. Cutting here loses revenue. |
| **Reduce** | Bench and under-utilised delivery capacity, support functions with weak workload drivers, discretionary spend | Cut to the driver (utilisation, spans of control, volume served), not to a percentage. |
| **Stop** | Spend with no measurable return: low-use tools and licences, agency or contractor spend that could be done in-house, unused office space, events without pipeline | Cut fully, first. |

**Illustrative waterfall to EUR 6.0M** (placeholders to be replaced by bottom-up numbers; not a forecast):

| Lever | EUR M | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Non-payroll: vendors, licences, travel, facilities, agency and contractors | 3.0 | About 12% of non-payroll, concentrated in "Stop" items |
| Payroll: hiring freeze and non-backfilled attrition | 1.8 | Slow, no exit cost, but only partly in-year |
| Payroll: variable pay, overtime, open requisitions removed from budget | 0.6 | Check CAO and contract terms |
| Payroll: spans, layers and utilisation-driven resizing of support functions | 0.6 | Only after the works council process; the one lever that carries exit cost |
| **Total** | **6.0** | |

This puts 2.4 of the 6.0 on people and relies mostly on natural attrition, which is the lowest-damage route. It only works if the non-payroll number holds, so the first two weeks go to proving that EUR 3.0M exists in the ledger.

## How to act (three weeks to the board)

1. **Week 1: Lock the baseline and definitions.** Confirm what "opex" includes (delivery cost or not), the comparison base (actual or budget), and indexation. Owner: CFO with CEO. Deadline: day 3.
2. **Week 1: Build the cost base from the bottom.** Headcount cost by cost centre and role, 12-month attrition by function, open requisitions, contractor and vendor list, utilisation by team. Owner: FP&A with HR. Deadline: day 7.
3. **Week 2: Cost-centre reviews on drivers.** Thirty-minute session per cost-centre owner: what do you deliver, what drives your cost, what would you stop first. Owner: CFO. Deadline: day 12.
4. **Week 2: Model three scenarios, downside first.** (a) Flat 8%, (b) the driver-based plan, (c) the driver-based plan with revenue 3% lower. Show P&L, in-year versus run-rate, one-off exit costs and margin. Owner: FP&A. Deadline: day 14.
5. **Week 2: Employment counsel and works council.** Counsel confirms thresholds and timelines. Agree with the CEO and HR when the OR is informed, so no decision is announced before advice is requested. Owner: CHRO with counsel. Deadline: day 14.
6. **Week 3: Pre-brief the CEO, then the chair.** Show why flat 8% costs more than it saves. Owner: CFO. Deadline: day 17.
7. **Board pack:** baseline, target, allocation by class, waterfall, scenarios, risks, quarterly triggers, and the decisions asked of the board. Owner: CFO. Deadline: day 19.

## Risks

- **Revenue erosion:** if cuts hit billable capacity, margin falls instead of rising. Trigger: utilisation or backlog dropping two months in a row. Mitigation: a protected class and a quarterly check.
- **Attrition is not controllable:** it may fall in a weak market or hit the wrong people. If actual leavers are below plan, the payroll lever slips and a decision on targeted reductions comes sooner.
- **Regretted attrition:** a visible cut and freeze make your best people the most likely to leave. Communicate the principle early.
- **Timing slip:** works council advice and notice periods can push exit savings into the second half or the following year.

## Your decision

- **Option A: Flat 8% per cost centre (CEO's proposal).** Simple, hard to argue with. But it falls mainly on payroll, cuts delivery capacity and risks revenue and margin.
- **Option B: 8% company-wide target, allocated by driver (recommended).** Slightly more work and a harder conversation with some cost-centre owners, but it protects revenue and lets you sequence the cuts from lowest to highest damage.
- **Option C: Ask the board for a phased target** (for example 5% in year one on a P&L basis, 8% run-rate by exit). Use if the numbers show 8% in-year is only reachable through compulsory redundancies.

Recommendation: B, with C ready as the fallback if the in-year math does not close. Present A to the board only as the comparison case.
