**Recommend to the board: fund the COO's €120 million in stages, keep a working-cash buffer, and return a smaller amount (roughly €80 million, my estimate) on its own merits, not as the fix for EPS. Take the few-cent miss openly and explain it.**

**Why not the €300 million**

- The two asks don't fit together. €300m + €120m = €420m against €400m of net cash. The buyback alone leaves about €100m, and a consumer electronics retailer has a heavy Q4 working-capital swing, leases and supplier terms. Net cash is not the same as spare cash.
- The buyback is being sized to a guidance number, not to excess cash. A gap of "a few cents" is closed by a fraction of €300m. On illustrative numbers (about 3% of EPS, shares at a €2bn market cap, 3% pre-tax interest forgone on the cash), roughly €50–100m would do it. So the €300m is not about the miss.
- Research on buybacks that firms made when they would otherwise just miss the EPS forecast links them to cuts in employment and investment and to lower cash holdings (Almeida, Fos & Kronlund, 2016). You would be joining that pattern, and the board should hear that.

**Why not simply say yes to the COO either**

€120m is a request, not yet a case. The platform and the service model are two different bets, and part of it (training, run costs) hits the P&L, not just the balance sheet. That makes the EPS gap bigger, and the board should see that number before it votes. I would not release it as one lump.

Doing nothing is also an option: it keeps the cash, but a weak platform and service model cost share each year, and the activist has a fair point about idle capital.

**How to run C**

1. **Invest only against the hurdle.** The COO brings a business case: incremental revenue, margin and service metrics, payback, and an IRR above our cost of capital (*say 8–10% for a retailer; use treasury's figure*). Anything below it waits.
2. **Gate the tranches.** Release the next €40m only when agreed measures are met: e-commerce conversion and share of sales, store service scores, staff retention and regretted attrition, and platform delivery milestones.
3. **Protect the training money.** Training is the first thing that gets cut when a quarter is tight. Ring-fence it, and don't fund anything here by cutting jobs, maintenance or training elsewhere.
4. **Set the return by policy.** The board sets a minimum cash level and a stated payout approach (buyback or special dividend), sized from excess cash after committed investment. Announce it with dates, and don't time it to the EPS number. If it lands near the gap, fine; just don't present it as the fix.
5. **Be straight on guidance.** Tell the market early and plainly why EPS is a few cents light: a deliberate investment to rebuild e-commerce and service, with measures that show whether it works. Long-term investors generally prefer that to a number bought with cash. Evidence suggests firms run for the long term grew revenue and earnings faster.
6. **Leaders carry the miss first.** If guidance is missed, executive bonuses reflect it before anyone else's pay does. Tie the multi-year part of incentives to the same customer, retention and delivery measures.

**The activist's view, fairly put:** €400m of cash earns little, management has an uneven record on big platform projects, and a buyback is certain while a rebuild is not. Our answer is a committed return plus staged, gated investment, which gives them accountability without betting the buffer.

**The COO's view, fairly put:** half-funding a rebuild can be worse than none. That is why tranche one must be large enough to deliver a visible result by itself.

**Human side:** a new store service model changes people's work. Bring in the works council (or equivalent) early, plan the training before the roll-out, and state that this programme is not a redundancy plan. If it were, I would want that modelled separately with its full costs.

**Things to check before the board, not legal or investment advice:** the buyback authorisation and trading windows, market-abuse and disclosure rules on guidance, loan covenants, and tax. Take counsel's and the auditors' view.
