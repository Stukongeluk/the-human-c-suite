**Recommend to the board: fund the COO's €120 million, in gated tranches. Do not run a €300 million buyback. Take the EPS miss to the market openly, and return only genuinely excess cash, sized on its own merits and not to close the EPS gap.**

Both asks together cost €420 million against €400 million of net cash. The board can't approve both as stated, and approving the buyback alone leaves €100 million, which has to cover a retailer's seasonal working-capital swing, leases and any investment you later decide you need.

I can't size either without the split between capex and opex and your deposit yield. As an illustration only, €300m at an assumed 2.5% pre-tax yield is about €7.5m a year of foregone interest, which is real but small next to the platform and service question.

**Why not the buyback now**

- **It buys an EPS number, not value.** Firms that would just have missed analysts' EPS forecasts were sharply more likely to buy back shares, and those EPS-driven buybacks went with cuts in employment and investment (Almeida, Fos & Kronlund, 2016). Your service-model plan is exactly the kind of spend that gets cut next.
- **Cash shouldn't be the first thing returned if the business can use it at a return above its cost of capital.** Consumer electronics retail is under channel pressure, so the platform and service model is arguably the thing that protects the earnings the buyback is meant to flatter.
- **Net cash is not free cash.** Before anything is called excess, deduct minimum operating cash, the seasonal working-capital peak (is the €400m measured at a seasonal high, after Q4 collections?), lease and supplier commitments, and any covenant or rating buffer. I'd want that figure from treasury before the board meets.

**Steelman for the activist and the COO**

- The activist is right that a net-cash balance sheet this size costs shareholders something, and that guidance misses erode credibility. A plan with no capital return at all will not survive the AGM.
- The COO is right that €120m is meaningful, but the plan has to prove itself. A blank cheque for a platform rebuild is how retailers lose €120m and two years.

**How I'd structure the recommendation**

1. **Approve the €120m in three tranches (about €40m each; the split is my estimate, the COO should replace it).** Each releases on evidence: conversion and availability for the platform, plus service scores, staff retention and repair/attach revenue for the new service model. Protect the store-staff training line explicitly; it is the first thing to be squeezed when the quarter is tight.
2. **Cost the programme like every alternative:** its one-off costs, run costs, delivery risk and a downside case where benefits arrive a year late. I need the COO's payback and return against our hurdle rate before the board votes.
3. **Set the capital-return framework before the number.** After investment, minimum cash and commitments, publish a policy: an ordinary dividend plus buybacks from excess cash. If treasury confirms, for example, that €130-180m is genuinely excess (an estimate until the working-capital peak is known), offer a programme within that range, executed over 12-24 months. Its size must stand on the excess-cash calculation. If it only makes sense as the fix for the EPS miss, don't do it.
4. **Reset guidance honestly.** Tell the market the miss, the investment and the timeline for returns. Reconcile adjusted EPS to reported, and don't label the programme costs "one-off" if a similar charge is likely again within two years.
5. **Nothing funded by cuts or borrowing.** No store-staff cuts, training cuts or debt to close the cents gap. If the board wants options to close it, work cash, supplier terms (without squeezing small suppliers), spend and pricing first.
6. **Management pay carries the miss.** The EPS shortfall lands on executive bonuses before anyone else's job. That is also the strongest signal to the activist that we take the miss seriously. Tie future incentives to customer, people and multi-year measures, not EPS alone.

**What I'd say to the activist:** we'll return excess capital on a published policy, and we won't buy a few cents of EPS with money the business needs. Over three to five years the platform and service model supports higher earnings than a one-off share count reduction does.

**Law and process:** buyback authorisation limits, insider and market-abuse rules around guidance and repurchase timing, and works-council consultation on the service model all need counsel and your auditors. This is not legal, tax or investment advice.

**Key assumptions and what would change my mind**

- Assumes the €400m is not a seasonal peak and the COO's case clears the hurdle rate. If treasury shows only about €100-150m is genuinely excess, the return shrinks and the tranches slow down.
- If the COO can't show a payback or the benefits are mostly cost cuts in stores, I'd shrink the first tranche and rethink the case.
- If the stock trades well below a conservative intrinsic value, a larger buyback from true excess cash becomes more defensible.
