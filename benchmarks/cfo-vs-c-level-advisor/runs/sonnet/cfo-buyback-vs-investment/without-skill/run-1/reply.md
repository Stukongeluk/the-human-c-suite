# Recommendation to the board: buyback vs. platform and service investment

## Short answer

Do not present this as a choice between the activist's plan and the COO's plan. Recommend a sequenced capital allocation plan:

1. **Approve the €120m transformation, released in stage-gated tranches** against a business case the COO has to defend.
2. **Approve a smaller buyback, about €100m this year**, sized from cash that is genuinely surplus. Pair it with a published framework for returning more as the cash proves out.
3. **Do not size or time the buyback to hit EPS guidance.** If the investment pushes EPS below guidance, reset the guidance and explain why.

Your plan is to invest first, return the surplus, and be honest about EPS. That is more defensible than either extreme.

## Why not simply do the €300m buyback

- **The money does not stretch.** €300m plus €120m is €420m, which is more than the €400m of net cash. Even before that, "net cash" is not the same as deployable cash. For a consumer electronics retailer, year-end cash is usually flattered by Q4 trading and supplier payables that unwind in Q1. Leases, tax, capex and the seasonal working-capital swing all need covering. Ask treasury for the lowest forecast liquidity point over the next 12 to 18 months, plus a stress case. Deployable cash is that low point less a minimum buffer, not €400m.
- **A buyback is a weak tool for a few cents of EPS.** EPS is measured on the weighted-average share count. A buyback executed through the year, which is what the market-abuse and safe-harbour rules practically require, only partly helps this year's EPS. To close the gap you would need to front-load it, which means buying at whatever price the market gives you.
- **It is the wrong reason, and investors and the press will notice.** A buyback justified by a guidance miss looks like earnings management. It is worse if management pay is tied to EPS. The remuneration committee should see that conflict stated openly before the board votes. You are also personally exposed if the rationale is later read as hitting a target rather than creating value.
- **A buyback only creates value if the shares trade below intrinsic value.** The board should decide that on valuation. If the stock is fairly valued, €300m returned is neutral at best. If the shares are cheap, that argues for a buyback, but it still does not argue for spending the cash the business needs to stay competitive.

## Why not simply fund the COO's €120m as asked

- **Electronics retail has thin margins and strong online competitors.** A weak e-commerce platform and an undifferentiated store experience are existential risks over a few years. That makes some version of this spend close to mandatory, not discretionary.
- **A request for €120m is not yet a business case.** I would not wave it through. Before the board votes, ask the COO for:
  - the split between capex and opex, and the profile by year. Much of it will not be spent this year, and the P&L hit depends on what can be capitalised.
  - the specific return drivers: conversion, online share, attach and service revenue, labour productivity, churn and NPS, each with a baseline and a target.
  - gates and kill criteria. For example, tranche 1 of about €40m to €50m covers the platform core and a store pilot. Tranche 2 is released if agreed milestones are met on time and on budget.
  - delivery risk: replatforming projects often overrun. Add a contingency and a named owner.
  - what happens if you do nothing: lost share and margin, so the board sees the cost of the alternative as well.

If treasury's trough is deeper than assumed, shrink the buyback first, not the investment. The investment is spread over several years while the buyback is cash out this year, so the real cash call this year is smaller than the headline.

## The EPS question

- **Model the P&L effect of the investment honestly.** Any expensed part (training, platform running costs, dual-running, depreciation start-up) will lower EPS. The "few cents" gap may widen, not close.
- **Options, in order of preference:**
  1. Reset guidance now, with a clear explanation: here is the investment, here is the payback, here is the EPS path. Do it with the next results or an earlier trading update. A candid, early reset costs less credibility than a late miss or a visibly engineered beat.
  2. If transformation costs are truly one-off, show them separately and consistently. Do not relabel recurring costs as one-offs, because analysts and your auditors will challenge it.
  3. A moderate buyback may close part of the gap as a side effect. That is acceptable. Using it as the means to hit guidance is not.
- **Rough mechanics.** Buying back x% of shares lifts EPS by roughly x/(1-x), less the after-tax interest you give up on the cash (perhaps 2 to 3 percent a year pre-tax on cash held). Timing within the year cuts the in-year effect further. Run this with the actual share price, share count and interest rate. I have not assumed any of them.

## How to handle the activist

- Meet them early and listen. Do not stonewall, and do not give in on the headline number.
- Bring a capital allocation framework, not a single number:
  1. Maintain a stated minimum liquidity buffer.
  2. Fund returns-justified investment first.
  3. Return surplus cash to shareholders through buybacks and/or dividends, with a disclosed trigger (for example, a target net cash level after the buffer and the committed tranches).
  4. Review it at set points (half-year, and when tranche 2 is released).
- Offer evidence: the investment case, the liquidity analysis and a credible return plan. Activists usually accept a well-argued middle path, particularly one that shows the buyback is real and not a "no".
- Ask your advisers to check the activist's position and likely next steps, such as requisitioning a meeting, proxy contests or public campaigns, so the board is not surprised.

## Governance and execution points

- **Authority and rules.** Confirm the AGM buyback authorisation, any cap on repurchasable shares, and the applicable market-abuse and safe-harbour requirements (price, volume and closed-period limits). A standing mandate with a bank lets the programme run through closed periods.
- **Board record.** Minute the liquidity analysis, the alternatives considered and the remuneration-conflict discussion.
- **Auditor and disclosure.** Brief the auditor early on how the investment will be accounted for. Take legal advice on timing of any guidance change and inside information.
- **Disclosure.** Tell the market what you are doing and why, in one coherent message: invest, return surplus, here is the framework.

## What I need to check before you take this to the board

4. How EPS features in executive pay and any bank covenants or credit ratings.
5. The activist's stake, track record and likely escalation route.

## Decisions to ask the board for

1. Approve the €120m transformation envelope with tranche 1 released now and later tranches gated.
2. Approve a first buyback tranche of about €100m, executed through the year under a bank mandate, with the sizing subject to treasury confirming the liquidity buffer.
3. Adopt the capital allocation framework and the review points.
4. Instruct management to update the market on guidance in line with the investment plan.
5. Mandate the CFO and Chair to engage the activist on that basis.
