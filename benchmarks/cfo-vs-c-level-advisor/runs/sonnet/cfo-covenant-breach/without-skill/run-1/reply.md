My advice is not to announce the 10% cuts before the bank meeting. Take a fuller plan to the bank, with the cuts held back as an option. The gap is small enough that you probably don't need them to close it.

## 1. How big the problem is

At 3.6x against a 3.5x limit, the gap is about 3% of EBITDA, or about 3% of net debt. Either of these closes it:

- EBITDA about 2.9% higher (3.6 / 3.5 = 1.029), or
- net debt about 2.8% lower (0.1 / 3.6).

For a 260-person manufacturer, that is a working-capital and timing problem. It is not a case for cutting 26 people. You have until the end of Q2 to move it, so use that time.

## 2. Why announcing the cuts first is a mistake

- **The legal process.** With 260 employees you have a works council (OR). A decision to cut about 10% of the workforce is a decision on which the OR has the right to advise (adviesrecht, art. 25 WOR). It has to be consulted before the decision is taken. If the owner announces the cuts first, the consultation is a formality, and the council can challenge the decision in the Enterprise Chamber. You also risk a bad start with the people you need most.
- **Collective redundancy.** 26 people in one reorganisation is more than 20 dismissals within three months in one UWV region. That triggers the collective redundancy notification to the unions and UWV, and the UWV dismissal permit procedure for business-economic reasons. The criteria are demanding: the redundancies must be necessary for business reasons, the selection must follow the reflection principle (afspiegelingsbeginsel), and redeployment must be considered. Please confirm the details with your employment lawyer. The point for now is that an announcement does not make the cuts happen. The process takes months.
- **The saving comes late and costs cash first.** Notice periods, the transition payment, any social plan, and advisers' fees all land before the savings do. The cuts would probably lower EBITDA and cash in H1, which works against a Q2 covenant test. Restructuring costs are only excluded from covenant EBITDA if your facility agreement says so.
- **It signals panic.** Banks get nervous when a company reacts to a breach with a sudden announcement. Cuts made without a plan can lose you the people you most want to keep. Customers, suppliers and credit insurers read the news too, and may tighten terms on you at the worst moment.
- **It answers the wrong question.** The bank wants to know three things. Do you understand the problem? Do you have a credible plan? Will the owners stand behind it? A headcount number does not answer any of them.

I would explain it to the owner this way: showing you are serious means bringing a plan with numbers and owner commitment. An announcement does not do that.

## 3. What to do in the next three weeks

**Week 1: get the facts straight**
1. Read the facility agreement closely. Check the exact definitions of net debt and EBITDA, the permitted add-backs, the test dates, the cure rights (including any equity cure), and what counts as an event of default.
2. Rebuild the forecast. Run a base case, a downside case and a management case. Show which assumptions drive the 3.6x and how sensitive it is to them.
3. Build a 13-week cash flow and a quarterly bridge to the end of Q2.
4. Check whether the breach is real or definitional. For example, one-offs or an IFRS 16 or lease treatment might move the ratio by that 0.1x.

**Week 1-2: line up the levers, cheapest and fastest first**
- Working capital. Look at inventory (raw materials, WIP, finished goods), receivables and payment terms, and supplier terms. A few percent of net debt can often be found here quickly.
- Capex. Defer or phase what is not essential. Check any leases or asset finance.
- Cost actions that do not need a collective dismissal. These include a hiring freeze, not renewing temporary contracts and agency staff, cutting overtime, reducing discretionary spend, and voluntary schemes. Use natural attrition first. Short-time working (the Dutch NOW scheme is gone, so check what is currently available) is another option to check with your adviser.
- Pricing and margin. Look at the price list, unprofitable SKUs, and material cost pass-through.
- Owner support. This is the strongest signal you can send. Options are a subordinated shareholder loan, an equity injection or cure, deferring dividends and owner withdrawals, or sale-and-leaseback of property or equipment.

**Week 2-3: engage the bank before the review, not at it**
- Call your relationship manager this week. Banks hate surprises far more than they hate bad news. Tell them early: you forecast a small breach at the end of Q2, you have a plan, and you want to agree the route.
- Ask for what you need. Usually this is a covenant waiver, a reset of the test level or the test date, or a covenant holiday for one or two quarters. Expect an amendment fee, perhaps a margin step-up, and tighter reporting such as monthly figures and the 13-week cash flow.
- Bring a short pack: what happened, the forecast with sensitivities, the actions with owners, dates and amounts, and the owner's commitment. If the bank asks for more, you can show it is ready.

## 4. How to handle the cuts

Do not drop the idea, because you may genuinely need to adjust the cost base. Treat it as a structural question to decide on its merits.

- Do an honest capacity and demand analysis. Does the order book support 260 people over the next 12-24 months? If it does not, a reorganisation is justified, and you size it to the real gap. It may be 5%, 10% or 15%.
- If it is justified, do it properly. Brief the OR early, ask for its advice before deciding, apply the selection criteria, and negotiate a social plan. This is more robust and, in my experience, cheaper in total than a rushed one.
- In the bank meeting, say it as a contingency: "If the working-capital and owner measures do not deliver by X date, we will start a consultation on a reorganisation of up to Y FTE." That shows you are serious without committing to something you have not yet validated.

## 5. Talking to the owner

- Acknowledge the instinct. The owner wants the bank to see action, which is reasonable.
- Put the numbers in front of them. The gap is about 3%, and the cuts would hurt cash before they help.
- Point out the legal and reputational exposure of announcing before the OR is consulted.
- Offer the alternative that does send the strong signal: a visible owner commitment, such as a subordinated loan, no dividends, or an equity cure, together with a credible plan.
- Agree the communication line for staff. They will hear about the bank issue sooner or later, and it is better they hear it from you.

## Assumptions and caveats

I do not have your facility agreement, forecast, or order book, so I have assumed a standard leverage covenant (net debt / EBITDA) tested quarterly. Check the definitions, because they could change the answer. Check the OR and UWV points with your employment counsel before the owner says anything publicly. A good insolvency or restructuring adviser, or your auditor, can also help prepare the bank pack at short notice.
