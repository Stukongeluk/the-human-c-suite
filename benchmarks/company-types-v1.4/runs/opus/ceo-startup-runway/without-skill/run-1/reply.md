**Short answer:** Don't make the 30% cut this month, and don't refuse it either. Take two weeks to build the real numbers. Then bring the board a plan that keeps the company alive until mid-2028 even if the Series B slips again. That plan will very likely include a headcount reduction. But size it from the cash plan, design it around what gets you the B, and pair it with a bridge from the investors who are asking for the cut. Your investors are probably right about the direction. They're wrong about the method and the timing, and in Sweden the timing matters more than they think.

## 1. The problem is bigger than the 30% question

Nine months of runway means your cash runs out around August 2027. If the B closes "next summer at the earliest", you'd be closing with weeks of cash left, and that only holds if it doesn't slip again. Rounds that slip once often slip again. Raising with no runway is how good companies end up with punishing terms or no deal at all.

So set the goal like this: **have cash through roughly mid-2028 without counting on the B**. Then you can raise next summer with 9–12 months still in the bank. That's about nine months more money than you have now.

Revenue growth won't close that gap on its own. 4% a month is about 60% a year. That's real growth, but it's slow next to a startup's burn. How long you'd take to break even at 4% a month depends on how much of your costs revenue covers today:

| Revenue covers today | Months to break even at 4%/month (costs flat) |
|---|---|
| 25% of costs | ~35 |
| 40% | ~23 |
| 50% | ~18 |
| 60% | ~13 |

Unless revenue covers well over half your costs, you can't grow or cut your way to profit inside your runway. You need a mix of lower burn, new money and faster cash. So the real question for the board isn't "30% or not". It's "what mix pays for the next 18 months?"

## 2. What a 30% cut actually buys in Sweden

You can't cut 30% "this month" in Sweden, and the savings arrive later than your investors' spreadsheet assumes:

- **MBL negotiation comes first.** You must negotiate before you decide. That's with the union you have a collective agreement with, or if you have none, with the unions that have members affected. Expect 2–4 weeks, and the Christmas holidays will slow it down.
- **You must notify Arbetsförmedlingen (varsel).** With 5–25 people affected, the notice has to go in at least two months before the first person's employment ends.
- **LAS notice periods** start at one month and rise with how long someone has worked for you: two months after two years, three after four, up to six. Many contracts and collective agreements set longer periods. You pay people through their notice, even if they're released from work.
- **Turordning (order of selection).** Within each group, the last person hired is the first to go. You can exempt up to three people. The alternative is to agree a different order with the union (if you have a collective agreement) or to make individual separation agreements. Both usually involve severance packages. The law doesn't require them, but packages are how most Swedish redundancies get agreed.
- **Rehiring rights.** Anyone with more than 12 months of service gets priority for rehiring for nine months after they leave. That matters if the B closes next summer and you want to build back up.

Realistically, if you decide in early December, people leave between February and April. Here's an illustration. The numbers are made up but typical:

- 48 people at about 90k SEK a month each, fully loaded
- 2.2 MSEK a month in other costs
- revenue of 1 MSEK a month, growing 4% a month
- 45 MSEK in the bank

| Scenario | Cash runs out |
|---|---|
| Do nothing | Aug 2027 |
| 30% cut, as if savings started today | Nov 2027 |
| 30% cut, Swedish reality (people leave ~March, 2-month packages) | Sep 2027 |
| 30% cut + 25% off non-payroll costs | Nov 2027 |

In this example, the 30% cut on its own moves your cash-out date by about a month. Its real value shows up over 18 months, where it saves roughly 16 MSEK. In practice, that means it shrinks the bridge you'll need:

| To pay for everything through mid-2028 | Bridge needed (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| No changes | ~46 MSEK |
| 15% headcount + 25% non-payroll cuts | ~29 MSEK |
| 30% headcount + 25% non-payroll cuts | ~21 MSEK |

Your numbers will differ, but I'd expect the pattern to hold. Two conclusions:

1. **A cut alone doesn't fix your runway. A cut plus a bridge does.** So the size of the cut and the size of the bridge are one decision, and you can trade one against the other.
2. **The delay is a reason to decide fast, not a reason to skip the plan.** Every two weeks of delay means two more weeks of full payroll at the end.

## 3. What I'd do in the next three weeks

**Week 1: get the truth on one page.**
- Build a 13-week cash forecast and a 24-month model with three cases: the B closes summer 2027, the B closes in 2028, and no B at all.
- Ask your current lead investors and two or three likely B investors one question: "What would we need to show, and by when, for you to give us a term sheet?" Their answers tell you what to protect.
- Work out your burn multiple: net burn divided by net new ARR. B investors will look at this number first, and it's probably what worries your current investors.
- Map which people and projects drive revenue growth and those B milestones, and which don't.

**Weeks 1–2: use the levers in order, fastest and least harmful first.**
1. **Non-payroll spend:** tools, cloud, office, travel, events and agencies. Also **consultants and contractors**. In Sweden you can usually end those much faster than employment contracts.
2. **Freeze hiring** and close open roles.
3. **Bring cash in faster:** offer customers a discount to prepay annually, bill pilots by milestone, chase receivables, and draw down any grants you've already been awarded.
4. **Voluntary measures.** Cut leadership pay, starting with your own. Offer voluntary reduced hours, unpaid leave or swaps of salary for options. In Sweden these only work by individual agreement, because you can't cut pay on your own. Voluntary departure packages can work too, but watch that your best people don't take them first.
5. **New money:** a bridge from your current investors as a convertible loan, venture debt (only alongside that bridge), Almi, and strategic or corporate investors. Grants from Energimyndigheten, Vinnova or the EIC are too slow to save you, but they strengthen your case for the B.
6. **Only then, size the redundancy to cover what's left, and cut work along with people.** Drop products, markets or projects. Don't ask 34 people to deliver the plan made for 48.

**Weeks 2–3: go back to the board with options.**
- Talk to each board member one-to-one first. Find out why these two want 30%. It could be pressure in their own fund, a rule applied across their whole portfolio, or a real view of your business. Each needs a different conversation.
- Present the trade-offs side by side: a 30% cut with bridge X, 20% with bridge Y, no cut with bridge Z. Then give your recommendation.
- Ask the two investors directly whether they'll put money into a bridge if you hit the burn target. If they want the cut but won't fund the gap, they're asking you to bet the company on a B that may not come. Put that on the table.
- If nobody will bridge, say so plainly and plan for it. That means a deeper restructuring, and starting talks with strategic partners or possible acquirers now, while you have nine months of cash rather than six weeks.
- Have a lawyer brief the board now on the kontrollbalansräkning rule (Companies Act, chapter 25). As equity runs down, board members who miss that trigger can become personally liable for the company's new debts. You don't want the board to learn about it in month seven.

## 4. If you cut, do it once and do it well

- **Make one cut, sized for the downside case.** A second round six months later damages trust far more than one deeper, honest cut.
- **Run the process properly.** Hire an employment lawyer this week. Bring the unions in early and be honest with them. File the varsel when MBL starts. Try to agree the order of selection with the union rather than fight over it.
- **Treat the people who leave well.** Give fair packages, transition support (TRR or similar if you have a collective agreement, Kammarkollegiet's support if you don't), references, and introductions across your investors' portfolio companies. Stockholm's climate-tech scene is small, and the people you'll want to hire in 2027 will remember how you handled this.
- **Tell the whole team the truth all at once:** the runway, the plan and the reasons. Don't let it come out in pieces. Once MBL starts, it won't stay secret anyway.
- **Look after the people who stay.** Give them a new, smaller plan and clear milestones to the B. Offer retention to the people you can't lose, for example with new option grants.

## 5. What I'd avoid

- Waiting for the B to rescue you.
- Choosing a cut because it's a round number rather than because the cash plan needs it.
- Cutting every team by the same share.
- Cutting the people who drive your revenue growth or your B milestones.
- Letting two investors make the decision for you, or ignoring them. You're the CEO. You should be the one bringing the number, with the math behind it.
