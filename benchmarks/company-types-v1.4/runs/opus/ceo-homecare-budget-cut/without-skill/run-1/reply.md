**Short answer:** Don't adopt the three measures as they stand, and don't just reject them either. Take charge of the gap yourself. Within about six weeks, go back to the supervisory board with your own plan that closes the 7% in numbers over a realistic period, and keep the sound idea inside each of their proposals.

The board is right about the problem: you can't absorb a 7% gap. But all three measures go after the same thing: your care staff's time, judgement and autonomy. That is what earns your revenue and keeps people in their jobs. In this labour market, measures that drive nurses away usually cost more than they save.

## The three proposals, one by one

### 1. Replace 15% of district nurses with lower-qualified carers

**Why it doesn't add up**

- **It saves less than it seems.** A rough illustration (use your own figures). Suppose about a quarter of your 650 staff are hbo-trained district nurses. That is roughly 100–120 FTE, so 15% is about 15–18 FTE. Under the CAO VVT, a district nurse might cost €12–18k a year more per FTE than a level-3 carer. The saving is then about €0.2–0.3M a year. You only get that once the change is complete, and in practice it will happen through people leaving, not overnight. Against a gap of roughly €2.8M (see below), that is about a tenth.
- **District nurses are the people who unlock your revenue.** Only a BIG-registered nurse with an hbo or master's degree may carry out the needs assessment (indicatiestelling) ([V&VN normenkader](https://www.venvn.nl/media/fycd5jjm/handreiking-normenkader-def-jan2025.pdf)). Insurers set staffing conditions too. CZ's 2027 purchasing policy, for example, requires "sufficient hbo-bachelor nurses (level 6) in employment" to handle access, needs assessment, coordination and care plans ([CZ Wijkverpleging 2027](https://zorginkoopbeleid.cz.nl/external/asset/download/project/f9ff770a-0425-0000-f337-a117fb0b90e7/name/Wijkverpleging2027_v12645.pdf)). Fewer nurses means slower intake, weaker coordination and possibly a breach of contract. Check every insurer's 2027 conditions before anything else.
- **It adds quality and safety risk.** Clients at home keep getting more complex. In hospitals, a richer skill mix (more registered nurses relative to assistants) goes with lower mortality and better quality ([Aiken et al., BMJ Quality & Safety](https://qualitysafety.bmj.com/content/qhc/early/2016/12/30/bmjqs-2016-006197.full.pdf)). That is hospital evidence, not home care, but it points the same way.
- **The labour market works against it.** Level-3 carers are scarce too. And nurses who watch their profession being downgraded are the first to leave, because they have the most options.

**What to keep:** "Is every nurse working at the level she's trained for?" is a fair question. If district nurses spend hours on personal care, paperwork or planning, let the teams redesign who does what. Carers and helpers take the tasks that fit their level, and nurses focus on needs assessment, complex care and coordination. Do this team by team, as people leave naturally, without a percentage target set from the top.

### 2. An app that registers every client visit to the minute

- **It goes against where the sector has deliberately moved.** In 2018 the minister, insurers, the NZa, accountants and nurses agreed to end minute registration in district nursing from 2019. Billing would be based on the care planned in advance instead ([Accountant.nl](https://www.accountant.nl/nieuws/2018/12/minutenregistratie-in-wijkverpleging-verdwijnt/); [Skipr](https://skipr.nl/nieuws/steeds-minder-vijf-minuten-registratie-maar-we-zijn-er-nog-niet/)). Funding is moving further in that direction. CZ refers to a coming new funding model built on client profiles, and ActiZ is pushing for monthly rates partly because they make minute registration unnecessary ([ActiZ](https://www.actiz.nl/sites/default/files/2025-08/ActiZ%20-%20Hand-out%20passende%20bekostiging%20wijkverpleging.pdf)).
- **You can't introduce it on your own authority.** Under art. 27 WOR, any system that *can* be used to monitor staff attendance, behaviour or performance needs the works council's (OR) consent, even if it isn't used that way ([Dirkzwager](https://www.dirkzwager.nl/kennis/artikelen/instemmingsrecht-van-de-or-volgens-het-or-privacyboekje-van-de-ap)). Under the GDPR, systematic monitoring of staff will almost certainly also need a DPIA (a data protection impact assessment).
- **It works against your biggest hidden cost: sickness absence.** In 2024, absence in the VVT sector was 8.9%, against 7.3% across healthcare as a whole ([IZZ](https://izz.nl/media/1/Factsheet-onderzoek-ziekteverzuim-VVT.pdf)). Intrakoop counted a record 9.4% in elderly care ([ANP/Intrakoop](https://nieuws.nl/algemeen/minder-rode-cijfers-in-ouderenzorg-maar-verzuim-blijft-stijgen)). One in four VVT staff report low autonomy, and their absence is significantly higher ([IZZ](https://izz.nl/media/1/Factsheet-onderzoek-ziekteverzuim-VVT.pdf)). Tracking visits to the minute takes autonomy away.
- **It saves nothing directly.** It costs licence fees, implementation and extra admin time.

**What to keep:** The board wants to know where the hours go, and that's fair. For each team you should know:
- the share of billable and non-billable time,
- travel time,
- care that was delivered but never invoiced,
- hours worked by agency staff and freelancers.

Most of this is already in your planning system and electronic client records. Report it per team, not per person. Register only deviations from the plan, and design the approach with your nurses.

### 3. Move all planning from the teams to one central office

- **Continuity of care is a contract condition, not a nice-to-have.** CZ's 2027 policy requires "a fixed team of care providers for every client" with as few different carers as possible. It also stresses recognisable neighbourhood teams ([CZ](https://zorginkoopbeleid.cz.nl/external/asset/download/project/f9ff770a-0425-0000-f337-a117fb0b90e7/name/Wijkverpleging2027_v12645.pdf)). Central planners optimising across a whole region tend to wear that down.
- **It adds overhead in the very year you need to cut it.** Buurtzorg's teams manage themselves and do their own planning. A 2015 KPMG study reported 8% overhead for Buurtzorg against a sector average of 25% ([Springer, 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41469-024-00184-y)). The figure is old and comes from a source close to Buurtzorg, so treat it with care. But the direction matters: a central office is a new layer of cost.
- **The local knowledge sits in the teams.** Teams know which client is deteriorating, which carer a client with dementia will accept, and who can swap a shift at short notice. Lose that and you get more gaps, more last-minute fixes and more agency staff.
- **You need formal advice first.** This is a major organisational change. The works council has advisory rights on it (art. 25 WOR), and so does the client council (Wmcz 2018).

**What to keep:** Planning does eat nurses' time, and some planning does benefit from scale. Offer central *support* rather than central *control*:
- a good planning tool that suggests routes,
- a shared pool of staff across teams to cover absence,
- central planning for night care and unplanned care,
- a planner shared by several teams, where those teams want one.

## How I would actually close the 7%

**Step 1: break the 7% down.** It's an average. It might be 3% short on one insurer and 15% short on one municipality's contract. Split it by funder, by service (nursing, personal care, domestic help and so on) and by team or region. That tells you where to negotiate, where to cut and where to stop.

**Step 2: work on several fronts at once.** The figures below are only an illustration. They assume total costs of about €40M (650 staff, roughly 450 FTE), so 7% is about €2.8M and each 1% is about €0.4M. Replace them with your own.

| Lever | What it means | Indicative effect | When it lands |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contracts and revenue | Renegotiate on the basis of your cost price; use the Wmo fair-price rules; tackle volume caps; invoice care you already deliver | 2–3% | Partly in 2027 contracts, fully in 2028 |
| Sickness absence | Bring it down from about 9% to about 7.5% | 1–1.5% | 12–24 months |
| Agency staff and freelancers | Move flexible hours to your own staff pool | 1–1.5% | 6–18 months |
| Overhead and admin | Management layers, offices, IT licences, lease cars, purchasing, internal forms and registration | 1–1.5% | 6–12 months |
| Scope choices | Stop or hand over contracts or areas that lose money structurally | Depends on the contract | Next contract cycle |

Together these can close the gap, but not all of it on 1 January.

**Contracts with municipalities.** The Wmo requires a "reële prijs" (fair price). The rules say municipalities must base it on:
- wage costs under the CAO, at your average pay scale,
- reasonable overhead,
- non-productive hours such as leave, illness and training,
- travel and training costs,
- indexation during the contract,
- the cost of meeting the municipality's own requirements.

That fair price is also the floor for tenders ([VNG guide](https://vng.nl/sites/default/files/20170807-handreiking-amvb-reele-kostprijs-wmo.pdf)). Hand in a substantiated cost price. If a contract still falls short, use the formal routes and the political one (aldermen and council members). Bear in mind that municipalities are under pressure themselves: the VNG expects their shortfall to reach €3.2bn in 2028 ([VNG](https://vng.nl/artikelen/gemeenten-vallen-in-financieel-ravijn)).

**Contracts with insurers.** CZ, for example, is giving existing providers one-year contracts for 2027.
- Negotiate on your own cost price, and find out whether the shortfall comes from the rate or from a production cap.
- Ask for multi-year agreements or agreements that fund changes in how you work.
- Use what insurers already want. CZ requires home-care technology for at least 50% of suitable clients, and pays a contribution for hybrid medication dispensing ([CZ](https://zorginkoopbeleid.cz.nl/external/asset/download/project/f9ff770a-0425-0000-f337-a117fb0b90e7/name/Wijkverpleging2027_v12645.pdf)). Technology that removes visits frees up nurse time, and that is worth more than making nurse time cheaper.
- Work with ActiZ or Zorgthuisnl, which are pushing for higher rates and fewer volume caps ([ActiZ manifest](https://www.actiz.nl/sites/default/files/2026-05/Manifest%20Wijkverpleging.pdf)).
- Before each negotiation, decide your walk-away point: which contract you will not sign at what price.

**Care you deliver but don't invoice.** Check this per team. Many organisations deliver more care than they bill for.

**Sickness absence and staff turnover.** With absence around 9%, each percentage point costs you roughly €0.3–0.5M, including the cost of covering the shifts. That makes absence your largest internal lever. It falls when workload goes down and autonomy and support from managers go up, which is the opposite of what the three proposals would do.

**Agency staff and freelancers.** These are your most expensive hours. Build your own flexible pool across teams. CZ also caps subcontracting, freelancers included, at 50% of care.

**Overhead.** Compare your management spans, back office, offices, IT and lease cars with similar organisations. Look into sharing a back office with another provider.

**A bridge.** Most of these levers won't deliver fully by 1 January. Propose a two-year path to a balanced budget, with a limited and clearly defined use of reserves in 2027 and hard milestones. If your bank has covenants on your loans, talk to it early.

## How to handle the board and the organisation

- **Take ownership.** In the Dutch governance model, the executive director runs the organisation, and the supervisory board supervises and approves. Thank the board for its proposals and say you share the goal: a balanced budget by a set date. Tell them you will bring a complete plan by [date], which weighs their three measures against the alternatives on the same criteria.
- **Use the same criteria for every measure:**
  1. the financial effect and when it arrives,
  2. the effect on billable capacity and contract compliance,
  3. the effect on staff retention and absence,
  4. quality and safety,
  5. whether it can be reversed.

  The board's three measures score poorly on criteria 2 to 4. Let the table make that case, not your opinion.
- **Involve people early:** the works council, the client council and, if you have one, your nurses' advisory council (VAR). You need them legally for parts of this anyway. Frontline teams usually know exactly where time and money are wasted, so give them the numbers and ask where they would find their share.
- **Talk to your main insurer and municipality before you decide.** They have a stake in the outcome: if you lose capacity, their waiting lists grow.

## Be honest about the hard part

Renegotiation may show that part of the gap is structural, for example one contract that pays 15% below cost. If so, the right answer may be to get smaller: drop that contract, hand over that area, or combine services with another provider. That hurts. It is still better than spreading the loss across the whole organisation by making everyone's work worse. In a market this short of staff, cutting care staff belongs at the bottom of the list, not the top.
