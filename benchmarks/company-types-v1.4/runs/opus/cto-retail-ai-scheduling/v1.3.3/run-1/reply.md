**Recommend the tool, but not the plan.** Say yes to a 12-week pilot in 8–10 stores from February, designed with the works council and set up for Dutch rules. Say no to 140 stores by March. The 12% is worth going after: on my rough numbers about 17,000 labour hours a week, or about €13.5–18 million a year (assuming an average of 16 hours a week per person at €15–20 an hour all-in; swap in your payroll figures). But two of its three levers, schedules three days ahead and changes on the day, run into your cao and Dutch law, so you can't bank the saving the vendor sold.

**Why not as pitched**

- **Three days' notice breaks the cao.** Both sector supermarket caos (VGL and Levensmiddelenbedrijf, 2024–2026 texts, art. 4.3f) say the full rooster must be published at least one full week ahead, barring the unforeseen. If it's later, staff only have to work their structural hours. A new cao is being negotiated now; on a company cao, check its version.
- **Same-day cuts mostly don't save money.** Fixed-hours staff are paid their contract hours anyway. For small-contract and on-call staff, the caos allow call-ins and withdrawals at 24 hours' notice, only for things unknown when the rooster was made; cancel inside that window and the hours are still paid. Contracts without fixed hours earn at least three hours' pay per call, which doesn't fit 15-minute slots. It only saves money if people "choose" to go home, and that pressure ends up with the works council and in the press.
- **Your workforce has hard limits.** 16- and 17-year-olds may not work nights, on-call duty or overtime. Their limits are 9 hours a shift and 45 a week, school hours included, and they need 13 free Sundays a year. The tool must enforce these as hard rules, not warnings.
- **The works council's consent is required, not just its advice.** Rules on working and rest times, processing of personal data and systems that can monitor performance all need it (WOR art. 27(1)(b), (k) and (l)).
- **It may be high-risk AI.** Allocating shifts on individual behaviour or traits, or deciding terms of work, puts it in Annex III of the AI Act. Informing workers and their representatives before use becomes a duty from 2 December 2027, so build for it now. It also needs a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) before go-live.
- **March means about 12 stores going live a week from mid-January**, since no store wants a new scheduling system in the December peak, while store managers are still being trained. That puts the COO's own target at risk.

**What the evidence says**

In a randomised trial in 28 Gap stores, more stable schedules raised median sales by 7% and labour productivity by 5%. A study of 41 stores found all of them understaffed in a three-hour peak. A good forecast may earn more by moving hours to peaks than by cutting them. Both are US studies; your pilot is what counts.

**What I'd put to the COO**

1. **Pilot in 8–10 stores against matched control stores:** large and small, student-heavy and not.
2. **Set it up for stability.** Publish two weeks ahead and keep shifts at three hours or more. Same-day changes only by choice, and paid as the cao requires. Staff enter their own availability and swap shifts in the app, youth rules are enforced, and managers can override with a logged reason.
3. **No individual performance scores** in shift allocation, and no rankings of staff. Report per store.
4. **Involve the works council from day one**, with the real numbers, while it can still shape the design. With cao talks on, brief the unions too.
5. **Agree the measures per store up front:** sales per labour hour, peak queues, paid-but-unworked hours, days of notice, changes inside 24 hours, turnover, absence and a short staff survey.
6. **Contract for evidence.** Price per store live, an exit after the pilot, and a success fee tied to savings measured against the control stores. Require data export, EU hosting and support for the bandwidth contracts due to replace zero-hours contracts from 2028. Ask how much of the 12% came from forecasting rather than from short notice.
7. **If it works, roll out about 22 stores a month from June**, done before next December's peak. Take the saving through lower hiring (student turnover gives you room) and moving hours to peaks, not by cutting hours people rely on. Spread evenly, 12% is about two hours a week for every employee.

Your part is the due diligence: integration with POS, HR and payroll, forecast accuracy per store, security, exit, the DPIA and the AI Act classification. Frame it as "how we make the 12% real", not a veto.

**The COO's case is real.** The cao offer on the table adds 3.5% in December, the minimum wage rises every January and July, and a 15-minute forecast beats a manager's spreadsheet. That's why the forecast deserves a fast, fair test.

**Questions that would change my answer:** What gap is the 12% meant to close, and by when? Are all 140 stores yours, or are some franchised, with their own employers and works councils? And where has the vendor actually delivered 12%, under which labour rules?
