**My recommendation: say yes to AI demand forecasting, and no to putting 140 stores live by March on the vendor's terms.** Run a 12-week pilot in about 10 stores against 10 matched control stores, under rules agreed with the works council, and roll out in waves after the summer if it holds up. Two numbers are behind this:

- **Three days' notice is shorter than Dutch law's default** of 4 days for on-call staff and 28 days for work patterns, unless your CAO sets another term.
- **12% of your hours is the work of about 1,080 of your 9,000 people**, and most of those hours are students' income.

**What's real in the pitch**

Your COO has a point: labour is your largest controllable cost, and rosters built from habit overstaff quiet mornings and understaff Saturday at five, so a 15-minute forecast per store is worth having. My rough estimate of your store labour bill is €100–170 million a year (9,000 people × 14–18 hours a week × €16–20 an hour including employer costs; swap in finance's figure), so the promise is worth €13–20 million a year. That deserves a proper test, not a dismissal.

**Where the 12% runs into Dutch rules** (not legal advice; check with employment counsel and your CAO)

- **Three days ahead.** The Working Hours Act ([art. 4:2](https://wetten.overheid.nl/jci1.3:c:BWBR0007671&hoofdstuk=4&artikel=4:2)) requires work patterns 28 days ahead, or working times at least 4 days ahead where the work makes that impossible, unless a CAO says otherwise. On-call staff may refuse a call made less than 4 days ahead; a CAO can shorten that to no less than 24 hours ([AWVN](https://www.awvn.nl/hr-van-a-tot-z/arbeidsovereenkomst-oproepovereenkomst/)).
- **Changes on the day.** An on-call shift cancelled or changed inside that window is still paid, so sending people home early saves less than the model assumes.
- **15-minute slots.** Staff on zero-hours or min-max contracts, or under 15 hours without fixed times, get at least 3 hours' pay per call ([Rijksoverheid](https://www.rijksoverheid.nl/themas/werk/arbeidsomstandigheden/vraag-en-antwoord/arbeidsovereenkomst-en-cao/krijg-ik-als-oproepkracht-ook-loon-als-ik-maar-1-of-2-uur-heb-gewerkt)). Short fragments don't get cheaper.
- **Works council.** Working-time rules and systems that can monitor attendance or performance both need its consent (art. 27 WOR); without it, the works council can have the decision declared void. Expect a data protection impact assessment. Under the AI Act, AI that allocates work or monitors staff is probably high-risk; the duty to inform staff starts on 2 December 2027, so not yet, but within this contract's life.
- **Union timing.** If you're under the sector Supermarkets CAO, it expired on 1 April and talks were suspended on 17 November ([FNV, 18 November 2026](https://www.fnv.nl/nieuwsbericht/sectornieuws/handel/2026/11/voortgang-onderhandelingen-cao-supermarkten)). "12% fewer hours" would land in the middle of that.

**The costs you won't see in week one**

The saved hours show up in the store labour budget straight away; lost sales, empty shelves, queues and leavers show up later, in other budgets.

- In a randomised trial in 28 Gap stores, more predictable schedules raised median sales by 7% and labour productivity by 5% ([Williams et al., 2018](https://worklifelaw.org/projects/stable-scheduling-study/report/)).
- In 25 restaurants, servers kept past their shift without warning sold 4.4% less per bill, while a few days' notice made no difference ([Kamalahmadi, Yu and Zhou, *Management Science*, 2021](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/New-Research-Shows-Unpredictable-Work-Schedules-Impact-Restaurant-Revenue)).
- Students pick jobs that fit lectures and exams, and every leaver costs recruiting and training.

March is risky in itself: rosters and payroll for 9,000 people switched straight after the December peak and just before Easter (28 March 2027), when a payroll error is a trust problem, not a bug.

**What I'd propose to the COO**

1. **Now:** don't sign the full rollout. Offer a paid pilot with later-wave prices locked, an exit clause and exportable data. Ask how the 12% was measured (baseline, country, contract types), and for two references from European grocers with many student staff that you choose, not the vendor.
2. **December–January:** bring the central works council in with the real numbers before anything is configured, and start the impact assessment. Agree the rules together: rosters at least two weeks ahead (Gap's standard; check your CAO's minimum), shifts of at least three hours, same-day changes only when staff volunteer through the app, exam weeks respected, store managers keep the final say, and reports per store and team, never rankings of individuals.
3. **February–May:** 12-week pilot in 10 stores against 10 controls, with success criteria fixed in advance: hours per €1,000 of sales, sales, shelf availability, checkout waits, customer scores, flex-staff turnover and absence, shifts changed within 4 days, payroll errors and managers' rostering time.
4. **June:** go or no-go. If go, roll out in waves of 20–30 stores outside peaks. Decide now that real savings come from hiring fewer replacements as students leave, not from cutting contracted hours, and that part goes back into peak staffing.

**A line for the COO, privately first:** "I want the savings too. Give me 10 stores and 12 weeks to prove the number on our stores under Dutch rules, before we bet 140 stores and Easter on a vendor's slide."
