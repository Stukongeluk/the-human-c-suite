Don't adopt the three measures as a package. Close the 7% gap with a mix that starts with the contracts and the organisation's own waste, with leadership taking the first cut, and treat the three proposals as hypotheses to test. The nurse swap and the central planning move are the two that can hurt clients and cost you more than they save.

**First, the numbers I don't have yet.** I haven't seen your accounts, so everything below is an estimate. If staff are roughly 80% of costs (typical for home care, but check yours), a 7% gap is about 9% of payroll. That is far more than a 15% skill-mix change on one group could safely deliver. Three questions would change my answer:
1. What is the gap in euros, and how much reserve (continuity) do you have? That sets how fast you must move.
2. Is it a one-year dip or structural? Are tariffs below cost price for the whole contract, or only for some products (Wmo, Zvw wijkverpleging, Wlz)?
3. What share of your hours is indirect time (travel, admin, planning, rework)? That is where most savings usually hide.

**What I would do, in this order**

1. **Reopen the contracts, with evidence.** Bring a cost-price calculation to each insurer and municipality. Ask for cost-covering tariffs, indexation, or lower administrative demands in return for the lower price. Join other providers in your region, because a shared case is harder to wave away. Also check which contract lines lose money and which are profitable. Decide what to keep, rather than cutting evenly.
2. **Go after waste and travel time.** Sort out route planning, travel time, overhead, vendors, housing, licences and sick-leave costs. Take admin time away from nurses before you take nurses away.
3. **Stop low-value work.** Ask the teams which tasks and reports nobody uses.
4. **Freeze hiring and use natural attrition.** Home care has high turnover, so attrition alone may deliver a large part of the gap over 12 to 18 months, without a single forced exit.
5. **Leaders go first.** Say plainly that this result is the leadership's responsibility. Cut your pay, bonuses and board fees before touching frontline jobs. It is a signal of trust, not a savings plan.
6. **Redesign care in the teams.** Work on skill mix with the nurses, not against them (see below).

**The board's three measures**

| Measure | Saving (estimate) | Main risks | My view |
|---|---|---|---|
| Replace 15% of district nurses with lower-qualified carers | Real but modest, since the pay difference is limited to a part of the workforce | Dutch rules reserve certain tasks (voorbehouden handelingen) for qualified nurses. Complex care needs assessment and indication. Quality and safety incidents, insurer and inspectorate (IGJ) attention, staff leaving. Rehiring qualified nurses in a shortage is slow and expensive | Not as a target number. Let teams decide task by task what a carer can safely do, with nurses leading. Do it through attrition, not dismissal |
| App registering every visit to the minute | Small. It mainly shows what you already bill | Without a clear reason it reads as distrust. Care is not minutes: clients with dementia or at the end of life need time. Staff register to the minute and quietly cut the conversation | Don't introduce it as monitoring. If registration is needed for invoicing and for proving to insurers that you deliver what they pay for, keep the legal minimum, use the data for teams and never for ranking individuals |
| Move all planning from teams to a central office | Possible, but unproven | Self-organising neighbourhood teams are the reason many Dutch providers are efficient. Centralising planning breaks continuity of care, increases travel, and loses local knowledge. Hard to reverse | Don't. Offer teams better tools and clear cost targets instead. If central help is useful, pilot it in one team and measure |

I'd also check the evidence: Buurtzorg-style self-managing teams have repeatedly shown lower overhead and higher staff and client satisfaction than central models in Dutch studies. Verify the current figures for your own board paper.

**Dutch law and process (not legal advice, check with counsel)**
- With 650 staff you must have a works council (OR). Reorganisation falls under its advisory right (art. 25 WOR). A system suited to monitoring attendance or performance, such as the visit-registration app, needs its consent (art. 27(1)(l) WOR). Bring the OR in now, at the problem stage, with the real numbers, and not after the board has decided. A plan changed by the OR is a better plan, not a delay. Also involve the unions under your CAO.
- The AVG (GDPR) applies to the app's data. You need a clear purpose and a privacy assessment.
- If dismissals ever become unavoidable: 20 or more in one UWV region within three months triggers the collective-dismissal procedure with UWV and unions, plus transition payments. Treat that as a last resort.

**Transition at human speed.** Phase it: pilot in two or three teams, learn, then expand. Give paid learning time. Tell staff through their own team leader first, with the real numbers, what is decided and what is open. Tell them what you will do for them.

**What would change my mind.** If the contracts can't be reopened, attrition and efficiency fall well short, and reserves run out within a year, you may need harder steps. Then we'd go through temporary measures and voluntary exits before any involuntary ones, and plan it once and decently.

**What I'd bring to the board.** One page: the real gap in euros, the options above with timing and savings, the effect on clients and staff, your own pay cut, and the indicators we will track every quarter (client complaints and incidents, waiting times, absence, turnover, travel time, financial result).
