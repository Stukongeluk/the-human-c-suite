# A 7% gap: what I would do

**Short version:** don't accept the board's three measures as a package. On the numbers they probably won't close the gap, and each one carries a risk that could cost you more than it saves. Treat the 7% as a problem to diagnose, negotiate and solve in several steps. Bring the board a different plan, and bring it quickly.

## 1. Check the size of the problem first

- Confirm the 7% is real. Is it the gap between contract value and *full* cost, or between this year's and next year's contract? Is it final, or still open in negotiation? Which contracts (insurers, municipalities, Wmo, Zvw) drive it, and which are loss-making on their own?
- Do a rough sum with your own figures. As an illustration only: 650 staff at about €60k all-in is roughly €39M of payroll. If payroll is ~80% of costs, total costs are ~€49M and 7% is ~€3.4M. If district nurses are around a quarter of staff (~160) and you replace 15% of them (~24), the saving is the pay difference between level 5 and level 3, perhaps €10-15k each. That is about €0.25-0.35M, **under 10% of the gap**. Replace these numbers with your actuals, but expect the same order of magnitude.
- Treat the board's three measures as three hypotheses. Ask them what saving each is supposed to deliver, by when, and what the risks are. If they can't say, that is your first finding.

## 2. Why each proposal is weaker than it looks

**Replacing 15% of district nurses with lower-qualified carers**
- Small saving (see above), large risk. Indication-setting, complex and clinical care and the reserved procedures (Wet BIG) need qualified nurses. Your remaining nurses will carry more supervision and escalation, so the saving partly reappears as workload, sickness absence and attrition.
- It is a reorganisation: works council advice (WOR art. 25), union consultation under the CAO, redundancy rules and probably a collective-dismissal notification. And you would be letting qualified nurses go in the tightest care labour market in decades. Many will be hired by a competitor within weeks.
- It exposes you to the Inspectie (IGJ), insurers' quality criteria and your own reputation. One serious incident costs more than the saving.
- Better version: **skill-mix by task, not by headcount**. Look at which visits really need level 5, which can move to level 3-4, and design it through natural attrition and re-deployment, not dismissals.

**An app that registers every visit to the minute**
- Registration itself is not the problem. Zvw district nursing is billed on time spent, so accurate registration protects revenue and defends you in audits. If you currently under-register or have write-offs, that is real money.
- Minute-by-minute control is a different thing. It adds admin time for carers, pushes behaviour toward the clock rather than the client, and is a known driver of dissatisfaction and leaving. It also triggers works council consent (WOR art. 27) and GDPR (AVG) questions about monitoring staff.
- Better version: simple, low-effort registration (ideally automatic), used for billing integrity and for team-level insight into travel time, no-shows and overruns. No individual surveillance scoreboards. Agree the purpose and the data rules with the works council up front.

**Moving all planning from the teams to one central office**
- Central scheduling can improve route efficiency and cover for absence. But in home care, continuity of the carer and the team's local knowledge are a large part of the quality and of the retention. Teams that lose control over their own schedule tend to lose their best people.
- It also removes the main lever self-organising teams use to resolve problems cheaply. Planning moved centrally often comes with a *bigger* central planning department, which adds cost.
- Better version: **shared planning tools and a central support desk, with the team still owning the schedule**. Pilot it in two or three teams, measure travel time, continuity and sickness absence, and only then decide whether to scale.

## 3. What I would do instead (in order)

1. **Negotiate before you cut.** Insurers and municipalities have an interest in you staying in business. Go in with open-book cost data, show what 7% below cost means for access to care in the region, and ask for tariff or indexation adjustments, longer-term contracts, or a narrower scope. Look for what they are willing to pay for (e.g. prevention, complex care, hospital-at-home, shorter hospital stays). Bring the board into those conversations.
2. **Drop or reprice what loses money.** Contract by contract and service by service. Sometimes the right answer to a loss-making contract is to hand it back or reduce volume.
3. **Attack the big non-care cost lines.**
   - **Sickness absence and flex spend.** In care these are usually the biggest hidden costs. Each percentage point of sickness absence, and each agency/ZZP hour at a premium, is worth more than most efficiency measures. Retention and a healthy roster *are* a cost strategy.
   - **Travel time and unbilled time**, through better routing and clustering of clients, not by timing carers.
   - **Overhead and housing**: management layers, offices, IT licences, shared back office with a neighbouring organisation (or a merger conversation if the gap is structural).
4. **Use attrition and a controlled hiring freeze** for non-clinical roles before touching anyone's job. A natural 8-10% annual turnover absorbs a lot without a single dismissal.
5. **Look at your balance sheet.** As a non-profit you may have a continuity reserve. Using part of it to bridge a 12-18 month transition while the structural fixes take hold can be the right call. Agree the limit with the board explicitly.
6. **Make smaller, targeted skill-mix and planning changes** (see above), piloted and evidence-based, as part of the package, not as the headline.

## 4. How to handle the board and the organisation

- Ask the board for a **decision on principles, not on measures**: what you will protect (quality, continuity, no compulsory dismissals unless unavoidable) and what you may trade. The board sets direction and limits; you design the route.
- Come back in 4-6 weeks with a **gap bridge**: a table showing each measure, the euro value, the timing, the risk and what you need from whom, adding up to 7%.
- Involve the **works council and team leaders early**, before any decision. They will see the risks fastest, and you need their consent on at least two of the three proposals anyway.
- Tell staff plainly what is happening and what is not decided. Rumours of "15% of nurses replaced" will cost you resignations before anything is implemented.
- Set **quality guardrails** that trigger a review: client complaints, incidents, continuity of carer, sickness absence, staff turnover. If they move the wrong way, the savings plan changes.

## 5. Bottom line

Do not sign off the three measures as proposed. The skill-mix swap saves little and carries high risk; minute-level registration is useful only in a lighter form; central planning is a pilot, not a policy. The real work is in contract negotiation, loss-making services, absence and flex costs, and overhead, with carefully designed changes on top.
