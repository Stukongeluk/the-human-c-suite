Don't agree to a 30% cut this month, and don't just refuse it either. Your investors are right that nine months of runway against a summer 2027 Series B is too thin. They are wrong about the size and the timing, and the cut alone doesn't fix the problem. What I'd do is take control of the plan: put a cash plan, a bridge and a pre-agreed trigger in front of the board within about two weeks, so the argument is no longer "cut or don't cut".

**1. The real problem is the gap, not headcount**

Nine months of runway ends around August 2027. A Series B that starts in summer takes four to six months to close, and you will have to raise from a position of weakness. You want 15 to 18 months of cash from today, or at least a credible line of sight to it. That means extending runway by 6 to 9 months, and a cut is only one of the ways to do that.

**2. Why a 30% cut this month doesn't do what they think**

- **Sweden slows it down.** You can't decide and execute in a month. Expect a union consultation first if you have a collective agreement (MBL), a notification to Arbetsförmedlingen before notices go out (for 5 to 25 people this is roughly two months, but check with counsel), then notice periods of one to six months depending on tenure and contract. Cash savings from a decision today start somewhere around March to May.
- **Illustrative math (replace with your numbers).** Say people are about 75% of burn. Cutting 30% of staff saves about 22% of monthly burn, but only from month 4 or 5. Against nine months of cash, that buys roughly one extra month, before any severance, retention costs or lost output.
- **LAS seniority rules (turordning)** mean you can't simply choose who stays. Last-in-first-out tends to remove the people you most recently hired for growth. You can negotiate exceptions or use voluntary packages, but this has to be planned early.
- **Growth risk.** 4% a month compounds to about 60% a year. If a deep cut slows growth to 2%, revenue in nine months is about 1.20x instead of 1.42x, and that weakens the Series B story you are trying to protect. Also, 4% a month is decent but not exceptional for a Series B. Investors will care a lot about burn multiple and efficiency, so your cost discipline is part of the pitch.

**3. What I'd do instead**

*This week: build the real model.* Cash, monthly net burn, revenue by month, gross margin, committed hires and contracts, and receivables. Run three cases: current plan, moderate savings, and a deeper cut with Swedish timelines built in. The key question is when revenue plus savings plus any financing gets you to 15 months or more.

*Next 30 days: do the cheap and fast things first.*
- Hiring freeze and no backfills without CEO sign-off.
- Non-people costs: cloud, vendors, tools, contractors, office, travel.
- Leadership pay cut or deferral, including yours. It's the credible signal and costs the team nothing.
- Cut or defer projects that do not feed revenue or the Series B narrative.
- A voluntary-departure package. It is cheaper and kinder than forced notices, and some people are already looking.

*In parallel: extend the runway with money, not only cuts.*
- Ask the investors directly: will you fund a bridge (a convertible or extension) at pro-rata? If they want a 30% cut but won't commit capital, they are asking you and your team to carry all of the risk. This is a fair question, and the answer tells you a lot.
- Non-dilutive funding fits climate tech well: Vinnova, the Swedish Energy Agency, Almi, EU programs such as the EIC Accelerator. They are slow, so start now.
- Venture debt or revenue-based financing, if your recurring revenue supports it. Customer prepayments or annual-upfront contracts can also help.

*Agree a tripwire with the board.* This is the most useful thing you can offer the two investors. Something like: "If by [end of February] we haven't signed a bridge or hit [revenue/burn milestone], we execute a reduction of X to reach Y months of runway." Work back from Swedish lead times, because you may need to start the consultation and legal preparation weeks before the trigger date. Prepare the plan now, who and which roles and what it costs, without announcing it. That gives investors the certainty they want without you cutting 14 people on spec.

**4. Handling the two investors**

- Find out why they want 30%. It may be their fund's reserve policy, a view that the Series B market will be tough, or a wish to force a decision before a bridge is needed. The answer changes your response.
- Check the governance. Do these two investors have a board majority or protective provisions that let them force this? Or is it pressure rather than a vote? Get your shareholders' agreement and board composition clear before you respond.
- Show them your own number. A credible plan with a smaller, earlier, targeted reduction plus a trigger is usually more persuasive than a refusal.

**5. If you do end up cutting**

Make it once, make it deep enough, and tie it to strategy rather than just percentages. Repeated rounds destroy trust and your best people leave first. Get Swedish employment counsel involved early, be open with the team about the situation and the plan before rumours do it for you, and offer real help to those leaving. If the cash horizon drops below about 3 to 6 months with no financing in sight, talk to counsel about the board's duties in Sweden (including the rules on capital shortfalls) and take advice on your own position as well.
