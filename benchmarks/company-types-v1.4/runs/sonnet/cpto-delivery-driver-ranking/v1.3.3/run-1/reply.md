Don't ship it as specced. Say yes to the goal (more stops per hour) and no to a live individual leaderboard plus a weekly bottom-10% flag. Offer a team-level, route-adjusted pilot with a control group instead. The 8% is a promise about a number, not about parcels delivered well, and the design guarantees side effects you will pay for.

**Why the design fails**

- **"Bottom 10%" is a rank, not a finding.** Some drivers are always in the bottom tenth, even if everyone is excellent. Stops per hour mostly measures the route: dense city centre against rural Flanders, apartment blocks against houses, heavy parcels, failed access, traffic. Unadjusted, you will flag the people with the hardest routes.
- **People optimise what is ranked.** Expect fewer careful attempts, parcels left in unsafe places, "delivered" scans before the doorstep, rushed parking and driving. That shows up as damage claims, complaints, redeliveries and road incidents, and none of it is in the leaderboard. Any speed metric needs quality and safety metrics beside it.
- **Two thirds of your drivers aren't yours.** Sending named driver performance to a subcontractor makes you a source of employment decisions about people you don't employ. Subcontractors can fire, cut hours or drop drivers on the back of it. Expect the good drivers to move to a competitor with a friendlier app, and expect press attention in a sector that already gets scrutiny.

**Legal and consultation, to check with counsel before anything is built**

I'm not giving legal advice, but these need an answer first:

- A system that monitors or evaluates individual drivers usually needs works council consent in the Netherlands (WOR art. 27(1)(l)), and Belgium has its own information and consultation rules. That applies to your own staff and likely to the subcontractors' too.
- Per-driver data sent to a third party raises GDPR questions: purpose, proportionality, who sees what.
- Detailed control over subcontractor staff can undermine the contractor relationship, and chain liability rules come into play.
- Algorithmic evaluation of workers is a high-risk use under the AI Act, with employer duties from December 2027. The EU Platform Work Directive may be relevant too; check whether it applies to you.

**What I'd propose to Operations**

1. **Start from the problem.** What is the productivity gap, where is it, and what causes it? Ask where the 8% comes from: a pilot, a vendor claim, another company? Ask what happened to quality in that case. My guess is that route planning, depot loading order, failed first attempts, app friction and access problems hold more of the 8% than driver effort does. That's worth checking against your own data.
2. **Pilot, don't roll out.** Pick a few depots as pilots and match them with comparable control depots. Run it 8 to 12 weeks, reversible and with a stop rule. Compare stops per hour against the depot's own history, not against other depots.
3. **Change what is measured.** Use route-adjusted performance (stops per hour against what the route should allow) at depot and subcontractor level. Put quality beside it: first-attempt success, damage and complaint rate, safety incidents, driver turnover. Subcontractors see their own team's trend, not named drivers.
4. **Give drivers their own numbers, privately.** Showing a driver their own stats and route feedback helps. Public ranking mostly breeds resentment.
5. **If a subcontractor has a real concern about an individual,** it goes through them: clear expectations, support, a fair chance, and evidence from more than one number.
6. **Involve people before building.** Take it to the works council and subcontractor representatives, and ask some drivers to help design it. They know why a route is slow, and their objections are free risk analysis.

**What I'd say to Operations**

"We want the same thing. A live ranking of drivers on stops per hour will move that number and also move damage, complaints and safety, and it puts us in legal territory with subcontractor staff. Let's run a route-adjusted, team-level pilot with a control group and quality measured alongside. If it shows a real gain without the side effects, we scale it."

**Two questions that would change my answer**

- What is the evidence behind the 8%, and what was measured next to it?
- Which agreements and works councils (yours and the subcontractors') cover driver monitoring today?

My assumptions: the gap is partly structural, subcontractor contracts don't already allow this kind of data sharing, and you have route and quality data to compare. What am I missing, and what would make this wrong?
