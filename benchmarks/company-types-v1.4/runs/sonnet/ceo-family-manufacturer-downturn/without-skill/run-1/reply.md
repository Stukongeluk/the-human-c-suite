Don't commit to the 45 yet, and don't rule out all job cuts either. Your grandfather's rule is a statement of values, not a plan, and the question is how to protect as many of the 210 jobs as possible over the next five years. The bank wants a credible plan by February, and a credible plan is not the same as a headcount number. Here is how I would approach it.

**1. Find out what is cyclical and what is structural**

Your adviser's 45 probably comes from one calculation: revenue down 35% at two customers, so cut cost in proportion. That mixes two different problems.

- **Customer A, which expects to recover.** This is a cyclical dip. You bridge it, and you should not destroy skills you will need in 18 months.
- **Customer B, which is moving to electric drivetrains.** This is structural. Part of that volume is not coming back, and a bridge only delays the reckoning.

Before anything else, get these numbers in a one-page table:

- Revenue share of each of the two customers, and gross margin on that work.
- Order book and backlog by quarter for the next 12 to 18 months.
- A 13-week cash-flow forecast, and a monthly one through 2027.
- Where the 45 comes from: which roles, which cost per head, what saving, and over what time.

Ask your adviser to show the arithmetic behind 45. If it is a percentage applied to payroll, it is a rule of thumb and not an analysis. Also ask for a sensitivity: what does the plan look like if Customer A recovers half, fully, or not at all?

**2. Check your liquidity position first**

Check this before anything else, because it changes how much room you have. As managing director you have personal duties under German insolvency law (§ 15a InsO) if you become illiquid or over-indebted, and personal liability if you ignore them. Have your Steuerberater or an insolvency-experienced adviser confirm where you stand, including a forward look. If you are comfortably above water, you have time to do this properly. If the 13-week forecast looks tight, tell the bank earlier and get professional restructuring help now. The StaRUG framework exists for companies in exactly your position, before insolvency.

**3. Use the levers that protect jobs, in this order**

1. **Kurzarbeit (short-time work).** This is the standard instrument for a temporary drop in orders. Check the current rules with the Agentur für Arbeit: the maximum duration and conditions have changed in recent years, and the rules for structural rather than cyclical loss of work are stricter. It works well for the cyclical part and badly as a cover for a permanent loss, so use it for the Customer A dip and not to delay a decision about Customer B. Talk to your Betriebsrat (if you have one) and, if you are tariff-bound, to IG Metall or Südwestmetall about the agreements that apply to you.
2. **Working-time flexibility.** Run down Arbeitszeitkonten and overtime, take vacation balances, and end temporary agency work and fixed-term contracts as they expire.
3. **Natural attrition.** Freeze external hiring except for critical skills. At a company with a mature workforce, retirements and resignations can contribute a meaningful share of the reduction without a single dismissal. Add Altersteilzeit or early-retirement offers where they make financial sense.
4. **Voluntary leave programmes.** Offer voluntary exits with a fair severance. People who want to go, for example those close to retirement, are better placed than people you pick.
5. **Cost measures that are not about people.** Look at the whole cost base: rent, energy, maintenance, purchasing, inventory, and subcontracted work you could bring back in-house to fill your own capacity.
6. **Management sharing the burden.** Cut your own pay and your management team's before you ask the shop floor for anything, and say so publicly. If the family has dividends, suspend them. If the family can put in capital or a subordinated shareholder loan, that is exactly what a bank wants to see, because it shows that the owners are carrying risk as well.

**4. Make the permanent reduction smaller, targeted and fair**

After those levers, if your analysis shows that part of Customer B's volume is permanently gone, a limited reduction may be unavoidable. It will almost certainly be smaller than 45 if you have used the levers above, and it should be sized to the structural gap, not the whole dip.

If you do it:

- **Involve the Betriebsrat early.** For redundancies of this size, expect an Interessenausgleich and Sozialplan negotiation. Cutting 45 of 210 (about 21%) also triggers the mass-redundancy notification to the Agentur für Arbeit (§ 17 KSchG). Do not issue notices before the formal steps are done, because mistakes there can invalidate the dismissals.
- **Get a specialist employment lawyer.** Sozialauswahl (the social selection criteria: tenure, age, dependants, disability) limits whom you can choose, and "keep the best performers" is only partly allowed. A badly run process can cost you more than the saving.
- **Design the severance and support properly.** Consider a Transfergesellschaft, outplacement, and help finding jobs in the Stuttgart region, where other manufacturers and suppliers are hiring for some skills. People leave better and your remaining team sees that you did it decently.
- **Do it once.** Repeated rounds destroy trust and productivity. Size it so you are not back in six months.

**5. Protect the apprentices**

Your 14 apprentices are your future skilled workforce, and in a labour market with a shortage of skilled workers, losing them is expensive. In practice most are also protected by law during training. Commit publicly to completing every apprenticeship, and think about retention after qualification. Ask whether any can be used productively on service or in-house projects while production is quiet.

**6. Build the new business, not just the smaller old one**

The bank will ask what the company looks like in 2028. A machine builder with two automotive customers whose volumes are shifting needs an answer:

- **Existing customers' new needs.** Electric drivetrains need different machines: battery, e-motor, power-electronics and thermal-management assembly and test. Ask Customer B what it will need to buy, because they may be your first customer for the new generation.
- **Other sectors** that use similar competencies: medical, energy, rail, packaging, defence and general automation. Do not chase all of them. Pick two for which you already have the skills.
- **Service, retrofit and spare parts.** These bring steadier revenue and keep skilled people employed through a downturn.
- **Reduce concentration.** If two customers can reduce your revenue by a third, no restructuring will fix that. A target such as no customer above 20% belongs in the plan.

Talk to those customers and to three or four prospects in the next month. The bank will give far more weight to a signed development agreement or a quote pipeline than to a slide.

**7. What to give the bank**

Prepare a restructuring concept that covers: the diagnosis (structural versus cyclical), a liquidity forecast with scenarios, the measures you have started and their effect, owner contribution, and a realistic business plan. For a financing of this size the bank may ask for an independent review along the lines of an IDW S6 restructuring opinion. Ask early, and ask whether they want it.

Talk to the bank now instead of in January. Banks react much better to an owner who says "here is what I see and what I am doing" than to one who delivers a plan at the last moment. Ask about Kurzarbeit, the Bürgschaftsbank Baden-Württemberg and KfW or L-Bank programmes as supports to the credit line. A guarantee may help if the bank hesitates.

Also remember that a bank wants a credible plan, and not specifically layoffs. A plan with 45 dismissals and no new business is weaker than one with 20 to 25 reductions through attrition and voluntary exits, Kurzarbeit for the cyclical part, and signed new-business leads.

**8. On your grandfather**

Be honest with yourself and your people about this. He never laid anyone off, but he also did not face a customer going electric. What you owe the people at the company is not an unbroken record, but a business that is still here in ten years and an honest process. A company that is too slow and fails costs all 210 people their jobs, not 45. If you eventually conclude a smaller reduction is needed, you can still honour his principle: exhaust the alternatives first, share the burden from the top, treat people fairly, and tell them the truth.

Tell your employees what is going on, in person, before the rumours do it for you. Hold a Betriebsversammlung or a team meeting, explain the numbers, what you are doing first, and what is not yet decided. People can cope with bad news better than with silence.

**A suggested timeline**

- **This week:** 13-week cash forecast, check of liquidity and legal obligations, request the arithmetic behind the 45.
- **Weeks 1 to 3:** customer conversations, Kurzarbeit eligibility, Betriebsrat briefing, brief your bank relationship manager.
- **Weeks 3 to 6:** scenario model, decide on the structural gap, retain an employment lawyer, and an independent adviser if needed.
- **By mid-December:** draft restructuring concept, including the owner contribution.
- **January:** negotiations with Betriebsrat if reductions are needed, final plan to the bank.

I am not a lawyer or a certified restructuring adviser. German dismissal, Kurzarbeit and insolvency rules are detailed and change, so have an Arbeitsrechtler and your Steuerberater check the specifics, and verify current Kurzarbeit terms with the Agentur für Arbeit before relying on them. The decision is yours, but I would not sign off on a number like 45 until the cyclical-versus-structural split and the liquidity picture are on paper.
