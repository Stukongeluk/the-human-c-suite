Don't sign off on 45 dismissals now, and don't promise the bank "no layoffs" either. Give the bank a staged plan: leadership and spending first, a short-time bridge for the part of the drop that will pass, and a smaller, targeted capacity decision for the part that won't, taken once and on evidence. Your adviser has given you a number, not a diagnosis. 45 is 21% of 210, and nobody has yet shown that this is the size of your problem.

**Your grandfather's rule is not the test.** The test is whether this firm is still here, with its skilled people, in ten years. Family firms that plan for the next generation tend to avoid layoffs and hold up better in downturns (Kachaner, Stalk and Bloch, HBR 2012). The reason is that they borrow less, run frugally and keep their skills, not that they never cut. If one customer's volumes are gone for good, some capacity may have to go too, and you should say that plainly to yourself and to your people.

**Two different problems are mixed together**
- A dip that will pass: the 35% drop, as far as the customer that expects to recover is concerned.
- A structural loss: the customer moving to electric drivetrains. Short-time work is the wrong tool here. Germany's IAB warned in 2026 that it can delay changes a company needs to make.

I can't size either one yet. I'm missing what share of revenue and margin these two customers are, and your 13-week cash forecast and covenant position. The adviser's 45 only makes sense once those are known.

**What I would do before February**
1. **Leaders go first.** Say in writing that the loss of two customers is a leadership and concentration risk, and that it is yours to own. Cut your own pay, the dividend and the management bonuses before anyone else's job. In a family firm this is the strongest signal you have.
2. **Take the cost of waste and spend out.** Energy, scrap, rework, overtime, subcontractors, stock and tooling for lost volumes. Freeze hiring and let attrition run.
3. **Bridge the dip with hours, not exits.** Ask your adviser and the Agentur für Arbeit whether Kurzarbeit is open to you. If you have a Betriebsrat, it needs its agreement (§87 BetrVG). Use the freed time for training and for the maintenance and tooling work you have postponed. For the structural part, ask about qualification support and a transfer arrangement, so that any later exits are funded retraining, not a gap.
4. **Fix the revenue side.** A 35% drop at two customers is a concentration problem. Put real effort into new sectors that use your skills (energy, rail, medical, automation, service and retrofit work). The VDMA and your IHK can help you find them.
5. **Keep the 14 apprentices.** You cannot buy this skill pipeline back later.
6. **Bring in the Betriebsrat now,** if you have one, with the real numbers and the options, not a finished plan. Under §§111-112 BetrVG a reduction of this size is an operational change that needs negotiation and a social plan. §17 KSchG also requires notifying the employment agency in advance when 25+ people or 10% of an establishment of this size are dismissed. Your employment lawyer has to confirm all of this; I am not giving legal advice.
7. **Write the bank plan around triggers, not a headcount.** Three scenarios (customers recover, stay down 35%, one stays lower for good), monthly cash for each, the levers above with dates and euro amounts, and a clear decision point. Example: "if order intake from customer B is not above X by 31 March, we start the capacity reduction in Q2." Banks accept a credible plan with a trigger. Show them the leadership cuts first.

**If cuts do turn out to be unavoidable,** do it once, deep enough that you don't have to repeat it, and cut work along with roles so the people who stay aren't given the same workload with fewer colleagues. Start with voluntary exits and early retirement, offer internal moves first, and fund coaching, training and job search from the day of notice, not after the last day.

**Pushback I'd expect.** Your adviser or the bank may say that waiting costs money every month and that a visible cut reassures lenders. That is a fair point. The answer is the dated trigger and the cash scenarios, which let you act fast if the numbers say so. Honestly, if the two customers are most of your revenue and the structural loss is large, the final number could end up near the adviser's. What should differ is how it was reached and who carries it.
