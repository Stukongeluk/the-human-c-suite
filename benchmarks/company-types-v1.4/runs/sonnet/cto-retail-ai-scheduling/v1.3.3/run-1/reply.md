**Recommend a gated pilot, not "all stores live by March": about 10 to 12 stores from March, with the works council involved now, and a chain-wide go/no-go in the autumn on results you measured yourselves.** The 12% is the vendor's number, not yours. The part of the plan most likely to fail is the legal and people side, and neither the vendor nor your COO has put a date on it.

**Why not March for all 140**

1. **Where the 12% comes from.** With a workforce that is mostly students on flexible contracts, almost all of the saving lands on their hours, and for them hours are their income. That is a pay cut for the people with the least protection, so it should be decided openly and not left as a by-product of a scheduling tool. Many of those hours can fall through attrition and fewer slack hours, and students churn fast. But the plan should say that, with a number, and not hide it inside "efficiency".
2. **Three-day publication and on-the-day changes may not be legal for your flexible staff.** As I understand Dutch law, on-call workers (oproepkrachten) must be called in at least four days ahead. Inside that window they can refuse the shift, and a shift you cancel late is still paid. Your CAO may set stricter roster rules. Please have employment counsel confirm this, because it could undermine the tool's central promise. The Working Time Act also limits rest periods and evening and Sunday work, and some of your students will be under 18.
3. **Works council consent.** The Dutch works council has a consent right (WOR art. 27) on arrangements for working and rest times and on systems that can monitor attendance, behaviour or performance. A scheduling tool that scores and adjusts shifts almost certainly falls under it. It also gives advice on the reorganisation as a whole (art. 25). Consent after a contract is signed gives them nothing to decide. Involve them now with the real numbers; their objections are free risk analysis. Under the EU AI Act, task allocation and worker monitoring count as high-risk, with employer information duties from December 2027 (moved from 2026 by the Digital Omnibus). Do the data protection impact assessment now.
4. **A March go-live collides with Easter (28 March 2027)** and with a vendor-paced rollout of 140 stores, which means a heroic effort for store managers and your own team.

**What I'd propose instead**

- **Frame the problem first.** Is the goal lower labour cost per euro of sales, or better availability and fewer queues? Forecast-matched staffing can deliver both, but only if service is protected.
- **Test the claim on your data before signing.** Back-test the forecast on your own 15-minute sales history, store by store, against your current method. Ask the vendor for references from chains of similar size in Europe that they did not pick. Their savings figure should come from control stores, not before-and-after comparisons.
- **Pilot with controls.** Choose stores across formats, sizes and student shares, with matched comparison stores. Success criteria are fixed in advance: hours per sales euro, queue time, on-shelf availability, fresh waste, sickness absence, resignations and schedule stability. We measure stores, never individual employees, and no worker is ranked by the tool.
- **Redesign the rules, not only the software.** Publish schedules 10 to 14 days ahead, with three days as the minimum notice for changes. Changes on the day should be offers workers can accept (shift swaps, extra-hours pick-ups), not impositions. Set a minimum shift length, a limit on split shifts, and let students enter their class timetables and exam weeks as constraints. A 15-minute optimiser produces fragmented shifts unless you forbid them.
- **Keep a human in charge.** Store managers can override, and the override rate is a signal about the model, not about the manager.
- **Contract terms.** A pilot-with-exit clause tied to the criteria above, pricing per live store, ownership of your data, integration with POS, payroll and CAO surcharges costed in, and no go-live commitment dates until the pilot passes. Have the evaluation criteria and weights agreed before any scoring, and have the COO and the vendor relationship declared as interests. "Keep our current scheduling" and "do nothing yet" stay on the table as real options.

**Seats**

- *CEO view:* labour is your biggest controllable cost and margins are thin, so the COO is right that demand-matched scheduling is worth real money. It also puts reputation and 9,000 people's goodwill at stake. Student turnover is expensive to replace.
- *Product and customer view:* queues and empty shelves cost sales that the labour saving never shows. Count that on the same page.

**What would change my mind:** pilot stores that hit the hours target while queues, availability and resignations stay flat or improve; counsel confirming the notice rules work for your contracts; the works council consenting. If those happen, accelerate the waves, and avoid December. If the saving only appears in student hours with worse service, stop.
