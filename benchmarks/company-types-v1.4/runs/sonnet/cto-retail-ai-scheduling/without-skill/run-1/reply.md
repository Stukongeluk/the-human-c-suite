# Recommendation: pilot first, do not go live in all 140 stores by March

**Recommend a staged rollout instead of the vendor's March date.** Run a 10-15 store pilot, with matched control stores, from roughly March to June. Make the full rollout conditional on pre-agreed gates, and aim for an H2 completion. Do not reject the tool. The core idea (forecast-matched scheduling) is sound and common in retail. The proposed design has two problems: the March deadline is the vendor's, not yours, and the plan as described probably breaks Dutch rules.

## 1. Likely legal blockers (check with Legal and HR before any commitment)

I am not your lawyer, so have these confirmed. Each one could stop a March go-live on its own.

- **Three-day publication versus the oproeptermijn.** For on-call (oproep) contracts, the Wet arbeidsmarkt in balans requires the employer to call the worker in at least 4 days ahead. If you call later, the worker may refuse without losing pay. If you cancel inside the window, the shift is payable. Publishing three days ahead and adjusting on the day would trigger this at scale for any student on an oproepcontract. Your CAO may set its own roster rules, so check the supermarket CAO and your own arrangements. Either the tool's defaults change, or you carry a real wage cost that erodes the 12%.
- **On-the-day shift changes.** The same issue applies, plus Arbeidstijdenwet rules on rest periods and breaks, and the specific protections for under-18s and for night and Sunday work. The tool must treat these as hard constraints, not preferences.
- **Works council (OR).** Under the Wet op de ondernemingsraden (art. 27), a working-hours regime, a personnel-data system and employee monitoring all need OR consent. With 9,000 staff you will have an OR. A roll-out the OR has not agreed to can be challenged and halted, and a vendor-imposed March date makes a good-faith process very hard. Involve them now.
- **GDPR (AVG).** The tool processes personal data on 9,000 people, including availability, attendance and possibly performance. You need a DPIA, a clear lawful basis, data-processing terms with the vendor, and an answer on where the data and models sit.
- **EU AI Act.** AI used for task allocation and for monitoring or evaluating workers is listed as high-risk. Obligations include human oversight, logging, transparency to workers, and a deployer's duty to inform worker representatives. The application dates have been subject to change, so ask Legal for the current timeline. In any case, you want vendor contract terms that assign compliance duties and give you audit rights.

## 2. The 12% claim is not yet evidence

- It is a vendor figure. Ask for how it was measured: which customers, which baseline, what happened to service levels, sales per hour, sickness and turnover, and for how long.
- Savings from tighter 15-minute matching come from removing slack. In a supermarket, slack is also what absorbs a delivery arriving late, a busy checkout, a spill or a short-notice absence. Cutting it can show up later as queues, empty shelves, lower availability and burnout.
- Your workforce is mostly students on flexible contracts, so the labour saving can arrive as fewer and more fragmented shifts. Predictable, adequate hours are why students work for you. If fragmented rosters raise turnover, then recruitment and training cost will erode the saving. Many of your people also have commitments such as lectures and exams, so a roster published three days ahead is hard to plan a life around.
- Treat 12% as an upper bound to test, not a budget line. Do not let it become a headcount or hours target in the COO's plan before the pilot reports.

## 3. Proposed approach

**Phase 0 (now to about mid-January): prepare**
- Brief the OR, start formal consent, and run the DPIA.
- Get Legal's written view on the oproeptermijn, the CAO and the AI Act.
- Ask the vendor for references from comparable European grocers (not US ones), their measurement method, and a contract with a pilot exit clause. Do not sign a 140-store commitment before the pilot.
- Agree the pilot design: success metrics and the stop conditions.

**Phase 1 (March to June): pilot in 10-15 stores**
- Choose a mix of store sizes, regions and student share, with matched control stores that keep the current roster process.
- Configure the tool for a 4-day-plus publication window for oproep staff, or an explicit and costed exception. Do not accept the vendor defaults.
- On-the-day changes are voluntary offers that staff may decline, with the store manager able to override. Nothing is auto-assigned.
- Let managers see why the tool proposes each roster and let them edit it. Log their overrides, because a high override rate is useful signal about forecast quality.

**Gates to pass before scaling** (agree these with the COO up front)
1. Labour-hour saving versus control stores, measured net of extra cost from late-notice rules.
2. No degradation in availability, queue times, customer satisfaction or shrink.
3. Staff measures: voluntary turnover, shift-decline rate, complaints, and a short pulse survey.
4. Forecast accuracy by store and department, and the manager override rate.
5. OR consent, completed DPIA, and legal sign-off.
6. Vendor performance: support, uptime, data quality and integration with your HR, payroll and time systems.

**Phase 2 (H2): waves of 20-30 stores**, each wave following the gates and with a rollback path. Keep the manual process available until the last wave is stable.

## 4. What to say to the COO and the vendor

- **To the COO:** you are backing the tool and the savings goal, and you are protecting the savings. A forced March rollout risks a legal challenge from the OR, wage costs from the 4-day rule, and a service dip across 140 stores at once, which would cost more than a three-month delay. A pilot gives you your own number to defend with the CFO and the board.
- **To the vendor:** a pilot with success criteria, a clean exit and clear data terms. If they will only sell on an all-stores-by-March basis, that tells you something about how confident they are in the 12%. Offer them a path to the whole chain in return for a successful pilot.

## 5. Risks if you proceed with March anyway

- OR objection or a legal challenge, which halts the rollout mid-way.
- Hidden wage cost from late changes, so the 12% turns into 3-6%.
- Student attrition and weaker staffing at peak times.
- A manager backlash if they cannot override the schedule.
- Public and press attention on "algorithmic scheduling" at a large employer.
- Lock-in to a vendor whose numbers you have not verified.

## 6. What I would need to firm this up

A few facts would change the details but not the recommendation: how many staff are on oproep versus fixed-hour contracts, which CAO applies, your current rostering lead time, the vendor's customer references, and what your forecasting accuracy is today. If the bulk of your students are on contracts with fixed minimum hours, the legal issue shrinks, but the commercial and consent arguments for a pilot remain.

**Bottom line:** say yes to the tool, no to the March date. Recommend a funded 10-15 store pilot with control stores and agreed gates, OR involvement from the start, and a 4-day-plus roster window for on-call staff.
