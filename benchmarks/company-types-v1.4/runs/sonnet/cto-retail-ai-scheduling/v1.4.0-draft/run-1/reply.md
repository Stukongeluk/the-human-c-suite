**Recommend a gated pilot, not "all 140 stores live by March": say yes to the tool in principle, no to the vendor's calendar.** The 12% is a vendor claim until it is backed by a backtest on your own till data. And as described, the design appears to collide with Dutch rules on flexible staff, which makes the March date a legal risk, not just an operational one.

**Why March is the wrong constraint**

- **Three-day publication may be unlawful for part of your workforce.** Under the Dutch on-call rules, on-call workers must be called at least four days ahead or can refuse. A collective agreement can shorten this to no less than 24 hours. A shift cancelled or changed inside that window is still paid. Many of your students are likely on such contracts, so "adjusting shifts on the day" could turn into paid hours you didn't plan for. Check your contract types and the supermarket CAO first. I'm not a lawyer; please have employment counsel confirm.
- **The works council has to be in before the tool is chosen.** At 9,000 staff you have one. It has a consent right on systems suited to monitoring attendance, behaviour or performance (art. 27 WOR), and probably on working-time arrangements. Rolling out first and consulting later invites a blocked rollout. Treat the council as free risk analysis, and give it the real numbers.
- **The AI Act applies.** AI that allocates tasks or monitors workers is high-risk. The duty to inform workers and their representatives starts 2 December 2027 (moved by the 2026 Digital Omnibus), so it doesn't bind you yet, but your contract should already require it. GDPR applies now: a data protection impact assessment, a processor agreement, and EU data location.
- **Timing.** March is about 14 weeks away, and the December peak sits in between. That leaves no room for learning, only for heroics at store level.

**Who carries the 12%**

Labour hours are the controllable cost, so they get cut first. If your average contract is around 15 hours (my assumption; send me the real figure), 12% is roughly 16,000 hours a week, about 450 full-time equivalents. In a flexible-contract workforce, those hours come out of students' pay packets. They are the people with the least voice and the thinnest buffer.

The evidence points the other way. In a randomised trial in 28 Gap stores, more predictable schedules raised median sales by 7% and labour productivity by 5%. Quarter-hour optimisation with short notice is the opposite design. It saves hours on paper and tends to show up later as turnover, absence and empty shelves.

So frame the target as removing idle and misplaced hours, not cutting people's hours. Take any net reduction through natural attrition and turnover, which in student-heavy retail is high, not through cutting contracts. Layoffs are not on the table for this decision.

**What I'd recommend**

1. **Now to mid-December: due diligence and consultation.**
   - Backtest the vendor's forecast on 12 to 24 months of your POS data, in 15-minute slots, against your current rosters. Ask for named references with the baseline behind their 12%.
   - Brief the works council and put the purpose, data and decision rules on paper. Agree guardrails together.
   - Check offline behaviour, human override for store managers and exit terms.
2. **January to April: pilot in about 10 to 14 stores, with 10 to 14 comparison stores.** Mix large and small stores, city and rural. Go live after the peak, not before it. Keep it to 8 to 12 weeks.
3. **Waves of 20 to 30 stores after that, each gated.** Realistically, all stores live by autumn. Negotiate the contract that way: per-wave pricing, a pilot exit clause, and no penalty for slipping March.

**Design guardrails to agree up front**

- Publish at least as early as the law and CAO require, and aim for a week where you can. Staff can see the rules behind their shifts.
- Hours changed on the day are paid or offered, not simply removed. Store managers can override, and staff can contest a decision to a person.
- Staff and student availability and minimum hours are respected inputs.
- Reporting is per store and team. No individual rankings.

**Pilot decision rule, fixed before results**

Scale if forecast accuracy and labour-hour variance improve by an agreed margin, while sales, on-shelf availability, queue times, turnover, absence and schedule-change frequency do not worsen. Also check that hours are not skewed toward or away from particular groups. Otherwise change or stop. Decide in advance where freed hours go: some back into stable hours for staff, some into savings. Don't leave it to be decided once the numbers are in.

**The COO's side, fairly put:** margins are thin, labour is the biggest lever, and better demand matching is real value. I'd keep that goal. The pilot gets you there faster than a failed big-bang that stalls on a council objection or a paid-shifts bill.
