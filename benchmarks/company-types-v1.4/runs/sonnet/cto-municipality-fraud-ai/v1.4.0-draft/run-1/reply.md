**Advise the council not to buy a system that scores every benefits recipient, and not to promise it live in six months. Offer them a six-month plan that delivers real results sooner and keeps a lawful, tested scoring pilot open as a later decision.** The reasons, in order of weight:

**1. "90% accurate" tells you almost nothing about the people you will flag.**
Illustration, with my assumptions: 10,000 recipients, 2% actually committing fraud (200 people), and "90%" meaning the model catches 90% of fraudsters and clears 90% of honest people. It flags 180 of the 200 fraudsters, but also 980 of the 9,800 honest recipients. Of roughly 1,160 flagged, about 85% are innocent, and your investigators would be working a list that is mostly wrong. Your real fraud rate may be higher or lower, so ask the vendor for these figures instead:
- Precision in the top 10% (what share of flagged people are actually overpayment or fraud cases), measured on your own data, not the vendor's reference data.
- Error rates per group: age, nationality or migration background, single parents, postcode, people with debts.
- What counts as "fraud" in their labels. In welfare, most findings are mistakes or missed notifications, not intent. A model trained on past investigations learns where you looked before, not where fraud is.

**2. You would be repeating cases that Dutch institutions have already paid for.**
- The childcare benefits scandal: risk profiling helped wrongly accuse over 20,000 families of fraud, and the cabinet resigned in January 2021.
- SyRI: in February 2020 the court in The Hague ruled the state's fraud risk-scoring system unlawful (Article 8 ECHR). It combined data from many government sources, as your vendor proposes.
- Rotterdam and Amsterdam have both piloted welfare fraud-scoring models. As publicly reported, Rotterdam's was stopped after it was found to be biased, and Amsterdam's recent pilot did not hold up against bias testing either. I have not checked the current details, so verify before quoting them to the council.

**3. The data plan is the weakest part.**
Debt counselling and housing data were collected so people could get help, not so they could be risk-ranked. Using them for fraud scoring raises purpose-limitation questions under the GDPR, and it teaches residents that asking for help makes them a suspect. Debt and housing stress are also close proxies for poverty. Your privacy officer and legal counsel must confirm there is a legal basis before any of this data is connected. I cannot give you that opinion, and I would not sign off on it without one.

**4. Law and procedure make six months unrealistic for the full system.**
- A system that evaluates eligibility for, or the revoking and reclaiming of, benefits is high-risk under the AI Act (Annex III). Most obligations apply from December 2027 after the 2026 Digital Omnibus, but a system bought now will still be running then. Whether the ban on purely profiling-based crime risk assessment (Article 5) touches this needs counsel's view.
- You will need a data protection impact assessment, a fundamental rights impact assessment, human review of every flagged case, and an explanation a resident can challenge. Also check whether your procurement route requires a tender.
- The cliëntenraad (benefits recipients' participation body) and the works council should be involved before a tool is chosen, not after.

**What I would put in front of the council instead**

| Within six months | What it delivers |
|---|---|
| Measure the real problem: overpayments and recoveries by cause (late notifications, data errors, intentional fraud), with euros attached | A fact base; the council is now deciding on evidence |
| Fix the causes that produce errors: clearer notification duties, earlier checks at intake, faster data matches through existing legal channels | Usually the cheapest euros saved, and fewer people wrongly pushed into debt |
| Give investigators better tools: case triage on transparent, human-set rules (specific signals, tip-offs), without debt-counselling and housing data | A visible start for the alderman, explainable to residents |
| Do the groundwork for a pilot: impact assessments, legal basis, procurement route, success and stop criteria agreed up front | A go/no-go in month six that is defensible |
| Gate any scoring pilot: run it in shadow mode on historical cases first, publish the results, and agree who is accountable for a wrongly flagged person | The model is tested before it touches anyone |

For the alderman, the honest line is: "Live in six months with everyone scored" is the version most likely to end up in the media and in court. "Measured savings and a lawful pilot decision in six months" is a version you can stand behind.

**Two questions that would change my advice**
1. Do you know how much is lost to overpayment and fraud, and from what causes? If the intentional fraud share is small, a scoring system is the wrong tool.
2. Who owns the consequences for a resident who is wrongly flagged: the vendor, the investigator or the alderman? If the answer is unclear, the system is not ready.

Also worth a conversation with your municipal secretary (the CEO view: reputation and legal risk) and the benefits team head (the product view: resident outcomes), so the council hears one answer.

**Assumptions:** I assumed the 2% fraud rate above, that the model scores individuals, and that you have not yet signed anything. The legal points are not legal advice; verify them with counsel and your FG (data protection officer).

What am I missing, and what would make this wrong? If you already have a valid legal basis, a clean independent bias test and a fraud rate well above 2%, the case for a pilot gets stronger, though not for a full launch.
