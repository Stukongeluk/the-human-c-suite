### Why the March / 12% pitch breaks in Dutch retail

1. **Dutch law eats the savings (WAB):** Under the *Wet arbeidsmarkt in balans* (WAB, since 2020), on-call workers must be called at least **4 days** ahead (or no less than **24 hours** if your CAO explicitly shortens it), and any shift cancelled or changed inside that window **still has to be paid**. On-the-day adjustments turn "saved" hours into paid non-working hours and payroll disputes. *(Have HR and employment counsel confirm your specific supermarket CAO rules; the *Wet meer zekerheid flexwerkers*, passed in July 2026 for 2028, keeps an exception for students' side jobs, but WAB notice/pay rules apply today.)*
2. **Works Council (OR) and AI Act timing:** With 9,000 staff, your OR has a **consent right** (*instemmingsrecht*, Art. 27(1)(l) WOR) over systems suited to monitoring attendance, behaviour, or performance, and an **advisory right** (Art. 25 WOR) on major organisational changes. Pushing 140 stores live in under four months—through the December peak—leaves no room for genuine OR consultation and invites an Enterprise Chamber challenge. Under the EU AI Act (Annex III), AI for task allocation and worker monitoring is high-risk; the formal duty to inform representatives (Art. 26(7)) applies from **2 December 2027**, but Dutch WOR co-determination applies right now.
3. **15-minute micro-slots hurt sales and retention:** Students juggle lectures, exams, and other jobs; 3-day notice and fragmented shifts drive no-shows and turnover. Worse, cutting store hours without reducing store work shows up as empty shelves and long queues. In a randomised trial across 28 Gap stores (Williams, Lambert & Kesavan, 2018), **more predictable schedules (2 weeks' notice, no on-call shifts) raised median sales by 7% and labour productivity by 5%**.
