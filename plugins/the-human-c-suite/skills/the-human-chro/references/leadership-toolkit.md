# Leadership toolkit: four templates you can use without AI

Read this when a team, a leader or a whole company wants better decision habits, or when the user asks for a decision record, a "how we decide" page, a no-surprises agreement or a pulse survey. Each template works on paper: copy it, fill it in, share it. When you fill one in for the user, keep their words and numbers, mark open items as open, and leave out anything you would have to invent.

## Contents

- 1. Decision record
- 2. How we decide here
- 3. No-surprises agreement
- 4. Pulse survey
- 5. Starting small

## 1. Decision record

One page per decision that changes people's work, money or direction. Write it before the decision is final, share it with everyone affected, and keep it where people can find it later.

```markdown
# Decision: <short title>

Status: proposed | decided on <date> | replaced by <link>
Owner: <the one person accountable>
Decision date: <date>      Review date: <date>

## The problem
What is at stake, for whom, and by when? Include the numbers and where they come from.

## Options considered
| Option | What it achieves | Cost and timing | Who is affected, and how | Reversible? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A |  |  |  |  |
| B |  |  |  |  |
| Do nothing |  |  |  |  |

## Decision and reasons
What we chose, and why it beats the other options. Say what we gave up.

## Who was consulted, and what changed because of it
"We heard X and changed Y. We heard Z and didn't change it, because ..."

## What would change our mind
The evidence or signals that would make us revisit this before the review date.

## What happens next
Steps, dates and owners. What is decided and what is still open.
```

Keep it to one page. If the reasons don't fit, the decision probably isn't ready.

## 2. How we decide here

A one-page agreement for a team or company about how decisions are made. Draft it together, not from the top, and revisit it once a year.

```markdown
# How we decide here

## Who decides what
| Kind of decision | Who decides | Who is consulted first | Who is told |
|---|---|---|---|
| Changes to a team's way of working | The team |  |  |
| Changes to roles, teams or reporting lines |  | The people affected, works council |  |
| Budget and investment |  |  |  |
| Tools that touch employees' work or data |  | Works council (where one exists) |  |

## How we disagree
- Reasons count, not rank. Anyone may challenge a decision, with evidence and an alternative.
- Challenge in private first, then in writing. No labels, no surprises in public.
- Once we have really heard each other, we commit, including leaders who were outvoted by better evidence.

## How we share decisions
- Big decisions get a decision record.
- The people affected hear it from their own manager first, before any wider announcement.
- We say what is decided and what is still open.

## How we check ourselves
- Twice a year we run the pulse survey and share the results.
- We look at which decisions changed because of input. If none did, we ask why.
```

## 3. No-surprises agreement

A short promise from leaders to their teams about changes to people's work. It works best signed by the leadership team and posted where everyone can see it.

```markdown
# Our no-surprises agreement

When a change affects your work, we promise:

1. **Problem first.** You hear the problem and the options before the decision is final, while your input can still change it.
2. **An input window.** At least <one to two> weeks to respond for changes to roles or teams, longer for big ones.
3. **Notice in proportion.** Changes to your role, team or manager are announced at least <X weeks> before they take effect, never on the day.
4. **Your manager first.** You hear it from your own manager, in person or live, before any all-hands or email.
5. **Decided and open, kept apart.** We say what is decided and what is still open, and we don't call a proposal a decision.
6. **Reasons in writing.** Every change comes with a short decision record.
7. **Representatives on time.** Where there is a works council or union, it is involved while it can still influence the plan, and the dates leave room for its advice.
8. **A way back.** If we break this agreement, tell <name or channel>. We'll explain what happened and what we'll do differently.

Signed: <leadership team>      Date: <date>      Review: <date>
```

Fill in real numbers before signing. An agreement with blanks left in it reads as a slogan.

## 4. Pulse survey

Five minutes, anonymous, twice a year or after a big change. Use a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

1. I can raise concerns without fear of negative consequences.
2. Decisions that affect my work are explained to me.
3. I hear about changes to my work before they happen, not after.
4. When I give input, I can see what was done with it.
5. My manager is open to being challenged.
6. I know what is decided and what is still open in current changes.
7. I have the time and support to adapt to changes in my work.
8. Open question: what is one decision you would like to have been asked about?

How to run it:

- Report only groups of at least five to ten people, so nobody can be identified. Never try to trace answers back to individuals.
- Share the full results with everyone within two weeks, including the bad ones.
- Pick one or two things to change, say who owns them and by when, and report back at the next survey.
- Watch the trend, not the score. A falling score on statement 1 or 4 is an early warning that people are going quiet.
- In the Netherlands and Germany, check whether the survey tool needs the works council's agreement, because it collects employee data.

## 5. Starting small

Don't introduce all four at once. Pick one upcoming decision, write a decision record for it, open a one-week input window, and afterwards ask the people affected whether it helped. Once that works, agree the "how we decide here" page together, then the no-surprises agreement. Run the pulse survey before and three to six months after, so you can see whether anything changed.
